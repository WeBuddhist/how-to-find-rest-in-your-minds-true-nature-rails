# Steps for Designing a New Curriculum

Curriculum design is usually approached as a cyclical, iterative process rather than a strict one-way sequence, but the steps below follow the standard order most frameworks use.

## 1. Conduct a Needs Assessment
Identify the gap between current and desired knowledge/skills. Gather input from learners, employers, teachers, or industry standards. Ask: *why does this curriculum need to exist?*

## 2. Define Aims and Goals
Set the broad purpose — what the curriculum is ultimately trying to achieve (e.g., "produce job-ready graphic designers" or "build foundational literacy").

## 3. Write Learning Objectives
Break goals into specific, measurable outcomes. Good objectives are usually framed with action verbs (Bloom's Taxonomy is commonly used here — "analyze," "design," "evaluate" rather than vague terms like "understand").

## 4. Select and Organize Content
Decide what topics, skills, and knowledge will be covered. Organize using scope (breadth/depth) and sequence (order), often building from simple to complex or foundational to specialized.

## 5. Choose Instructional Strategies
Decide how the content will be taught — lectures, projects, discussions, simulations, hands-on labs, etc. This should match the nature of the objectives (e.g., skills-based objectives need practice-based methods, not just lectures).

## 6. Select or Develop Materials and Resources
Textbooks, readings, software, equipment, case studies — whatever supports the instructional strategies chosen.

## 7. Design Assessment Methods
Decide how you'll measure whether objectives are met — exams, projects, portfolios, rubrics, peer assessment. Assessments should align directly with the stated objectives (this alignment is often called "backward design" when done first, before content selection).

## 8. Sequence and Schedule
Map content and assessments onto a realistic timeline — units, weeks, semesters — accounting for pacing and cognitive load.

## 9. Pilot the Curriculum
Test it with a small group before full rollout, if possible. This surfaces gaps in pacing, clarity, or feasibility.

## 10. Gather Feedback and Revise
Collect input from learners and instructors after piloting or initial delivery. Refine content, sequencing, or assessments based on real results.

## 11. Implement Fully
Roll out at scale, with training/support for whoever will be delivering it.

## 12. Evaluate and Iterate
Set a regular review cycle (e.g., annually) to check whether the curriculum still meets its goals, and update as needs, standards, or content evolve.

---

## Backward Design (Wiggins & McTighe)

A useful shorthand many designers use is **backward design**:

1. **Identify desired results** (objectives)
2. **Determine acceptable evidence** (assessments)
3. **Plan learning experiences and instruction** (content/methods)

This flips steps 3–6 above so that assessment is designed *before* content, ensuring everything taught actually serves the end goal.
