# The Structure of a Curriculum

A curriculum generally has several core components that work together, though the exact layout varies by education system, level, and philosophy. Below is the typical structure.

## 1. Aims and Objectives
The overarching goals — what learners should know, understand, or be able to do by the end.
- **Aims** are broad (e.g., "develop scientific reasoning")
- **Objectives** are specific and often measurable (e.g., "solve quadratic equations")

## 2. Content / Subject Matter
The actual knowledge, skills, and concepts to be taught — organized into subjects, topics, or themes, and often sequenced from foundational to advanced.

## 3. Scope and Sequence
- **Scope**: how broad or deep the content goes at each level
- **Sequence**: the order topics are introduced, usually building in complexity over time (this is why curricula are often mapped across grades/years)

## 4. Learning Experiences / Instructional Methods
How the content will actually be taught — lectures, projects, labs, discussions, fieldwork, etc. This connects objectives to real classroom activity.

## 5. Materials and Resources
Textbooks, readings, tools, technology, and other resources needed to deliver the content.

## 6. Assessment and Evaluation
How learning is measured — tests, assignments, portfolios, rubrics — and how the curriculum itself is evaluated for effectiveness (are students actually meeting the objectives?).

## 7. Time Allocation
How instructional time is distributed — semesters, units, weeks, class periods.

---

## Common Organizing Models

| Model | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tyler's Model** | Objectives → Content → Methods → Evaluation (a linear, classic framework) |
| **Spiral Curriculum** | Topics are revisited at increasing complexity over years |
| **Modular Curriculum** | Broken into self-contained units that can be sequenced flexibly |
| **Core + Electives** | A required base plus optional specialization tracks |
