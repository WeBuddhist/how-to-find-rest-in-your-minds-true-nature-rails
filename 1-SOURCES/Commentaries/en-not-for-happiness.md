# Preface

Before embarking on any new project, learning a new language for example, it helps to know without doubt *why* you are doing it and to be sure it is something you feel is worth seeing through to the end. Once that basis has been established, it is only natural to ask, “What should I do first?” It’s a good question, and one that’s quite easy to answer if, for example, you want to learn a new language. Beginners, obviously, start by learning the alphabet. But when it comes to the somewhat more ambitious and far-reaching project of fully accomplishing a spiritual path, things can get a little tricky. Although the same question will have to be asked, it’s a line of inquiry that is far more dangerous for an aspiring Buddhist than it ever could be for a language student. Why? Because in the Buddhist world both the question and its answer have become institutionalized.

Theoretically, as each individual’s spiritual journey will necessarily be different, “What’s first?” could elicit any number of answers. Unfortunately, though, only a fully realised buddha or a higher-stage bodhisattva is capable of giving explicit, individually tailored instructions about what each person should do first, and the likelihood of bumping into such sublime beings as we go about our daily business is extremely slim. So, how should we proceed? What should we do, and when? And is there anything at all that can be ignored?

Without the personal advice of Buddha, our only option is to rely on the existing generalizations provided by the dharma that are themselves the result of centuries of guesswork. Not very encouraging news in some ways, but do not despair. This guesswork may be hundreds of years old, but it was originally developed by some of the greatest scholars and practitioners ever to have walked this earth. So rest assured that if the answer to the question “What do I do if I want to follow the path of dharma?” turns out to be shamatha meditation or the four foundations, you won’t go far wrong.

One of the problems we are faced with today is that ngöndro practise is increasingly perceived as a kind of custom. It’s not a new phenomenon. Customs and traditions have always grown up around spiritual methods. In fact it is difficult to see how they can be avoided, or even if they should be. In places like Burma, Japan and Thailand, the local colour that now adorns the wisdom of the Buddha was instrumental in helping the teachings take root and flourish successfully. But today, almost the moment an aspiring vajrayana student ventures into a teaching, she is told that before doing anything else, she must first complete the ngöndro. Yet, the intention behind all Buddha’s teaching is to transcend man-made customs and culture, which change depending on the times in which we live, our country’s geography and its prevalent attitudes. Had the vinaya been taught somewhere dusty and windy, no doubt Buddha would have suggested that the monks protect their faces with what we now call a “burkha.” If that tradition had then been transplanted to a humid and completely windless tropical forest, it’s unlikely the monks living there would have found it inspiring.

As your studies deepen, you will come to realise that ngöndro is the most distinctive element of the vajrayana. Sadly, though, it is fashionable these days to try to get it out of the way as quickly as possible. New vajrayana students are learning to view ngöndro as a hurdle they are required to overcome before being allowed to receive higher teachings. It is such a big mistake! And a potentially dangerous one, because it is virtually impossible to refute. Yet many hold this view, and the consequences are beginning to mushroom out of control. For example, in Buddhist circles, where a kind of spiritual “political correctness” operates, even the gentlest suggestion that not everyone has to accumulate one hundred thousand prostrations is extremely unwelcome. The more people who think like this, the greater the risk that this precious practise will be reduced to meaningless ritual.

Of course, to follow a step-by-step path can be both beneficial and rewarding, but the problem is that contemporary dharma students tend to follow prescribed practises far too slavishly. Each student’s needs are different, and one of the skills a teacher must develop is the ability to discern the method most appropriate to each person’s capacity.

Imagine, for example, you have been given the task of teaching your next-door neighbour to ride a bicycle. The first thing you discover about her is that she has difficulty concentrating early in the morning, which is exactly when she has to ride her bike to work. As her teacher, you suggest she wake herself up by drinking a cup of coffee before leaving home. It works like magic, and within a couple of days your student is riding to work safely every morning. Before long, her cousin asks her to teach him how to ride, and since the cup of coffee you suggested made such a difference to her own bike-riding experience, she passes it on without considering whether he really needs it or not. He then passes the same information on to his brother, who passes it on to his daughter, and so on and so forth, until, five hundred years later, a closely knit cult has grown up of bicycle riders who only cycle after drinking coffee, and anyone who does not drink coffee will not ride a bike.

## How Useful Is Cultural Paraphernalia?

Spiritual paths born in Asia, such as Buddhism and Hinduism, are wrapped in all kinds of cultural paraphernalia and employ rituals that have been customized to meet the needs of each adoptive ethnic group. Asians love all the pomp and ceremony of Buddhadharma (its theatricality continues to benefit practitioners to this day); however, now that Buddhist philosophy is being taught beyond Asian borders, we face a number of challenges. While many ancient Buddhist rituals work very well for Asians, they are less easily digested by Europeans, Americans and Australians. Even young people born into traditional Buddhist societies in Nepal, Bhutan and Tibet find it hard to make sense of some of the more ritualistic aspects of dharma.

But just because many time-honored Eastern traditions seem a little old-fashioned in today’s changing world, we should not jump to the conclusion that they are now obsolete. Although some aspects may no longer prove useful, much of Buddhadharma’s so-called religiosity is still extremely worthwhile. As culture is neither independent nor permanent, it is still possible to teach and manipulate the ceremonial side of things, which means that those elements of ancient cultures that are a necessary part of Buddhist practise should, without doubt, be passed on to modern people.

The “anjali mudra” of pressing the palms together as a sign of respect and greeting is a beautiful and widely recognized gesture, and in the context of prostrations an accepted form of practise. Imagine if the practise were updated and you were told to shake hands one hundred thousand times instead. It would hardly have the same effect, although theoretically, there is no logical reason why it wouldn’t. But strong and long-lasting habits almost always overcome logic, and often the old ways really are the best, bearing testimony to the extraordinary insight and vision of the masters of the past who came up with so many universally applicable practises, such as sitting with a straight back during meditation practise, which continues to be appropriate for all human beings no matter what their background.

Certainly, the customs and traditions connected with ngöndro accumulations are still relevant for today’s practitioners. People like me, for example, find a ready-made structure and measurable goal both encouraging and inspiring—the proverbial carrot tied to a donkey’s hat. But we live in degenerate times and it is not easy to identify which of the many skilful methods available will still work for modern people. Logic suggests that, as times are so tough, each element of ngöndro should be repeated not just the customary one hundred thousand times but three hundred thousand times, or even more. Yet there are many who advocate the reduction of repetitions to ten thousand as a way of encouraging those put off by big numbers.

Lama Shang Rinpoche said those who want to practise mahamudra should not make a big deal about first accumulating the ngöndro practises; ngöndro and the so-called main practise must both be practised at all times. The crucial point here is that the aim of ngöndro is not merely the accumulation of numbers, but to penetrate our minds, ruffle the feathers of our pride and make a satisfying dent in our egos.

## The Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro

A great many of the so-called preliminary practises in Buddhadharma include unique and profound teachings expressed using very specific terminologies. Take, for example, the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro. It is well known as a “preliminary” practise, yet contains some of the most profound language in Buddhadharma. Words such as *prana, bindu* and *nadi* appear right at the beginning of the text and are repeated over and over throughout. The mahasandhi, or dzogchen, tradition is the great inspiration behind the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro and includes some of the most ancient and direct wisdom teachings in Tibetan Buddhism. In guru yoga practise, for example, the section on empowerment includes references to the “four stages of a vidyadhara*,*” and for many, merely to read the names of the vidyadharas is extremely encouraging.

However mind-boggling these concepts may initially appear to a beginner, they are not saved until the end of the path, but introduced right at the beginning, at ngöndro level, to help students become familiar with both the terms and the radical ideas behind them. Devoted, diligent practitioners are likely to repeat such words daily for ten or perhaps twenty years before gradually discovering, with great joy, the profound meaning they represent. The word *bodhichitta*, for example, is familiar to most dharma practitioners. Most of us are fairly sure that it has something to do with the good, kind thoughts we try to instill in our minds, but in reality the true meaning of *bodhichitta* will only emerge after many, many years of practise.

## About This Book

This book has not been written for those who are completely new to Buddhism. It is aimed at those who have at least some idea about the Buddhadharma, are committed to understanding the language of dharma no matter how long it takes, or have a strong desire to practise. My aspiration is that *Not for Happiness* will be useful to *all* those who wish to practise the vajrayana path of the Tibetan Buddhist tradition. But as you will see, although these pages are packed with generalizations, I have taken the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro as my main reference point and relied on much of the jargon and clarification developed by the Nyingma school.

I must admit, though, part of me is worried that by doing so, I have done a disservice to all the other profound vajrayana lineages, each of which has its own unique vocabulary of painstakingly crafted words and expressions. Why, then, have I not incorporated the language of these other lineages, especially since we are always being encouraged to cultivate a nonsectarian attitude and perceive all traditions purely? To pick and choose amongst terms used by different lineages and mix them together in a kind of linguistic chow mein is about as far away from being “nonsectarian” as it’s possible to be—and it doesn’t help. The term *ordinary mind,* for example, means one thing to a mahamudra practitioner and something else to a practitioner of mahasandhi; similarly, “do not be distracted” in the shravaka tradition has quite a different meaning in a vajrayana context. This is why I have tried to stick to Nyingma explanations of these practises, and why I must also plead with you not to dismiss lightly any terminology you come across in the future that does not appear within these pages.

Although I refer most often to Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo’s commentary on the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro and the Chagchen ngöndro from the Kagyupa tradition, a great deal of excellent supporting literature is available for whichever ngöndro tradition you follow. Practitioners of the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro can refer to one of the most remarkable of all ngöndro commentaries, Patrul Rinpoche’s famous *The Words of My Perfect Teacher,* which is a series of direct, experiential pith instructions; practitioners of the mahamudra ngöndro can enrich themselves with *The Jewel Ornament of Liberation* by Gampopa, and many other texts; practitioners of Lam Dre (Path and Fruition) can consult the various Three Perceptions teachings from the Sakya traditions; and we must not forget the profound and excellent teachings of Lam Rim (the Gradual Path) by Tsongkhapa.

Other texts worth reading include the *Lankavatara Sutra,* Shantideva’s *Bodhicharyavatara,* Kamalashila’s *Stages of Meditation of the Madhyamika* and Atisha Dipamkara’s *Lamp for the Path to Enlightenment*.

You will notice I have paraphrased most of the quotations included in this text. It’s a choice I made out of sheer laziness and over which I have since experienced the odd twinge of guilt. At the end of the day, though, the information we pass on to others can only ever be based on our own interpretation of what we have learnt. To me it therefore makes sense to paraphrase the great masters instead of struggling for months to translate them accurately, because however “good” the translations may be, they would only be correct from my point of view.

God Karma only knows why, but the modern world is completely obsessed with “gender issues.” So, I should warn you that you might find my use of gender offensive to your own ideas of “correctness,” political or otherwise. However, no conscious decision was made about whether “him” and “her” was more appropriate in each example.

As far as the details of various visualisations are concerned, some may appear overdone while others have been galloped through far too quickly. So, rather than solely relying on the information you find in this book, I recommend you refer to the original commentaries by the masters of your specific tradition.

If you would like to know more about the masters or teachings mentioned in this book, or the terminology used, I suggest you consult the excellent resources that are now available on the Internet and in glossaries and bibliographies produced by, for example, the Padmakara Translation Group, the Tibetan Buddhist Resource Centre, Rigpa Translations and Rigpawiki, the Nalanda Translation Committee and Rangjung Yeshe Publications, to name but a few.

Maitreya said that to hear the dharma is to open the door to liberation, and many students imagine that study in the form of hearing and contemplating the dharma will suffice as a dharma practise. It’s true that for those on the threshold of spiritual practise, to listen to and read dharma is extremely rewarding—and an activity that should not be entirely abandoned by more seasoned dharma practitioners—but words are abstractions that rely wholly on centuries-old assumptions, making the language we are forced to use obscure and vague. Ultimately, merely to hear and think about dharma is not enough; we must also practise it. So, hearing, contemplating *and* meditating on dharma are all vital to our spiritual path, with meditation lying at its very core.

# Introduction

Adjust Your Mind

## The Heart of Dharma Lies in Everyday Activities

Many contemporary dharma practitioners, myself included, have been attending dharma teachings for a donkey’s years, yet our minds remain as rigid as a block of wood. When life seems to be going well, we feel happy, but when our plans go awry, we are hurt and often unleash a storm of unbridled emotion—troubling signs that the teachings have gone in one ear and straight out the other. We find ourselves wondering, “Is it really possible to uproot all my habits? Or even adjust them a little? Is it plausible to imagine that I will ever reach the point where every movement I make is a dharma practise?” We are like novice drivers watching their instructor change gear, check his rearview mirror and apply the brake in quick succession, thinking, “How will I ever learn to drive?”

In the mahayana sutra requested by Matisagara, Buddha is asked an interesting question. If “truth” and “enlightenment” really are inexpressible ideas, how can ordinary, deluded beings like us even aspire to become students of the path to enlightenment? By showing respect for those who teach this inexpressible path, replied the Buddha. Stand when they stand; pay homage to them and honor them with polite and courteous words; provide them with protection; emulate them; offer them clothing, food, bedding, medicine and other material goods; or simply admire their activities, rejoice in their virtues and ignore their flaws. This is how to “uphold the practise of the inexpressible,” and indicates that the heart of dharma practise does not lie in the quantity of mantras we chant or how long we spend in meditation, but in simple, everyday activities.

Take, for example, how we deal with people we dislike. Although few of us attract any really vicious enemies, we always have to deal with people who irritate us. For beginners, quietly to avoid confrontation can be considered a practise. But if you ever get stuck with the most exasperating person on the planet, take a leaf out of Lord Atisha’s book. When he traveled to Tibet, Atisha kept the most infuriating person he knew close to him to make certain he had plenty of opportunities for practicing patience. We may not be able to go quite that far, but faced with an unavoidable and exasperating companion, the least we can do is use the opportunity to take irritation as the object of our practise.

The point the Buddha was making, though, is that the state of our minds is inevitably reflected in our everyday reactions. For example, how quickly does your mood change when your plans are frustrated? One minute you feel fine, then a north wind blows up a whole host of memories of past emotional crises and you find yourself reliving every painful detail over and over again. You get into such a state that you cannot keep your unhappiness to yourself and you phone a friend. He listens attentively as you exhibit your pain with no thought of the effect you are having, and soon your friend is as depressed and unsettled as you are. What’s the point? As an aspiring bodhisattva, if you feel the need to indulge your sufferings, do it alone. Don’t drag anyone else into your emotional extravaganza, particularly if you are a tonglen practitioner and committed to taking on the suffering of all sentient beings rather than sharing it out.

Basically, it is high time the dharma actually penetrated the minds of dharma students, especially those who, like myself, have been around a while. And even if you only manage to pull off that kind of penetration once in a hundred attempts, it is an achievement worthy of a medal.

## Spiritual Practise Constantly Changes

Whatever you do, whether it’s a daily shamatha meditation or long retreats, your practise will be constantly changing. Every day will bring a different experience. You may find, for example, that your mind is quite clear in the mornings but that you keep nodding off in the afternoons—a torment all human beings suffer as a result of being trapped in the three shells of body, speech and mind, and therefore at the mercy of our aggregates and elements. Or you might experience huge shifts of mood as you yo-yo from one emotional extreme to another, happy and stable on Tuesday evening, but by Wednesday morning so bad-tempered that even the sound of rustling leaves depresses you. It happens to all of us some of the time. What inspires us today will cast us down tomorrow; what makes us sad and eager to renounce the world one day will make us long to dive into the thick of samsaric existence the next. The culprits for these mood swings are our aggregates (form, feeling, perception, formation, consciousness), which are entirely dependent on the elements (earth, water, fire, air, and so on), making our practise anything but consistent.

This lack of consistency is why all practitioners, and beginners in particular, must do whatever it takes to encourage themselves to practise. In the same way babies are generally given more than one toy to play with, it’s a good idea for practitioners not to limit themselves to just one method. One day, if you feel the shravakayana teachings might help clarify the benefits of renunciation, by all means apply them. If tomorrow, contemplating the illusory nature of phenomena is more inspiring because it helps you appreciate that you have almost nothing to renounce, don’t hesitate to apply that view. Be skilful and practise whichever method works for you at this very moment. And don’t restrict yourself, because as a beginner it is so important for you to develop a taste for practise. Once you find the one or two practises that work best, concentrate on them. It’s a bit like moving to the city. At first you wander all over the place, trying the different routes between your home and your job, until eventually you find the most convenient way, and then stick to it.

There will also be times when none of the usual logic works and you find yourself incapable of accepting even the most patently obvious truth that death is imminent and inevitable, and not one living being—friend, family or even you yourself—can escape it. When such stubborn delusions take hold of your mind, pray to your guru, the buddhas and the bodhisattvas and beg them for help truly to accept the inexorability of death. And don’t fall into the trap of imagining you should first learn to despise samsara and develop strong “renunciation mind” *before* you resort to such a prayer. As the great Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé said, you should *rely on your guru for everything,* including the blessing of no longer hungering for samsara. In fact, you should pray to your guru and ask him to make sure that you pray!

One common misconception amongst practitioners is that in order to do any serious practise, you must first move either to Kathmandu or to a cave, and that by doing so the practise will automatically be accomplished. This is simply not the case. You just have to look at the dharma students currently living in India and Nepal to see that geography does not guarantee spiritual progress. Some of these dharma bums have been resident in Kathmandu for thirty years or more, but they never change. Their minds are as inflexible as they were when they first arrived, sometimes even more so. They continue to carry around exactly the same garbage as worldly people, but camouflage it under the guise of dharma practitioner, and although it is not difficult to see through their disguises, when you do, they can hardly bear the humiliation. So be assured, it is not necessary to leave home in order to practise effectively.

## Dharma Instructions

Buddhist practises are techniques we use to tackle our habitual self-cherishing. Each one is designed to attack individual habits until the compulsion to cling to “self” is entirely eradicated. So, although a practise may look Buddhist, if it reinforces self-clinging, it is actually far more dangerous than any overtly non-Buddhist practise.

The aim of far too many teachings these days is to make people “feel good,” and even some Buddhist masters are beginning to sound like New Age apostles. Their talks are entirely devoted to validating the manifestation of ego and endorsing the “rightness” of our feelings, neither of which have anything to do with the teachings we find in the pith instructions. So, if you are only concerned about feeling good, you are far better off having a full body massage or listening to some uplifting or life-affirming music than receiving dharma teachings, which were definitely not designed to cheer you up. On the contrary, the dharma was devised specifically to expose your failings and make you feel awful.

Try reading *The Words of My Perfect Teacher*. If you find it depressing, if Patrul Rinpoche’s disconcerting truths rattle your worldly self-confidence, be happy. It is a sign that at long last you are beginning to understand something about the dharma. And by the way, to feel depressed is not always a bad thing. It is completely understandable for someone to feel depressed and deflated when their most humiliating failing is exposed. Who wouldn’t feel a bit raw in such a situation? But isn’t it better to be painfully aware of a failing rather than utterly oblivious to it? If a flaw in your character remains hidden, how can you do anything about it? So, although pith instructions might temporarily depress you, they will also help uproot your shortcomings by dragging them into the open. This is what is meant by the phrase “dharma penetrating your mind,” or as Kongtrul Rinpoche put it, “the practise of dharma bearing fruit,” rather than the so-called good experiences too many of us hope for, such as good dreams, blissful sensations, ecstasy, clairvoyance or the enhancement of intuition.

For Kongtrul Rinpoche, when a practitioner stops making a big deal about things she used to fuss over incessantly, it is a sign that her dharma practise is bearing fruit. For example, before you became a genuine practitioner, to receive a compliment about you hair would intoxicate you with delight, while the merest suggestion that it was any less than perfect would immediately send you spinning into a terminal depression. Not to react at all in either situation is a sign that your practise is bearing fruit and that you are becoming an authentic dharma practitioner, and is far preferable to the experience of a million gratifications, encouraging dreams or blissful sensations.

Whether or not a dream is a good or bad sign is difficult to tell. Patrul Rinpoche said that an apparently good dream could just as easily be the manifestation of an obstacle or a mara because if you imagine it is a sign that you have achieved your goal, you might stop practicing, or become puffed up and arrogant about your abilities. Therefore, he said, even if you dream you had dinner with the Buddha, treat it as you would a gob of spittle; don’t give it a second thought and certainly don’t write it down or talk about it. More than anything else, be wary of it, in the same way you should be wary and cautious about any feeling that suggests you have a little more compassion than usual, or a little more devotion, or anything else that might cause you to relax your discipline of study and practise.

## The “Heart of Sadness”

Kongtrul Rinpoche suggested we pray to the guru, buddhas and bodhisattvas and ask them to grant their blessings, “So I may give birth to the heart of sadness.” But what is a “heart of sadness”? Imagine one night you have a dream. Although it is a good dream, deep down you know that eventually you will have to wake up and it will be over. In life, too, sooner or later, whatever the state of our relationships, or our health, our jobs and every aspect of our lives, everything, absolutely everything, will change. And the little bell ringing in the back of your head to remind you of this inevitability is what is called the “heart of sadness.” Life, you realise, is a race against time, and you should never put off dharma practise until next year, next month or tomorrow, because the future may never happen.

This race-against-time kind of attitude is so important, especially when it comes to practise. My own experience has shown me that promising myself I will start to practise next week more or less guarantees that I never get around to it. And I don’t think I am alone. So, once you understand that real dharma practise is not just about formal sitting meditation, but a never-ending confrontation with and opposition to pride and ego, as well as a lesson in how to accept change, you will be able to start practicing right away. For example, imagine you are sitting on a beach admiring the sunset. Nothing terrible has happened and you are content, even happy. Then suddenly that little bell starts to ring in your head, reminding you that this could be the last sunset you ever see. You realise that, were you to die, you might not be reborn with the ability to appreciate a sunset, let alone the capacity to understand what a sunset is, and this thought alone helps you focus your mind on practise.

## The Certainty of Death

We must pray to the guru, buddhas and bodhisattvas to grant their blessings so that we take to heart the certainty of death. Bring to mind again and again the reality that you and everyone you know are constantly moving closer to death. Of course, we are not complete fools, so we all know we have to die. But we also have to live with something far worse than the fact of death, and that is the uncertainty about when we will die and exactly how it will happen. It is this uncertainty that we must contemplate again and again as we strengthen our trust in and devotion for cause, condition and effect.

Remind yourself continuously about death and karma because, perhaps surprisingly, most of us find them quite easy to forget about. A sign we have forgotten about karma is that we never stop complaining about everyone, from the Buddha and our guru, to our husbands, wives, friends and strangers on the street. For example, imagine a very stubborn person who insists on driving along a dirt track at the edge of a high cliff. He is told how dangerous that particular route is, and that driving when drunk is just asking for trouble, but he ignores all warnings. Then, inevitably, he is so addled with alcohol one day that he accelerates too close to the edge and his car plunges to the rocks beneath. Even then, he spends his last seconds whining about how unfair it is. This is how we human beings run our lives. Were we to examine the causes of all the many tragedies we live through, we would discover how we personally and systematically organize precisely the causes and conditions that guarantee the results we experience. Yet, all we do is complain! This shows how little we understand cause, condition and effect, and how little we trust the Buddha, dharma and sangha.

Patrul Rinpoche said there is no such thing as a person who has perfected both dharma practise and worldly life, and if we ever meet someone who appears to be good at both, the likelihood is that his skills are grounded in worldly values.

It is such a mistake to assume that practicing dharma will help us calm down and lead an untroubled life; nothing could be farther from the truth. Dharma is not a therapy. Quite the opposite, in fact, dharma is tailored specifically to turn your life upside down—it’s what you sign up for. So when your life goes pear-shaped, why do you complain? If you practise and your life fails to capsize, it is a sign that what you are doing is not working. This is what distinguishes the dharma from New Age methods involving auras, relationships, communication, well-being, the Inner Child, being one with the universe and tree hugging. From the point of view of dharma, such interests are the toys of samsaric beings—toys that quickly bore us senseless.

## Go Beyond Concept

A sincere wish to practise the dharma is not born of a desire for personal happiness or to be perceived as a “good” person, but neither do we practise because we want to be unhappy or become “bad” people. A genuine aspiration to practise dharma arises from the longing to attain enlightenment.

By and large, human beings tend to prefer to fit in to society by following accepted rules of etiquette and being gentle, polite and respectful. The irony is that this is also how most people imagine a spiritual person should behave. When a so-called dharma practitioner is seen to behave badly, we shake our heads over her audacity at presenting herself as a follower of the Buddha. Yet such judgments are better avoided, because to “fit in” is not what a genuine dharma practitioner strives for. Think of Tilopa, for example. He looked so outlandish that if he turned up on your doorstep today, odds are you would refuse to let him in. And you would have a point. He would most probably be almost completely naked; if you were lucky, he might be sporting some kind of G-string; his hair would never have been introduced to shampoo; and protruding from his mouth would quiver the tail of a live fish. What would your moral judgment be of such a being? “Him! A Buddhist? But he’s tormenting that poor creature by eating it alive!” This is how our theistic, moralistic and judgmental minds work. In fact, they work in a very similar way to those of the world’s more puritanical and destructive religions. Of course, there is nothing necessarily wrong with morality, but the point of spiritual practise, according to the vajrayana teachings, is to go beyond *all* our concepts, including those of morality.

Right now the majority of us can only afford to be slightly nonconformist, yet we should aspire to be like Tilopa. We should pray that one day we will have the courage to be just as crazy by daring to go beyond the eight worldly dharmas and care not one jot about whether or not we are praised or criticized. In today’s world such an attitude is the ultimate craziness. More than ever, people expect to be happy when they are admired and praised, and unhappy when derided and criticized, and so it is unlikely that those who want the world to perceive them as sane will risk flying from the nest of the eight worldly dharmas. Sublime beings, though, couldn’t care less either way, and that is why, from our mundane point of view, they are considered crazy.

# Part One. Why We Do Ngöndro Practise

## Part One

## 1. What Is Dharma Good For?

### Will Dharma Make Me Happy?

The word *happiness* is a general term we use to describe the common goal shared by all human beings. Each and every one of us wants to be happy. What “happiness” means, though, and exactly how we achieve it is a matter for debate.

Hundreds of millions of people all over the world cherish the belief that their happiness depends on how much material wealth they own. They dream of living the life of A-list Hollywood celebrities with all the trappings, such as an immense house in Beverly Hills and the option of drying a single handkerchief in an industrial-sized drying machine. Practically speaking, though, only a fraction of these dreamers could ever squeeze themselves into Los Angeles. And even if other similarly desirable locations could be found, were hundreds of millions of people really to achieve such a luxurious and wasteful lifestyle, the ecological consequences alone would be devastating. Neither the dreamers nor anyone else on this planet would ever again enjoy a single moment of happiness.

Another version of happiness is exemplified by an Australian student of mine called Douglas. Douglas lives on the unemployment benefit provided by his government and feels no gratitude for it whatsoever. For Douglas, “renunciation” and “revulsion for samsara” are the excuses he uses to avoid work. And even though he is supposed to be a student of the dharma, a deeply ingrained laziness and lack of personal responsibility means that in spite of having plenty of time on his hands, neither does he practise. Not working and living on the benevolence of others seem to bring him a certain kind of bliss and happiness. But the problem with people like Douglas who become accustomed to living on state handouts, is they grow callous and start to assume that unlimited financial support is their right. Many so-called dharma practitioners fall into this category, particularly in liberal Western countries with generous social welfare systems. Like Douglas, they use dharma as an excuse for avoiding work and responsibility altogether, which is wrong. In fact, the version of “samsara” they have concocted for themselves is far worse than that of the Hummer-loving materialist, simply because they have neatly camouflaged it with dharma.

So, before we start our pursuit of happiness, it is important that we first define what being “happy” really means. And to those who fall into the categories of luxury-loving A-list celebrity or laidback freeloader, I can say categorically that as a method for bringing about your idea of happiness, the dharma will be of no use whatsoever. If, however, your vision of happiness has more to do with the transcendence of all worldly concerns and desires than wealth and celebrity, then dharma may be just what you are looking for.

### Develop “Renunciation Mind”

If worldly happiness is not the goal of dharma, then what is it that prompts a person to want to practise? Chances are that stepping onto a spiritual path would not even occur to a person who is rich, enjoys their life and has a strong sense of personal security. Of course, all of us, even the rich, experience moments of sadness and hopelessness, and may even momentarily feel the urge to turn our backs on all this world has to offer. But this is not a genuine experience of “renunciation mind,” as it has far more to do with weariness and boredom than renunciation, and is often a sign that, like a spoilt child tired of his toys, we are in desperate need of a change.

Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé said that if deep down you continue to believe a tiny corner of samsara could be useful or that it might even offer the ultimate solution to all your worldly problems, it will be extremely difficult to become a genuine spiritual seeker. To believe that life’s problems will somehow work themselves out, everything bad is fixable and something about samsara has to be worth fighting for makes it virtually impossible to nurture a genuine, all-consuming desire to practise the dharma. The only view that truly works for a dharma practitioner is that there are *no* solutions to the sufferings of samsara and *it cannot be fixed*.

It’s a view that is easy to uphold when we think about death, which the vast majority of human beings fear above all else. Birth, on the other hand, tends to provoke quite a different response. After all, isn’t birth about hope and the promise of a better future? Sublime beings such as the great Indian scholar Nagarjuna didn’t think so. Nagarjuna considered birth to be just as terrifying as death because it signals a return to samsara, which should frighten dharma practitioners just as much as imminent death.

Nagarjuna said in his *Letter to a Friend,*

> Samsara is like this, and thus we are reborn

> As gods, as humans, denizens in hell,

> As ghosts or animals; but you should know

> That birth’s not good, a pot of many ills.

> Then once there’s birth, comes misery untold,

> And sickness, ageing, wants frustrated, death,

> Decay, in short the whole great mass of pain.

> If birth is stopped, all this will be no more.1

It is vital to understand that however positive this worldly life, or even a small part of it, may appear to be, ultimately it will fail because *absolutely nothing* genuinely works in samsara. It is a very difficult attitude to adopt, but if we can at least accept it on an intellectual level, it will provide us with just the incentive we need to step onto the spiritual path. (Other incentives include making fools of ourselves or becoming entangled in worldly systems by trying to fix them.) The bottom line, though, is that only once a beginner can truly appreciate just how hopeless and purposeless samsara really is will a genuine aspiration to follow a spiritual path arise in his mind.

As Shakyamuni Buddha, compassionately and with great courage, explained to an autocratic king, there are four inescapable realities that eventually destroy all sentient beings:

- We will all become old and frail.
- It is absolutely certain that everything will constantly change.
- Everything we achieve or accumulate will eventually fall apart and scatter.
- We are all bound to die.

Yet our emotions and habits are so strong that even when the truth is staring us in the face, we are unable to see it.

In addition to recognizing the futility of samsara, the point of dharma practise is that it penetrates our minds and diminishes our affection for our ego and worldly life by pressing us to detach ourselves from the eight worldly dharmas. Practise should intensify our devotion to the truth, increase the value we place on going beyond the worldly, never contradict the essential teachings of the Buddha and be in complete accord with his ultimate teachings, and if our version of practise fails to accomplish all this, it is not real dharma practise.

However beneficial a practise appears to be, however politically correct or exciting, if it does not contradict your habit of grasping at permanence, or looks harmless but insidiously encourages you to forget the truth of impermanence and the illusory nature of phenomena, it will inevitably take you in the opposite direction to dharma.

The great Jigme Lingpa said that when you practise dharma, if you become wealthy effortlessly, you should offer fire pujas and make water and tsok offerings; and if you become famous and attract many followers, you should divert them to dharma practise. Although there is little point in going out of your way to please someone just because they are rich or highly born, if by doing so dharma will benefit in some way, or some good will come of it, there is no sense in upsetting them. More precisely, to be guided by the sublime dharma is like being a king; thinking solely about the dharma makes an ordinary human existence extraordinary; the practise of bodhichitta cannot be compared with the practise of ordinary religions; and to remain in the natural state far surpasses abiding by an ordinary view.

He also pointed out that even though obtaining a human body is like discovering a wish-fulfilling jewel, we must have noticed that many people never experience the “heart of sadness” and therefore waste their precious lives. Even though meeting a guru is more worthy of celebration than being enthroned a king, we must have noticed those who lack devotion and waste their good fortune by perceiving him impurely. And even though catching a glimpse of “this-moment mind” is like seeing the Buddha, we must have noticed those who lack diligence and allow their minds to drift into distraction.

### Develop the Willingness to Face the Truth

Most of us tend to resent being confronted with the truth, and from resentment springs denial. The most obvious example is that we feel annoyed when we are forced to acknowledge the illusory nature of our lives and the reality of death. We also take exception to contemplating it, even though death is an irrefutable universal truth. Our habitual reaction is to pretend it will never happen—which is how we deal with most of the other inconvenient truths we find difficult to stomach.

Instead of becoming resentful, though, it is important for anyone who sincerely wishes to become a dharma practitioner to develop a willingness and openness to embrace the truth, because the *dharma is the truth*. The Buddha himself made no bones about it. He never once provided his students with rose-tinted glasses to take the edge off the horror of the truth of impermanence, the agonies that are “emotion,” the illusory nature of our world and above all, the vast and profound truth of shunyata. None of these truths is easy to understand, or even to aspire to understand, particularly for minds programmed by habit to long for emotional satisfaction and aim for ordinary bliss. And so, if someone is able to hear teachings about emptiness and tolerate them intellectually as well as practically and emotionally, it is an indication that they have a real affinity for the dharma.

### How to Appreciate the True Value of the Dharma

The first step in learning to appreciate the true value of the dharma is genuinely to admit and fully accept that samsara is a terminal illness, and one we all suffer from. This illness makes us weak and lethargic and lacking in any form of self-control, yet we remain firmly convinced that we are capable of managing and directing every aspect of our lives. We really believe we are in control. The reality is, of course, that we have no power whatsoever, and until we recognize and admit how ill we are, we have no hope of truly appreciating dharma. It is a good idea to remind yourself of these two realities on a daily basis, like a patient surrendering to the treatment prescribed by their doctor, and by doing so you will lay the foundations of humility.

Once we genuinely accept that we are ill and frail, the urge to find a cure will arise naturally and lead us to develop a “seeking mind.” Actively to seek out a cure is extremely important, especially at the beginning of our spiritual journey, when “seeking” is far more important than “finding.” Throughout the process our teacher is the doctor we rely on to diagnose our illness, and his teachings the medicine he prescribes to cure us. But in these degenerate times few of us manage to perceive our situation in this way. On the contrary, we are proud of our apparently robust health and of having provided ourselves with everything we need and more. As Kongtrul Rinpoche pointed out, the dharma tends to be our lowest priority, and as a result, if any one of us were offered the choice between an immense suitcase overflowing with profound dharma teachings and a small purse containing all that is necessary to make us rich and famous, most of us would choose the latter.

Having fully acknowledged that we are ill, the urgency to find a cure will leave us little time to do much else. Or so you might have thought. For some, their longing for the dharma is the excuse they use to spend their entire lives window-shopping for a spiritual path. Sadly these are the people who tend to fall victim to fraudulent advertisements promising instant, painless spiritual solutions that carefully avoid anything involving penance or hardship. Although it’s true that the Sugata’s path of bliss (in other words, the spiritual path laid out by Buddha) discourages the more self-destructive kinds of penance such as self-flagellation, a spiritual path that involves absolutely no hardship at all simply does not exist. Unfortunately, in the name of practicing dharma, spiritual window-shoppers tend to be mindless and indiscriminate about the kinds of teachings they listen to and read, and lack the curiosity and genuine interest to photocopy even one page of an authentic dharma text. The idea that they might try putting what they hear into practise never even enters their heads.

Kyabje Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche said that both ordinary and extraordinary people behave in this way. There are even lamas and tulkus who look for easy ways of practicing the dharma without having to suffer privation. And although they should know better, they rarely show any appreciation for the precious teachings they receive, or make any kind of offering, let alone the offering of their body, speech, mind and entire life. They like teachings that don’t require them to make any commitments, and rather than actively seeking out a lama and creating the conditions necessary for practise, they expect both to fall effortlessly into their laps.

### Overcome Poverty Mentality

Many of us feel spiritually impoverished. Kongtrul Rinpoche said this is because we never stop desiring comfort and happiness. Until that kind of poverty mentality is overcome, a large portion of our mind will always be busy trying to secure personal comfort and happiness, making letting go of anything at all extremely difficult. Even those who present themselves as spiritual practitioners will find it impossible to make the superhuman effort necessary.

The problem here is that on a superficial, worldly level everything spiritual, especially the Buddhadharma, appears to be utterly useless and a complete waste of time. We are practical beings who like to build houses so that we can be comfortable and happy, and to put our resources into erecting a stupa with no bedroom or toilet or anything functional in it strikes us as being wasteful. But as Kongtrul Rinpoche pointed out, by clinging to the merest hint of an idea that worldly values and ideals might somehow be useful makes it extremely hard for anyone to tackle something as apparently futile as spiritual practise. And to cut the ties of the habits that bind us to worldly values, especially when it comes to material wealth, is virtually impossible.

Jigme Lingpa said that in these degenerate times, the prosperity of dharma is measured by how many disciples follow a master and by the strength of influence monasteries have over their disciples. “Wealth,” from an authentic dharma perspective, is understood entirely differently. For a dharma practitioner, wealth is not gold, silver, or a healthy bank account; wealth is contentment—the feeling that you have enough and need nothing more. Jigme Lingpa went on to say that although renunciants may not necessarily hunger for wealth, they might well long for fame, which he warned practitioners is even worse than craving wealth.

### Be Greedy for “Noble Wealth”

Whether it is worldly wealth or the “noble wealth” of dharma that we long for, neither is easy to come by. However hard we have to work for worldly wealth, we must work that much harder for the noble wealth of renunciation mind, loving-kindness, devotion and compassion, especially during the early stages of our spiritual path.

Kyabje Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche said that the one thing dharma practitioners should never be content with is the amount of dharma teachings they receive, study and practise, because the wealth of dharma is the only kind worth having. This is a truth an astonishing number of masters, saints and scholars of the past understood so well that they were willing to trade their very lives for a single word of dharma. They also knew that even though we have to leave our body, home and bank balance behind when we die, the dharma is the one form of wealth we can take with us.

If you have taken a fancy to noble wealth and would like to acquire a little for yourself, you must first undergo some basic training. Realistically, a genuine fondness for a spiritual path only arises naturally once in a blue moon, which is nowhere near often enough to secure your spiritual future. Authentic, heartfelt inspiration and longing are so rare that you might wait an entire lifetime and not experience it once. A genuine disgust and revulsion for samsara are equally scarce, and as they are unlikely to happen of their own accord, you must fabricate or manufacture them. Therefore, one of the first tasks for those new to the spiritual path is to revise your basic assumptions and fake it.

To make believe that samsara is disgusting may feel odd and unnatural at first, but by training the mind again and again to accept this truth, a genuine sense of renunciation will eventually begin to develop in your mind. The same goes for devotion, trust, contentment or sadness. Once any one of these emotions has been fabricated and contrived for long enough, it will automatically become genuine, which is why beginners must be content to base the majority of their practise on make-believe.

### Hear and Contemplate the Dharma

In the *Uttaratantra,* Lord Maitreya praises wisdom above all things.

> One who hears but one word of this and, having heard, has faith in it, will attain greater virtue than that of the meditative stability of others whose dhyana rids the three worlds of defilements’ fires and brings achievement of the divine state of Brahma’s paradise, even if it is practised as a means to supreme immutable enlightenment.

> Generosity brings affluence, right conduct leads to the higher states, meditative stability removes defilements, but prajna [wisdom] removes all defilements and the cognitive. Therefore this prajna is the most excellent thing: its cause is to study such as this.2

By listening to the dharma we begin to discover the hidden flaws and surprising fragility inherent in samsara that, until now, has appeared to be so intricately and perfectly constructed. At the same time, as samsara’s true colours are exposed, we also discover nirvana is not as boring or incomprehensible as we first thought. On the contrary, the remarkable strength and serviceability of nirvana suddenly becomes blatantly obvious, and we start getting better at discriminating between the activities that are worthwhile and those that are harmful.

Even though listening to the dharma is an indispensible method for those who wish to attain liberation, it isn’t easy. To listen to a stream of words is one thing, but fully to understand what we hear is something else entirely and depends wholly on how much merit we have accumulated.

### Practise According to Your Own Capacity

For the most part, *what* we practise and *how much* we practise are determined by our capacity, work and family situation, and how much time we have available—all perfectly valid criteria, as they were sanctioned by the Buddha himself, for example in the story of the village butcher. During the Buddha’s lifetime he permitted a butcher to take a vow not to kill animals at night, although he continued to kill them during the day in order to make a living. As a result, the butcher was born into an ephemeral hell where he suffered excruciating agonies during the daytime and at night his pleasures knew no bounds.

In this story Buddha is telling us that although this man earned his living by murdering animals, his profession did not bar him, and those like him, from also becoming spiritual practitioners. Yet one of the most widely held misconceptions about students of Buddhadharma is that we have to be either solitary yogis, like Milarepa—who practised all day, every day, for years on end—or celibate monks. If butchers can become practitioners, so can soldiers, fishermen and prostitutes. In fact *anyone* can be a Buddhist practitioner, because whatever your situation in life and regardless of your lifestyle, there is nothing to stop you from also practicing Buddhadharma. And although most people are unable to do everything recommended in the teachings, adopting one or two activities or attitudes will make a big difference to your life. Therefore, does a person have to become a monk, a nun or a yogi in order to be a Buddhist? Absolutely not!

Perhaps one aspect of the story about the butcher needs some clarification. It would be a mistake to assume that the Buddha was sanctioning murder when he asked the butcher not to kill during the night. That was not the case at all. Taking a vow was simply a stepping-stone that would eventually lead the butcher to a situation in which he no longer had to kill to earn a living. In fact, all Buddhist practises are like stepping-stones along the path to enlightenment, not an end in themselves. For example, the Buddha taught his monks a meditation on ugliness that reduces women to their fundamental physical constituents of pus, blood, meat, piss and shit, not as a way of sanctioning the denigratation of all women but to help monks detach themselves from their desire. He was certainly not trying to turn his monks into misogynists.

Buddhadharma is generally very permissive, and of course, as you take your first tentative steps along the Buddhist path, it goes without saying that you will do as much or as little as your situation allows. This is how it should be. It would be such a shame if those attracted to Buddhism were immediately put off because they felt compelled to become a monk or take hundreds of vows.

### Vows

As a beginner you will make a good start if you take just one or two vows that you are confident you can keep. For example, try making the vow never to take a human life, which is quite easy to stick to because even the least disciplined of us is unlikely to make a habit of killing people. If you do not take the vow, although it is unlikely you will accumulate the bad karma of committing murder, neither will you accumulate the good karma of not committing murder. But once you make the vow, for every moment you are not engaged in killing, including while you sleep or surf the net, you will accumulate merit. So take an easy vow to start with, then gradually, as you develop courage and enthusiasm, take more and more—for example, a vow always to offer advice, or not to be judgmental.

Always remember that the Buddhist practise of making vows is not a goal but a method, one of many offered by Buddhadharma. Should you engage in this practise? Yes, definitely! It comes highly recommended because as Sakya Pandita said, the difference between those who have taken vows and those who have not is similar to that between a landowner who cultivates his land and one who leaves it fallow.

### Liberation from Illusion and Delusion

As the Buddha said in the *Vajracchedika Prajnaparamita Sutra* (the *Diamond Sutra*),

> Like a star, hallucination, candle,

> Magical illusion, dewdrop, bubble,

> Dream, lightning or a cloud—

> Know all compounded phenomena to be like this.3

From a Buddhist point of view, each aspect and moment of our lives is an illusion. According to the Buddha, it’s like seeing a black spot in the sky that you are unable to make sense of, then concentrating on it intensely until finally you are able to make out a flock of birds; or hearing a perfect echo that sounds exactly like a real person shouting back at you. Life is nothing more than a continuous stream of sensory illusions, from the obvious ones, like fame and power, to those less easy to discern, like death, nosebleeds and headaches. Tragically, though, most human beings believe in what they see, and so the truth Buddha exposed about the illusory nature of life can be a little hard to swallow.

What happens once we know that everything we see and experience is an illusion? And what is left once those illusions have been liberated? To be liberated from illusion is to dispel all the limitations that false perception brings and entirely transform our attitude. So, “liberate” means to be released from the delusion of imagining illusions to be real. But crucially, we have to *want* to be liberated, we have to *want* to become enlightened. And it is only once we develop a genuine longing for enlightenment that, almost automatically, we start to learn how *not to want* to be ambitious in a worldly sense. Such a longing is not easy to generate, but without it, to step aimlessly onto the spiritual path would be utterly pointless.

Millions of people in this world are interested in some version of meditation, or yoga, or one of the many so-called spiritual activities that are now so widely marketed. A closer look at why people engage in these practises reveals an aim that has little to do with liberation from delusion, and everything do to with their desperation to escape busy, unhappy lives, and heart-felt longing for a healthy, stress-free, happy life. All of which are romantic illusions.

So, where do we find the roots of these illusions? Mainly in our habitual patterns and their related actions. Of course, no one of sound mind imagines any of us would willingly live an illusion. But we are contrary beings, and even though we are convinced we would shun a life built on self-deception, we continue to maintain a strong grip on the habits that are the cause of countless delusions. Small wonder the great masters of the past have said that although everyone longs to be free from suffering, most of us simply won’t let go of it; and although no one wants to suffer, we find it almost impossible not to be attracted to samsara.

### Continuous Existence

A spiritual path is only important to those who can accept the notion of the continuous manifestation of phenomena from past, present and future lives. For those unable to believe and trust this truth, to follow such a path would be pointless.

The sutras liken reincarnation to the relationship between teachers and students. A singing teacher teaches students how to sing. His students learn techniques and benefit from direct experiential advice from their teacher. But the teacher doesn’t remove a song from his throat and insert it into a student’s mouth. Similarly, reincarnation is a continuity of everything we have learnt, like lighting one candle from another, or a face and its reflection in a mirror.

If there were no continuous existence, in other words if there were no such thing as reincarnation and we really did only live once; if, as many followers of new-age spirituality suggest, everyone, from a mountain yogi to a hoodie from the Bronx or an ambitious New York banker, merely became one with the elements when they died, what point would there be in practicing the dharma or any other kind of spiritual path? Why bother sitting on a meditation cushion for hours on end? Why restrain yourself from indulging in all the good things the world has to offer? Why miss out on anything at all?

We practise the dharma so we can cut through the endless round of cyclic existence. If it were cut automatically at the moment of death, why bother following a spiritual path? We make a big fuss about Shakyamuni having become “enlightened” under the bodhi tree precisely because he managed, finally, to cut through the continuous illusion of birth and death. If it happened naturally at death, if cyclic existence were cut through the moment a doctor turned off the life support machines, what is stopping any of us from killing ourselves this very moment? Yet there are Westerners claiming to be scholars and teachers, and professing to seek enlightenment, who maintain just that. Were they to follow their argument to its logical conclusion, they would shoot themselves in the head right now!

Having said that, karma is almost synonymous with reincarnation, and both are difficult to understand. To do so first requires an understanding of the Buddhist concept of shunyata (emptiness), at least on an intellectual level.

### Buddha Nature: Develop Confidence That the Worst Kind of Dirt Can Be Washed Away

Those of us who take to the spiritual path should try to look at our stubborn defilements in the same way we view last night’s washing up. For a start, it’s a huge pile and looks terrifyingly solid and real. It’s easy for your mood to sink into extreme hopelessness when you consider the sheer size of your task and realise how utterly incapable you are of dealing with it. Yet, however permanently the left-over food appears to have cemented itself to the plates and cutlery, always remember that as soon as some hot, soapy water is put to work, it will all be washed away, leaving everything clean and sparkling. So, rather than allowing appearances to overwhelm you, remembering that all the dirt can be removed will give you confidence, and the whole process of following a path will become almost joyful.

Feelings of aggression, jealousy and pride come and go, and although all our emotional states and the very lives we pass through are illusory and temporary, we continue to make the mistake of believing them to be never-ending and permanent. Ironically, as we fail to see through these delusions, we also miss the one enduring element that has no expiry date: buddha nature. We simply don’t notice it, even though it is so close it might as well be right in front of our noses. Instead, we fixate on everything that does not truly exist, like the aggregates that create the illusion of “self,” and mistake the causes of suffering, and even suffering itself, for happiness. We are extremely skilled at constructing the web that binds us to samsaric life, yet powerless to dismantle it, even if we wanted to—which we don’t.

For example, we go to school to earn a qualification to get a job, not just to pay the bills but to strengthen our belief in the stability of our existence. We make friends, not because we are gregarious by nature but so we have someone to protect and comfort us when we feel insecure and lonely. We get engaged to make our relationship feel more solid than mere dating, then marry in the hope it will make us feel more secure. The icing on the cake is having children, because children will finally make our family feel permanent.

In broader terms, we promote the concept of democracy as a method for achieving freedom. Yet in reality it is just another chain that shackles us to the disastrous decisions those we vote into power make in our names, decisions that not only undermine our country but wreak economic and environmental havoc on the rest of the world. These are the ways we bind ourselves to samsara.

### Mindfulness

#### The Problem: Distraction

Most of us know that aggression is a problem, as are pride and jealousy, but the truth is that *all emotions* cause problems one way or another and each has a distinctive character. “Passion,” for example, is starkly different from “aggression.” Fundamentally, though, all emotions spring from one basic source, distraction. What is “distraction”? Clearly, it is not merely the sound of a chainsaw firing up or blaring Bollywood music that interrupts our meditation practise. On a more profound level, distraction is any of the emotional responses we are sidetracked by—for example, hope for praise and fear of blame, as well as its more subtle manifestations, like being spaced-out, distracted, lost in thought or worked up.

#### The Solution: Mindfulness

Since our fundamental problem is distraction, its fundamental solution is to be mindful. There are an infinite number of methods for developing mindfulness that all fall into one of two categories: shamatha or vipashyana. The point of shamatha practise is to make mind malleable. But a pliant mind alone will not uproot samsara completely, we also need to see the truth, which is why vipashyana practise is so crucial.

Unfortunately, though, mindfulness is difficult, mostly because we lack the enthusiasm to develop it, but also because our habit of longing for distraction is both deeply ingrained and extremely tenacious. It is therefore vital for a dharma practitioner to develop renunciation mind and to recognize the defects of samsara, both of which lie at the core of the Buddhist approach to training the mind.

The masters of the past suggest we should constantly remind ourselves about: the imminence of death; the futility of our worldly activities; and the worst news of all, that there is no end to samsara’s sufferings. Just look around you and you will see that the world never ceases to churn out more and more of the same thing, and that the result is unremitting pain and unbearable suffering. It’s no surprise, then, as the great masters have pointed out, that to maintain mindfulness for as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea accumulates more merit than years of practicing generosity, discipline and asceticism.

### Discipline, Meditation and Wisdom

Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé wrote that he aspired to accomplish the vast and infinite activities of a bodhisattva on his own, without having to deal with sycophantic servants, or subdue enemies, or endure the burden of many friendships, and he prayed, “May I remain in isolation and tame my mind.”

The “taming the mind” Kongtrul Rinpoche refers to is categorized in Buddha Shakyamuni’s teachings as the three higher trainings in discipline, one-pointed concentration and wisdom, and are excellent methods for protecting ourselves against Mara’s five arrows. The sutras tell us that Mara (Buddhism’s “devil”) is a tricky character and an expert archer. To avoid straying into the sights of one of his five arrows requires tremendous effort because each one is trained on our most vulnerable spots.

- The first of Mara’s arrows is aimed at those who feel great *pride* in their accomplishments or in their material or spiritual wealth.
- The second is aimed at those who are *ignorant* because they have no idea which activities and attitudes need to be abandoned and which adopted.
- The third is directed at those with *wrong views,* such as not believing in cause, condition and effect.
- The fourth is fixed on those whose *forgetfulness* continually distracts them from mindfulness.
- The fifth strikes those *distracted* by the eight worldly dharmas.

As practitioners, it is best not to advertise our weaknesses or expose them as targets for Mara’s arrows, so we must learn to protect ourselves, either by wearing armour or cleverly camouflaging ourselves with:

> 1. *discipline,* to contradict and unsettle our emotions;

> 2. *meditation,* to subdue our emotions so they do not leap out of control the moment they are aroused; and

> 3. *wisdom* to uproot all emotion.

Training in discipline purifies wrongdoing and wrong thinking; training in meditation stabilizes right view, right motivation and right action; and training in wisdom liberates us from the root of ignorance.

Were we to examine during the course of a single day all the different emotions that arise in one person’s mind, we would get an inkling of the unimaginable quantity and variety of emotions it is possible for sentient beings to feel. The Buddha has provided us with an antidote or training for every one of these emotions. For those who want to escape suffering, he taught the shravakayana; and for those who long to escape the extremes of samsaric life, have no interest in nirvana and instead cherish the wish to help all sentient beings become truly happy, he taught the bodhisattvayana. Both these vehicles (yanas) are complete paths that ultimately lead to liberation from delusion.

## 2. The Causal and the Resultant Paths

Wherever and whenever Buddha taught, his audience was always made up of a wide variety of people whose individual capacities, preferences and personalities led each of them to listen to and hear his teachings quite differently. And so, although Buddha himself did not set out to create the separate schools and traditions that now exist within Buddhadharma, as time went on, a multitude of vehicles for his teachings began to evolve. Eventually, to simplify things, scholars of the past began to categorize those vehicles, and one of the categorizations they came up with divides the teachings into two: the “causal” path and the “resultant” path.

### The Causal Path

How much of the Buddha’s teachings we understand depends entirely upon our attitude and capacity, and for the majority of us, the idea of the “result” of practise is baffling. To encourage us—and all those who can only absorb the vaguest intellectual impression of the effect of spiritual practise—Buddha taught the cause-driven paths, such as the shravakayana, pratyekabuddhayana and bodhisattvayana, which diagnose our symptoms and recommend the application of appropriate remedies.

According to the shravakayana and pratyekabuddhayana, the “truth of suffering” is that the ordinary, samsaric world is impure, and therefore the logical thing for spiritual seekers to do is to try to escape it. In addition, the bodhisattvayana teaches that samsara is imperfect on a relative level, but points out that ultimately its nature is empty. So, rather than putting all his energy into avoiding samsara, a bodhisattva is advised to focus on practises like the bodhichitta of aspiration and to seize every opportunity to help sentient beings, even if it means jumping into the thick of samsaric life, like a swan diving into the muddy depths of a lotus pond.

### The Resultant Path

Other practitioners are able to grasp inconceivable ideas and believe in incredible realities. They are courageous, audacious and rarely reproach themselves for thinking and acting outside the box. For them the resultant path is more appropriate, as it includes result-driven activities, like washing up after breakfast.

As we have already seen, when you wash your cereal bowl every day, you know that however stuck the dried-out muesli may appear to be, the fact the bowl was clean before you started breakfast must mean it is possible for it to become clean again once you have eaten. You are therefore confident that the dirt is temporary and once it has been washed away, a fundamentally clean bowl will be revealed. This is why washing dishes is described as a result-driven activity.

What, then, does “believe in incredible realities” mean? The *Heruka Galpo Tantra* sums it up quite neatly: on the causal path sentient beings are said to have the *potential* to become buddhas, whereas on the resultant path they already *are* buddhas. Nevertheless, imagine your reaction to someone who insists that not only are you a buddha, but that every other sentient being is too. Would you believe them? If you think you would, you should know that your perspective is either naïve and new-agey, or at best, you believe the other person has extremely pure perception—which, as a justification for “belief,” is a bit thin. In fact, the only way to develop a genuine understanding that we are all buddhas is by following the vajrayana path.

### The Path Is the Goal

Theoretically from the very beginning, practitioners of the resultant path apply, or at least imagine they are applying, methods that unite “cause” and “result.” Here, “imagine” means that although you appear to be working with the *cause* of your enlightenment, you are already picturing the *result,* which is enlightenment itself. Take cooking an egg as an example. An ordinary egg looks like an egg, not an omelette. But if you intend to make an omelette, you will already have a picture of it in your mind, and even though uncooked eggs look nothing like omelettes, because you have visualised how you want the eggs to look when cooked, the result will be an omelette. This is an example of the result being the path and is a method that forces the cause to mature more quickly.

Another example is that although milk, which is liquid, looks nothing like a solid pat of butter, once it has been transformed using the very simple but dynamic method of churning, even though it has taken on a different form, it is still milk. Similarly, water and ice look completely different, but essentially both are water.

One of this method’s infinite number of benefits is that it provides students with an incentive to practise and instills in them the confidence and understanding that the *path is the goal*.

### That Which Binds Samsaric Beings Can also Liberate Them

In the tantric texts we are told that discursive thoughts can only be overcome by discursive thoughts, and cyclic existence can only be dismantled by cyclic existence. The great Saraha also pointed out that what binds the fool liberates the wise. Followers of the dharma who lack the openness and guts to grasp this truth will find their spiritual path to be long-winded, the methods available limited and the journey difficult and penitential.

At the same time, don’t kid yourself that you have the superior faculties and capacity necessary to enable you to apply the vajrayana’s extraordinary methods exclusively. Right from the beginning, great masters of the past have warned us over and over again that although we must aim to practise the resultant path, we should never imagine ourselves to be above the causal paths of the shravakayana and bodhisattvayana. Outwardly we should practise the shravakayana, inwardly the bodhisattvayana and secretly the vajrayana.

To practise in this way brings enormous benefit. For example, being seen to practise the shravakayana may inspire others to start practicing too, and encourage them to include in their practise renunciation, humility and simplicity. By practicing the bodhisattvayana, inwardly your courage and compassion will be strengthened and you will also avoid the side effect of becoming pompous. By keeping your vajrayana practise secret, you will save others from making hasty judgments or being scandalized by its methods, and avoid any of the subsequent negative consequences that so often tarnish the reputation of tantra.

Those who are not ready for tantrayana yet ostentatiously exhibit themselves as tantric practitioners not only ruin their own spiritual path, they also shut the door of inspiration to others. Although human beings may attempt to visualise poison as medicine before consuming it, those who try but lack the peacock’s capacity for *transforming* the poison will die. After all, there must be a good reason why Shakyamuni Buddha chose to appear serene, robed and barefoot in this world instead of opting for his richly ornamented sambhogakaya manifestation with its splendid palaces and large retinues.

### Using This Physical Body as a Path

The path of the shravakayana practitioner is one of discipline. For example, he learns to discipline his physical body through a variety of practises, from refraining from sexual misconduct and shaving his head, to sitting straight when he meditates, controlling his breathing by concentrating one-pointedly on the out and in breaths and breathing through his nose.

Followers of the path of a bodhisattva also use the body to learn discipline, and in addition they use it to perform compassionate activities. For a bodhisattva, the body is quite a useful gadget, at least for the time being, although its form, elements, function and needs are not considered indispensible tools for practise as they are in the vajrayana. In fact, the bodhisattvayana often regards the body as a burden. As Shantideva wrote,

> If servants who cannot be set to work

> Are not rewarded with supplies and clothing,

> Why do you sustain with such great pains

> This body, which, though nourished, will abandon you?

> So pay this body due remuneration,

> And then be sure to make it work for you.

> But do not lavish everything

> On what will not bring perfect benefit.

> Regard your body as a vessel,

> A simple boat for going here and there.

> Make of it a thing that answers every wish

> To bring about the benefit of beings.1

The attitude of vajrayana practitioners to the physical body is quite different to the other two yanas. The vehicles of the causal path view the body as something we are stuck with, whereas the vajrayana promotes it as our most easily accessible aid.

The wisdom and skilful means found in the bodhisattvayana allow for far greater use of the physical body than the equivalent methods offered by the shravakayana. Wonderful stories are told of bodhisattvas who selflessly offered their physical bodies to help others, such as the monk who satisfied a girl who was madly in love with him even though he was celibate, or the famous story about the Buddha in one of his last lives before attaining enlightenment, willingly surrendering his own body to satisfy the hunger of a starving tiger and her cubs.

Not only is the body used by vajrayana practitioners to serve others, it also plays a significant role in meditation practise. For example, to enhance their practise, practitioners make various gestures (mudras), dance and engage in the very ordinary activities of eating and drinking. Right from the start, vajrayana students are taught how to understand the elements and energies of the body as jnana (wisdom) and kaya (deity) (these terms will be discussed later in this text), and that the boundaries between body and mind are, if not nonexistent, very small indeed. It therefore makes sense for those who want to tame the invisible, intangible, unreachable abstract mind to take the opportunity also to manipulate the body. And to do so can be extremely helpful. For example, if you have never had a headache and want to know what one feels like, you could either imagine it or hit yourself over the head with a brick. The first method might take a while to perfect, and there is no guarantee that your imagined headache will be anything like a real one. But the second method will undoubtedly result in the immediate and absolutely authentic experience of a headache.

Lord Buddha taught these different paths to ensure that all potential students would be able to find teachings appropriate to their capacities. But no one path is more precious or higher than another. The much too common attitude of mahayana and vajrayana students is to look down on the shravaka traditions. It is a disgusting perspective, as it downgrades the Buddha’s own words to being somehow lower than the other vehicles. How can one word spoken by the Buddha be “higher” or “lower” than another? It can’t! And as students of Buddhadharma, we must aspire to put *all* the teachings of the Buddha into practise, regardless of the lineage we happen to be following.

A classic example is of a man suffering from jaundice who, as a result of his illness, perceives a white conch to be yellow. One interpretation maintains that what the man should see is a white conch, and that he must therefore be prescribed a medicine that will ensure he sees the correct colour. The medicine, in this case, comes in the form of the skilful means offered by the shravakayana, pratyekabuddhayana and bodhisattvayana.

A quite different interpretation, aimed at those with a greater capacity for understanding, is offered by the vajrayana. The colour the man perceives the conch to be is irrelevant; what matters is that, right from the start, he is introduced to the reality that the conch is white. From then on, even though he continues to see a yellow conch, he will always remember that the conch is inherently white. In this case, since the first thing students are told is that the result of their cure will be to see a white conch not a yellow one, the vajrayana is described as a “resultant vehicle.” This approach brings many students great confidence and ease on the path.

Nevertheless, having spent many lifetimes reinforcing the belief that a conch is yellow, it is not easy to accept that the same conch is actually white. When I look at my own hand and see its crooked knuckles, uneven fingers and battered nails, I know that what I see is the result of my ignorance and because I cling to my own idea of “self”—basically I am “ignorant.” If I were not ignorant, my hands would look perfect. But then, what is a “perfect” hand? If it were possible to describe, contemplate and imagine a perfect hand, then obviously it would not be perfect, which is why the hardest part of this process is to convince ourselves that we have all always been perfect buddhas.

We tend only to believe what we can see, and what we see is always filtered by our personal perception. Take the example of a man who is already extremely paranoid about imaginary problems and firmly believes he has ghostly tenants living in his cupboard. To tell such a person that the ghosts are a figment of his imagination will not help him at all because he is quite certain that ghosts exist. A far less time-consuming and more efficient approach would be to agree skilfully that in a way his paranoid delusion is real, and then offer a method for dispelling that delusion—such as calling in the Ghostbusters!

Other people may have the capacity to see things as they would a rainbow, vividly, even blatantly discernible, yet in reality they are aware that nothing is actually there. To this kind of person the vajrayana explains that an antidote does not look like its antithesis, but is identical to the problem itself. This is the opposite of how we usually think: if our problem is black, we expect the antidote to be white, or if the problem is a pencil mark, the antidote must be an eraser. Yet the most powerful kind of antidote often looks exactly like the problem it seeks to resolve. As it says in the tantric texts, the best method for removing water from your ear is to flush it out with yet more water.

## 3. Introduction to Ngöndro Practise

### “Ngöndro”: A Misnomer?

Unfortunately, the meaning of the word *ngöndro,* “preliminary,” is a little misleading and often misinterpreted. Practitioners tend to assume that “preliminary” implies ngöndro practise is little more than a series of obstacles they should get out of the way as quickly as possible. In the same way that, having learnt to read, we no longer need to think about the alphabet, they imagine that once they are done with ngöndro accumulations, they will finally be able to sink their teeth into the “real” practise. It’s a shame, because although it’s true that ngöndro is a necessary prerequisite for many other practises, by undervaluing it we lose sight of its true meaning and purpose.

The ngöndro is a series of practises that involve a great deal of repetition. A custom has grown up of repeating each element at least one hundred thousand times, and there are many very specific instructions about how each practise should be carried out, which substances and ritual implements need to be gathered, and how offerings should be made. Accumulating practises is an important and useful method, particularly for lackadaisical, undisciplined people like me, who find having a preset goal helps overcome laziness and eventually achieve something worthwhile—which is precisely why such goals were originally set. As practitioners, we should exploit every method available to try to discipline ourselves, and for those who are able to practise in retreat, dividing each day into three or four sessions is also an excellent method.

Patrul Rinpoche tells us that ngöndro is the foundation of all vajrayana practise and therefore even more important than the so-called “main” practises. If ngöndro were just a passport to more significant practises, great masters would not bother with it once they had completed the requisite accumulations. But they do, as I witnessed personally when I spent time with Kyabje Dilgo Khyentse during the latter part of his life and saw him practise the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro regularly—which alone demonstrates just how vital a practise it is.

Vajrasattva, mandala offering and guru yoga are all vajrayana methods, while taking refuge and arousing bodhichitta are usually categorized as shravakayana and bodhisattvayana practises, respectively. Which of these methods do you think is unnecessary? Which would you leave out? The answer, of course, is none. Each practise is equally important, because ngöndro brings together the essence of all vehicles in a nutshell, contains everything that we as practitioners need to work with, and all that is necessary on the spiritual path.

### The Structure of the Ngöndro

Taking refuge is the first of the preliminary practises, and its purpose is to divert us from the wrong path and onto the right path. Once safely on the right path, we then direct ourselves from the lesser path to the greater path by arousing bodhichitta, which is the embodiment of the entire bodhisattvayana path. But before we pour the nectar of dharma into the container of our body, speech and mind, it must first be cleansed and purified through the practise of visualizing our guru as Vajrasattva. Then to enrich ourselves with the ability to understand and maintain the practise, not least to complete it, we must accumulate an unimaginable amount of merit, and so we offer the mandala. And finally, guru yoga. The only way to achieve liberation quickly is to realise the nature of mind, and such a realisation can only be accomplished by mind itself. As our minds are currently so rigid, distracted and confused, they need training, but to follow the more general methods takes aeons and just to arouse the necessary enthusiasm to embark on such a long path requires immense effort. So, for those impatient to realise the nature of their mind this lifetime, the only truly effective method available is to receive the blessings of the guru, and this is why we practise the most profound of all paths, guru yoga.

### A Few Words of Advice Before You Begin

#### Patience: You Will Make Mistakes

I often tell practitioners, especially beginners, that no one can learn everything there is to know about how to do the ngöndro correctly and *then* start to practise. It is only possible to know everything, including how to perform all the rituals correctly, once the practise has been completed and fully accomplished. Inevitably, therefore, you will constantly make mistakes, large and small, and it is through these mistakes that you learn. No language student imagines they can learn the entire vocabulary and grammar of a new language in one go, then immediately speak it perfectly. We pick up language as we go along, make mistakes, stumble over words here and there and improvise. Eventually, though, everything comes together and suddenly we find we are fluent. Dharma practise follows a similar pattern, and most of what we learn comes to us through the mistakes we make as we practise.

Bear in mind that between starting to practise and the achievement of your first real taste of dharma will be a lengthy period of intense effort. But once you have experienced that first taste, you will be able to practise in every situation, in all the corners of the world and with every conceivable kind of person. And not only will you relax in yourself, but you will no longer sit in judgment on others for not practicing “properly.”

#### Asking Questions

Modern practitioners feel the need to ask the most detailed questions over and over again, but it’s really not necessary, especially since the vast majority of the so-called problems practitioners think up are barely problems at all. Lamas who lack courage tend to feel pressured into responding to such questions and work hard to come up with impressive-sounding responses. Their students dutifully write down every word they utter, then return the next day with a new problem that looks almost exactly the same as the old one.

The main reason we receive teachings is first to contemplate them and then to put them into practise, and questions about when to eat, or whether it is better to practise in the morning or evening, do not need to be asked every time you start a new practise. These days, though, asking too many questions has become yet another rather sophisticated form of laziness. It’s as if you want someone else to do it all for you: practise, answer your questions, make the necessary arrangements and fix it so that all you have to do is experience realisation.

In the case of those who want to do a ngöndro retreat, the instructions about how to practise each section of the ngöndro are the same as those for daily practise, and so you will need no more information than appears in these pages.

#### The Place and Time to Practise

It doesn’t matter *where* you practise, so try not to be too fussy. If you are, you risk expending all your time and energy on creating, in minute detail, the right conditions for practise and leave no time at all for the practise itself.

The great Longchenpa said that it is relatively easy to pacify mental dullness on mountaintops, where our minds are naturally clearer, so if you are ever offered the opportunity to spend time in the mountains and have received teachings on practises related to visualisation, shamatha and vipashyana, these are the practises you should do. He also said that to contemplate impermanence in rocky places is very beneficial, as it helps a sense of sadness about the sufferings of samsaric life to arise in our minds. Therefore caves are good places to practise both vipashyana and shamatha meditation. To sit by running water is said to develop a sense of urgency about practise that encourages renunciation and a revulsion for samsara, and to practise in cemeteries brings many blessings and great accomplishment very quickly.

So, if it is easy for you to practise in a cave or by a river or in a cemetery, by all means follow Longchenpa’s advice, but if not, there is no need to waste time trying. The most crucial prerequisite for the practise of dharma is complete isolation because when we are alone, we are subject to fewer distractions, creating the perfect conditions for sadness to grow in our minds. For those who know how to use it, sadness is a fertile ground from which all kinds of beneficial thoughts can spring with very little effort. Jigme Lingpa described sadness as one of the most invaluable kinds of noble wealth, and in the sutras Buddha hailed sadness as the trailblazer for all subsequent good qualities.

With sadness comes trust and devotion, which, once developed, mean the practises of shamatha and vipashyana require very little effort. Shamatha practise ensures that mind becomes malleable and workable, and a flexible mind makes vipashyana relatively easy to accomplish. As the Buddha said to the gathering of monks during his teachings on the vinaya, discipline helps us maintain samadhi, becoming accustomed to samadhi lengthens our periods of sobriety, and sobriety is none other than wisdom. Having realised wisdom, we are no longer bound by desire, anger and ignorance, and are able to perceive all phenomena as they truly are.

These days, though, just to find the time to be entirely alone to practise can be extremely problematic, especially for those with families, and so to organize the ideal place would be an unimaginable luxury. So keep it simple and do your best to be completely alone for an hour or two every day. As Jigme Lingpa said, the greatest merit of all is to be able to remain alone and do nothing but practise the dharma; even an offering of the entire world and everything in it would not be enough to merit the chance of practicing in isolation. He also said it is only possible to see the flaws of samsara and the benefits of nirvana in solitude. So pray from the bottom of your heart that one day you will encounter such an opportunity.

Another very good reason for remaining alone is that it is so rare to meet unbiased people who compliment the dharma and harbor no envy.

If we still find it difficult to create both the opportunity and the right conditions to live a solitary life, at the very least we can try to set our mind apart from confusion. As Kongtrul Rinpoche said, of all experiences of isolation, the segregation of mind from confusion is the supreme retreat, the supreme solitude, the supremely secluded place. So, when you find yourself caught up in a crowd, perhaps at a party or a football match, try, for a few moments, not to be involved with what is going on around you.

#### You Should Be Frustrated

Practicing dharma is necessarily a frustrating business. What practitioners, especially beginners, often fail to realise is that frustrations are the signposts of our success. An exasperating lack of concentration, devotion or inspiration might be just what you need to make the extra effort to tune in to your practise fully. Alternatively, of course, it may topple you in the other direction and stop you practicing altogether—a temptation you must resist at all costs. Always remember, though, that frustration with your spiritual path is often an indication that you are becoming a genuine dharma practitioner.

#### Good Days and Bad Days

The character of our practise will change depending on whether we are in retreat or practicing in everyday life. As the human constitution is irrevocably bound to the constantly changing elements that make up the universe, human beings could be said to be little more than by-products of such interactions. As a result, our physical constitution and mind are perpetually changing; one day our meditation is inspiring and encouraging, because we can concentrate easily and visualise clearly, and the next a dull, frustrating disaster. But we must not let these experiences colour our expectations of practise.

When practise goes well, try not to get overexcited or use that level of concentration and inspiration as a benchmark for all future practise. Tsele Natsok Rangdröl said that dharma practitioners should not be like the child who becomes so excited in a playground full of toys that she is unable to choose one to play with and ends up doing nothing at all. When your practise goes badly, don’t let it undermine or erode your determination. The advice Jigme Lingpa gives is, when suddenly faced with bad circumstances and obstacles, consider them all to be the compassionate blessings of the guru and the dharma, and the result of practise.

Our lives will be stirred up by practise. We might even attract obstacles, like Shakyamuni Buddha who attracted Mara’s wrath in the hours before he achieved enlightenment. Difficulties are therefore a sign that your practise is working and should make you happy.

The key is consistency. Often what happens is that in the heat of inspiration practitioners overdose on practise, then feel deeply frustrated when they fail to experience a good dream, or cannot concentrate properly or control their temper. Having gorged themselves on practise, they stop for a few months, and when they eventually return to it find they are right back at square one. At this rate, progress is very slow. A far better approach is that of the tortoise. Each step may seem to take forever, but no matter how uninspired you feel, continue to follow your practise schedule precisely and consistently. This is how we can use our greatest enemy, habit, against itself. Habit clings to us like a bloodsucking leech, becoming more rigid and stubborn by the moment, and even if we manage to flick it off, we are still left with an itchy reminder of its existence. By becoming accustomed to regular dharma practise, though, we use our enemy against itself by countering our bad habits with the good habit of practise. And as Shantideva pointed out, nothing is difficult once you get used to it.

#### Supports for Practise

If you have somewhere to live, it might be a good idea to make yourself a shrine, and although it need not be elaborate, always include a statue of the Buddha Shakyamuni. As Nagarjuna mentioned in his *Letter to a Friend,* even a wooden image of the Tathagata should be considered to be the Tathagata himself. Longchenpa agreed. He said that all images of the Buddha should be thought of as manifestations of the Buddha. Just the fact that a statue has been made in the form of the Buddha means it is automatically blessed by him, and therefore precious and not to be mistaken for an inanimate object. Often, though, vajrayana students fill their shrines with images of vajrayana deities and omit the Buddha altogether, which makes it hard to tell whether they are Buddhist at all. While you practise ngöndro, it might help to include a representation of the field of merit specific to your practise in the form of a thangka, a painting or a line drawing. And remember that the purpose of a shrine is as a support for your practise, a reminder of the Buddha, the dharma and the sangha, and a reminder of mindfulness.

Shrines should be inspiring and clean, not like a mantelpiece piled high with don’t-know-what-to-do-with garbage. Photos of lamas can be inspiring, but remember not to flaunt them like some kind of plaque or certificate as a way of boasting about your connections.

Buddha himself said that in the future he will appear as writing on a page, so we should always wash our hands before handling sacred texts. We should also always show the greatest respect for representations of the Buddha wherever they may be, near or far, up or down, and one excellent method is to discipline ourselves to be mindful about not stepping over holy objects such as dharma texts and monks’ robes. It is a habit Indian musicians formed millennia ago. They revere and pay homage to their instruments as a matter of course, and would never think of stepping over them.

Dharma texts these days are often photocopied rather shoddily. Nevertheless, the words that appear on those grubby pages have the power to liberate us from delusion, and by making the effort not to step on or over a text in itself accumulates a great deal of merit. As a result, the next time you read a dharma text, you will find you are able to understand the words of the Buddha even more clearly. And so, when you dedicate the merit of your activities towards the enlightenment of all sentient beings, always add, “May all dharma practitioners never tread on their dharma books.” It may require a bit of reconditioning, but is a good habit to adopt.

## 4. Your Spiritual Warm-Up

It may be difficult to accept, but right now, this very moment, is the perfect time for you to start practicing, regardless of the many reasons you have, good and bad, for trying to put it off. Perhaps you would rather wait until you are less busy at work, or win the lottery, or get over your partner leaving you, but life will never be free from disruptions and to wait for things to settle down will merely ensure that you never, ever take the first step. So, no matter how tumultuous your life, today, this very moment, is the best time for you to start to practise, and here are a few suggestions and recipes to help get you going.

### The Four Attitudes of Practitioners

The development of these four attitudes is strongly recommended, even if only on an aspirational level, as they help trim the dangling threads that so easily become entangled in the complex trappings of samsara.

#### 1. The Attitude of a Wounded Deer

In the same way a wounded deer seeks solitude in which to heal, find an isolated spot where you have the time and space to practise.

#### 2. The Attitude of a Lion

Be as courageous as a lion and pay absolutely no attention to difficult situations or bad news that, were they to distract and suck you in, would only lead to far more serious problems in the future.

#### 3. The Attitude of the Wind

The wind is indiscriminate about where it blows. In the same way, do not try to summon good circumstances or avoid bad ones.

#### 4. The Attitude of the Crazy

Like a crazy person, harbour no preference for or aversion to any of the eight worldly dharmas; for example, care nothing for either praise or criticism.

Jetsün Rinpoche Drakpa Gyaltsen of the Sakyapas said that as long as we remain attached to our belongings, house and family, we will not even attempt to extricate ourselves from samsara. Therefore, we should begin by practicing *generosity* and *discipline*.

Once we have escaped from the trap of samsara, it is imperative that we not fall back into it. Often, though, bodhisattvas lose heart, because the countless sentient beings they meet are so exhausting and the whole process so dispiriting. Working for the benefit of others can feel like swimming, naked and alone, across an immense ocean, and just a glimpse of the far-distant, watery horizon is disheartening and terribly depressing. If this happens, a bodhisattva will take far longer than is necessary to develop the more virtuous qualities of devotion, renunciation and not being distracted, and this is why we need to practise *patience* and *diligence*.

Lastly, even when all obstacles have been vanquished, we can still ruin everything if we allow ignorance and distraction to dominate our mind, and so we practise *samadhi* and *wisdom*.

### The Three Noble Principles

The three noble principles are indispensible to both bodhisattvayana and vajrayana practitioners.

#### 1. Arouse Bodhichitta

No matter which dharma practise you engage in, from ngöndro to offering a single candle, always do it with the intention that your practise will benefit all sentient beings. In this context, “benefit” does not only mean giving practical help, such as offering food or medicine, or feeding people’s emotions, egos and delusions. Here, “benefit” includes aspiring to be instrumental in the enlightenment of all sentient beings; without such an aspiration, it is easy for dharma practise to become self-serving. It’s vital always to bear in mind that we practise for the sake of all other beings, and that the enormity of this aspiration is what makes dharma practise both extremely powerful and inexhaustible, virtually guaranteeing that the result will be infinitely beneficial.

#### 2. Apply the Awareness of Nonduality

While practicing or performing dharma activities, we must remain constantly aware that everything we do is illusory—or at least try to bring that thought to mind. If we prick our flesh, our logical mind tells us we will feel pain. The pain itself will feel real because the idea that phenomena are both solid and truly existing has an almost unbreakably strong hold on us. We must therefore try to get used to the notion that everything we see, do and think is an interpretation created by our mind, which itself is an important stepping-stone towards the practise of nonduality. And “getting used” to it means reminding ourselves about it over and over again. For example, when your knees start to hurt as you accumulate prostrations, remind yourself that the “I” in “I am doing prostrations” and the “my” in “my knees hurt” are both mind-made illusions.

Remembering everything you experience is created by mind is also the direct antidote to pride and ego, and once it becomes second nature, you will no longer cling to your dharma activities. This does not mean you will not practise. On the contrary, in the same way someone dying of thirst cannot resist taking large gulps of water, once you know everything is an illusion, your only thoughts will be about the dharma. Of course, the dharma itself is the antidote to ego, but for those who take pride in being good practitioners, dharma activities can be just another means of boosting their egos. And this is why it is so important to remember that absolutely everything we experience is just a product of mind, even if it’s only for five minutes a day.

The classic question students often ask at this point is, “If everything I experience is just a product of my mind, is there such a thing as ‘accumulating merit’?” In this context, the concept that merit either exists or does not exist is just another of mind’s constructions.

At first, it may be difficult to arouse the motivation of bodhichitta and remember nonduality every time you act. It’s also unlikely that you will immediately be able to meditate on emptiness for a whole hour each day. Instead, start by trying to remember that everything you see and experience is merely the product of your own perception. However simple your dharma activity, for example offering a flower to your teacher, remember that although you accumulate merit by making the offering, in reality the idea of accumulating merit is itself a creation of your mind. At every opportunity, get used to the thought that everything you perceive is produced by mind and there is no such thing as a truly existing “holy” activity.

These realizations will have a profound effect on the way you function, first and foremost because they release you from the consequences of defilements like pride and jealousy. And when your teacher throws the flower you offered to the ground without glancing at it, you won’t mind at all.

#### 3. Dedicate

Always conclude your practise by not only dedicating any merit you may have attained towards your own well-being, but also for the benefit and enlightenment of all sentient beings. And you don’t have to wait for the end of your practise session to do it; you can dedicate at any time, for example after every prostration, so you can be sure that none is wasted. You can also dedicate the merit you may have forgotten to dedicate in all your past lives, by saying something like,

> I dedicate all the merit I have just accumulated,

> All the merit I have no memory of accumulating in my past lives, and

> All the merit I will accumulate in the future

> Towards the benefit and enlightenment of all sentient beings.

However experienced you are as a dharma practitioner, it is crucial to bear in mind that the only way to ensure that all your actions, not just formal spiritual practise, are beneficial for yourself and others is by applying these three noble principles.

Be ambitious about the magnitude of the motivation you arouse. Don’t settle for simple kindness when nothing less than the full-fledged mind of bodhichitta is what is needed. Kyabje Dudjom Rinpoche said that dharma practise is really not that difficult, it’s all a matter of motivation. So never forget to arouse the motivation of wanting to bring all sentient beings to complete enlightenment. And the more magnanimous your motivation, the more merit you will accumulate, even when all you do is light a candle.

- If you light a candle merely as a decoration for the living room, your motivation is that of an ordinary person.
- If you light it with the wish to accumulate merit and eventually destroy samsara, you share the attitude cultivated by shravakayana practitioners.
- To light the candle with the wish that any merit attained be dedicated to the enlightenment of all sentient beings, your attitude is the same as that of bodhisattvayana practitioners.
- To consider the candle to be the light of wisdom that illuminates all sentient beings, with the aspiration that wherever its light falls becomes the mandala, is the attitude of a tantric practitioner.

Most of the time, though, we cannot seem to remember these crucial instructions, and when we do, putting them into practise often becomes unnecessarily complicated. I have heard many practitioners these days say they want to do long retreats, or make enormous offerings to their teacher, or some other grandiose gesture that will accumulate a great deal of merit in one go. In practise, however, they have neither the time nor the resources to do anything at all. And, ironically, such gestures are really not necessary. All any one of us needs to do to accumulate a vast treasury of merit is to seal our every action with the motivation of bodhichitta. Then, by offering your teacher a single flower, thinking, “May this offering ultimately benefit all sentient beings,” you will accumulate immeasurable merit. Dedication is therefore an extremely simple yet powerful method for accumulating merit. Sadly, the majority of practitioners seem to consider it too insignificant a practise for them to notice, which indicates they lack the merit to be able to understand the profound power of dedicating merit.

So, these are the three noble principles. If you can remember to apply them to all your daily activities, you will quickly become great dharma practitioner.

### Cut the Chain of Thoughts

It is a good idea to avoid plunging into a practise session without having first cut the chain of thoughts. Suppose you quarrel with your fiancée just before you start to practise; with a mind full of thoughts and reactions to what just happened, your ability to concentrate will evaporate. So, rather than immediately opening your practise book, first take a few moments to cut that chain of thoughts.

There are countless methods you can use. For example, sit quietly with your back straight and legs crossed if it’s comfortable, or in a chair if you prefer, and start to practise. Every time a thought comes along, remind yourself about how rare and precious it is to have been born a human being, the imminence and unpredictability of death, the terrible sufferings of this life, the karmic consequences of your actions that cannot be avoided, and anything else you can think of that will help develop a sense of renunciation for worldly life. Bring these thoughts to mind over and over again, and even if you only practise for ten minutes a day, by devoting two or three to cutting the chain of thoughts, your mind will be completely transformed.

### Discipline as a Preparation

Nagarjuna said that discipline is like the ground we walk on; if there were no ground, planting flowers and vegetables would be impossible and there would be nowhere to put a table. Likewise, discipline is the ground, or foundation, for all virtuous activity. (Even to make a good espresso requires a certain amount of discipline.) And to do anything really well, we first need the discipline to learn the rules and accepted practises.

Depression is one of the biggest challenges faced by modern people. It is often triggered by bad habits that are the result of a lack of discipline, such as procrastinating over taking out the garbage, or longing to be lazy, or hating routine. Were depressed people to apply a little discipline to their lives, I feel sure that a huge chunk of their depression would simply disappear. Imagine, for example, that your doctor puts you on a low-carbohydrate diet because carbs precipitate depression. A couple of days into the diet you spend an evening at an excellent Italian restaurant with friends who order some delicious-looking pasta. You think to yourself, “I have only just started this diet, what harm can one plate of pasta do? I’ll make it my last ever and start the diet tomorrow.” So you break your discipline and quickly sink into a post-pasta depression. This is precisely how a lack of discipline generates depression.

Avoiding carbohydrates is an ordinary, everyday kind of discipline, but other, more extraordinary disciplines are regularly employed on the spiritual path: celibacy, for example; shaving your head, or never cutting your hair; or getting up at six A.M. every day even though you are not a morning person. Activities you enjoy can also be described as disciplines, such as taking a walk in the park every Wednesday. Whatever the activity, if you maintain the discipline of practicing it regularly, your mind (the storehouse of habitual patterns) will be prevented from sinking into depression even when you are forced to do things you don’t enjoy. In other words, discipline is a way of ensuring that mind does not become spoilt.

Since avoiding routine can result in depression, a lack of discipline early in life could cause a great many problems later on. A child who has become accustomed to being denied nothing is likely to grow into an unhappy adult simply because he has never learnt how to deal with the anger that arises in his undisciplined mind when he doesn’t get his own way, and the almost inevitable temper tantrum that follows.

Within the context of ngöndro practise, it takes discipline to practise prostrations. It may not be possible for you to do five hundred prostrations a day, but everyone has the time to do three, five, seven or ten, and if you also adopt the discipline of counting your prostrations, you will not be able to avoid eventually completing one hundred thousand, or perhaps even five hundred thousand. Do bear in mind, though, that dharma practise is not a race to the finish line of complete enlightenment.

Practitioners tend to be self-conscious about how many or how few prostrations they have accumulated, but they should probably be more embarrassed about how much or how little discipline they have. Try to discipline yourself to practise every day instead of just once in a while; or practise for half an hour every day, instead of five minutes one day and an hour the next. Or try dressing up for your practise to help make it a special and important event in your day instead of crawling to your meditation cushion in your pyjamas and rattling off your prayers parrot-fashion, as if you were being forced to pay some kind of spiritual tax.

### Remembering the Guru Is the Best Preparation for Practise

For a vajrayana practitioner, it is vital to learn to think constantly about the guru. Not just for the sake of the information he passes on to you as your spiritual instructor, but more importantly because to think of your guru is to think of the Buddha. And ultimately, bringing your guru to mind is exactly the same as remembering the nature of your mind.

One method to help us remember our guru is to recite inspiring prayers written by great masters of the past—Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé’s *Crying to the Guru,* often translated as *Calling the Guru from Afar,* for example. If you don’t like reading prayers, a more spontaneous approach is to make up your own invocation on the spot. There is no need to worry about the technicalities of writing “poetry,” or to feel inhibited by your lack of eloquence. The point here is to tell your teacher precisely what is troubling you (within reason; to ask him to fix your plumbing would be a little inappropriate). Best of all is to call on your guru with the prayer that your dharma practise will never be derailed, but on the contrary be completely successful, and that you will be of benefit not only to yourself but to all sentient beings.

Many people today cherish the desire to practise the dharma and long to renounce worldly life, but are beset by mountains of obstacles. Too quickly their desire to practise is smothered by ordinary thoughts that are the offspring of habitual laziness. Yet the only thing they have to do to fulfill their wish is remember the guru. Have you ever lost one end of the drawstring that keeps your pyjamas up? Remembering the guru is like managing to grasp that lost end, so that you can then pull it out and tie up your pyjamas.

### Three Pith Instructions for Before You Begin to Practise

#### 1. Expelling the Stale Air

A very good way of preparing yourself for practise is to “expel the stale air.” It’s a practise that is best learnt from someone who can show you how to do it personally, but if that is not possible, try following this very simple method.

Having spent a few minutes cutting your chain of thoughts, reestablish a good posture and make sure your back is straight. As the Buddha said, all phenomena is “dependant arising” and that includes the body and the mind. But whereas mind can seem virtually unreachable, our bodies are far more accessible, and by simply straightening your back, the power of dependent arising will ensure that prana and nadi function properly, making realization far more possible. (Ideally, practitioners should practise the seven-point posture of Vairochana.)

- Breathe in through both nostrils, filling your lungs completely.
- Bend the index finger of your left hand and press the finger pad just above the knuckle of your middle finger. (This shape is one half of a half-vajra, which is made by creating the same shape with both hands and pressing them together.)
- Close your left nostril with the middle finger of your left hand and empty your lungs by breathing out strongly through your right nostril.

> —As you breathe out, imagine all your anger and frustration is expelled from your body in the form of dirty, polluted smoke.

> —As you breathe in, imagine all the wisdom and compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas dissolves into you in the form of white light.

- Release your left nostril.
- Bend the index finger of your right hand and press the finger pad just above the knuckle of your middle finger.
- Close your right nostril with the middle finger of your right hand and empty your lungs by breathing out strongly through your left nostril.

> —As you breathe out, imagine all your desire is expelled from your body in the form of dark red light.

> —As you breathe in, imagine all the wisdom and compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas dissolves into you in the form of white light.

- Release your right nostril.
- Leaving both nostrils open, breathe in then out strongly through both nostrils.

> —As you breathe out, imagine all your ignorance billowing out in a dark cloud.

> —As you breathe in, imagine all the wisdom and compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas dissolves into you in the form of white light.

- Once you have completed the practise, breathe normally.

Do not linger over or obsess about the details of these visualisations, just consider whatever you visualise as really happening. And do not dwell on any one part of the visualisation and start asking questions about it, for example, how bright should the white light be? The purpose of vajrayana visualisation is to occupy our minds with extraordinary rather than mundane thoughts, and to get caught up in too much detail is precisely the kind of activity that will open the door for obstacles to enter your practise, such as falling back into your ordinary, habitual way of thinking. Simply follow each step of the practise, and when one step has been completed, move quickly on to the next.

Having said that, if you wish to follow a specific practise tradition, it is best to consult a teacher experienced in that tradition.

#### 2. Transform the Environment into a Buddha Realm

The vajrayana is the path that transforms our habitually impure perception so we can perceive everything and everyone we encounter purely. Therefore, wherever you practise—at home, or in Germany, Hong Kong, Australia or at a meditation centre in the south of France—convince yourself beyond any shadow of doubt that you are in a completely pure realm.

“Impure” in this context has nothing to do with filthy streets or piles of rubbish, like those in Kathmandu. Dualistic perceptions are considered “impure” when you get stuck with the idea that a ceiling can only be a ceiling, or a floor can only be a floor, or a thousand people could not possibly fit into a broom cupboard. In other words, the dualistic distinctions you make limit the manifestation of a phenomenon to a specific purpose. By contrast, you have pure perception when you no longer fixate on a single perception as an exclusive phenomenon and instead connect with your inherent ability to see and accept that one thousand people can easily fit into a tiny broom cupboard. While your ordinary way of perceiving the world still operates, you are adamant that it is absolutely impossible to fit so many people into a such tiny space; as soon as your perception begins to shift, you are equally adamant that they can.

Once you accept that anything is possible, a buddha realm will no longer be like one of those far-distant planets visited by the Starship Enterprise from time to time, nor the sentimentalized vision of heaven so firmly embedded in the popular psyche. But how can we genuinely come to accept that anything is possible? The answer is, through ngöndro practise.

If Guru Rinpoche is the main deity in your ngöndro text, visualise yourself in the realm of the Copper-Coloured Mountain. Of course, this does not mean that you try to imagine the ceilings and walls are made of copper! Instead, adjust your ordinary perceptions with the thought, “What I see is not how things are.” Tell yourself that all the “things” you are surrounded by are not bound by bricks, mortar, highways and traffic, nor hosts of heavenly angels. Each “thing” is infinite, and each object, such as the pen lying on your desk, encompasses billions and billions of pure realms. This is how you start to adjust your mind so that ultimately you will be able accept that anything is possible.

The *transformation* of perception is the quintessence of vajrayana practise, but it is important it is not mistaken for the complete elimination of perception. To transform perception is to change your attitude to the way you perceive the world and is a process you should go through daily.

#### 3. Create an Inspiring Atmosphere and Environment

If you can, create an inspiring atmosphere in which to practise by cleaning your practise space, burning incense, arranging beautiful pictures of the Buddha, and making offerings. But even though we are conditioned to be affected by our surroundings, be careful, as mentioned before in this book, not to fall into the trap of spending more time and energy on creating an atmosphere than doing the practise itself.

### Calling the Guru

The name of this practise, Calling the Guru, or Crying to the Guru, is in itself very interesting because it implies that it is necessary both to remind the guru that we exist and to call on him for help. The heart of dharma practise is mindfulness and, as we have been told repeatedly, the main task of a practitioner is to remain undistracted. Therefore, with mindfulness as the core of our practise, the moment we think of our guru, we have called to him and can be certain he has heard us. In a way, thinking of the guru is the most effective kind of mindfulness. But as most of us are unable to think of him constantly, another way of putting it is that to invoke the guru is to ask our own mind to remember him.

By reciting any one of the extraordinary Calling the Guru prayers that exist, not only are we crying out to our guru for help, we are reminding ourselves of the habitual faults common to all dharma practitioners. To accept and understand our faults and weaknesses is an extremely valuable exercise, although recently it has become rather unfashionable. A genuinely spiritual individual’s personal reflections on their own character are far more likely to be critical than those of someone born in the modern world, where, particularly in America, far more emphasis is placed on building self-esteem and being encouraging than on critical analysis. To more modern eyes, therefore, a practise that highlights critical reflection might appear on the surface to be the product of an alien culture. But is it? Is the kind of reflection we find in Jigme Lingpa’s *Invoking the Lama* (from the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro) and Kongtrul Rinpoche’s *Calling the Guru from Afar* merely the product of Tibetan cultural influences on the dharma?

I have thought about this point a great deal and I can say with some confidence that no, such critical reflections are not culturally inspired. The whole purpose of the dharma is to dismantle the protective system we have created for ourselves that we call “ego,” and the purpose behind each syllable of the dharma and every one of its methods is to contradict, disrupt and rip that ego apart until the goal of complete liberation from it has finally been achieved. Traditionally, the gashes and dents we make in ego’s cocoon are said to herald the dawn of a genuine dharma practitioner.

Most of us, though, are not dharma practitioners at all. We may be dharma students who are interested in and inspired by the dharma, but to be a practitioner is quite a different matter. A practitioner can both see the futility of the eight worldly dharmas and make the extremely difficult, but not impossible, heroic effort necessary to give them up. On the other hand, the whole point of study for a student of dharma is bound up in the eight worldly dharmas: we study because we are ambitious and want to increase our knowledge, or win the heart of another innocent and naïve dharma student, or become a spiritual guru of one sort or another.

Those who have time or who are in retreat should read as many of the great *Calling the Guru* prayers as they can. In Jamgön Kongtrul’s *Calling the Guru* and Jigme Lingpa’s *Invoking the Lama,* we call to our gurus and blast holes in our ego by reminding ourselves of our most insidious faults. I strongly recommend Kongtrul Rinpoche’s prayer because it invokes gurus from many different lineages and is therefore particularly good from the Rimé (nonsectarian) point of view. (Sectarianism is one of the most vicious kinds of spiritual materialism.)

### The Common Foundations

Contemplation of the “common foundations,” sometimes known as the four thoughts that turn the mind away from samsara, is a classic method for preparing yourself to practise the dharma. It still works extremely well to this day and is not in the least archaic. Of course, it’s best if you can keep these contemplations in mind at all times, but if nothing else, try to read them daily at the beginning of your practise.

#### 1. The Preciousness of This Human Body

Here we develop our appreciation for having both the ability and the opportunity to practise the dharma in this lifetime. To have a human body is extremely important for dharma practise. Unlike animals or those who live in countries overrun by gangsters and private armies, the majority of you reading this book enjoy a fundamental sense of personal security. Your lives are not at risk on a daily basis and you don’t spend all your waking hours in a desperate search for food and a safe place to sleep. Most of us are even able to appreciate great endeavours and remarkable beings, and once in a blue moon great historical figures like Milarepa inspire us with awe and wonder. Above all, we can make the wish, “May all sentient beings be happy” and not consider it a waste of time. Therefore in some ways, we do have the qualities that qualify us as having a precious human body.

The trouble is we do not necessarily take advantage of our good fortune. For example, there is nothing to stop any one of us from spending an hour with friends in the local coffee shop, whereas very few beings in the other realms are able to enjoy the luxury of wasting time in this way. Imagine what it must be like to have been born in Jerusalem or the Gaza Strip, perpetually under threat of being shot or blown to pieces. So, when the opportunity to enjoy the luxuries of leisure time and personal security presents itself, we really must try to appreciate and use it well.

To be born a human being is extremely precious because, to some degree, human beings have the intellectual capacity to understand the nature of suffering and its causes. Beings in the hell realms, on the other hand, know only the unrelenting and unimaginably intense pain and agony that never ceases to numb and stupefy them. Beings in the god realms know only uninterrupted happiness and possess everything they could possibly want. Consequently they lack the experience of sadness and the curiosity to examine their minds. Of the six realms of samsara, therefore, human beings, who are neither too happy nor too overwhelmed by suffering and pain, have the greatest capacity for understanding suffering.

Buddha explained that human life becomes “precious” when a human being is free and rich. So, what is it that prevents us from being free? Distraction. If we had no distractions, we would be free; and if we were mindful, we would be rich. So, don’t imagine that a human body qualifies as being “precious” just because it is healthy, educated and well connected. It only becomes precious once that person has met the dharma and embarked on spiritual practise.

#### 2. Impermanence

A practitioner’s concern for personal happiness, fame and comfort diminishes as their understanding of impermanence increases, and a large part of learning how to appreciate impermanence is to develop a sense of how urgently we need to practise. None of us has much time. Today might be the last day of your current life; tonight’s supper might be your last; and there is absolutely no guarantee that any of us will live long enough to see tomorrow, let alone live forever.

In this dangerous and unhealthy world, it would be quite an achievement for someone who is fifty years old today to live to be eighty. The lives of most fifty-year-olds are already more than half over, and the older we get, the quicker time seems to pass. The thirty years we imagine we have left will pass in the blink of an eye. For a start, we sleep for about eight hours a night, which accounts for ten of those thirty years. Let’s assume that watching one movie a day and eating three times takes about four hours. We also gossip and catch up with friends, check the football results, do housework, pay bills, keep in touch with family and exercise, all of which probably eats up about two hours a day. And of course, most of us work for seven or eight hours a day. Therefore, if we fifty-year-olds are lucky, we have less than two hours a day, or about two and a half years, left to live. And a great deal of that will be taken up with paranoia, anxiety, self-doubt and so on. So the bottom line here is there is very little time left for practise.

#### 3. Karma

Longchenpa said that although ultimately the sun of our true nature continuously rises, it is obscured by the white clouds of our virtuous actions and the black clouds of our failings. All effort to abandon bad karma and accumulate good constantly flashes like lightning and is followed by a deluge of confusion that is both happy and unhappy. This, he tells us, is how samsara’s harvest is fortified. “Alas! How pitiful!”

We are all subject to the laws of cause, condition and effect, and as such not one of us enjoys true independence. We take great pains to gather the causes and conditions that will bring us the longed-for result of “success” or “fun,” but forget that those very causes and conditions function in a similar way to antibiotics. When we take antibiotics, on the one hand we feel better because one set of symptoms has been relieved, but on the other we feel worse because the antibiotics themselves upset other parts of our bodies. Without being aware of it, everything we do in pursuit of independence, wealth and success is also the cause of all the subsequent causes and conditions we experience that will rule and control the direction our lives then take. Therefore, we cannot guarantee that our plans will turn out as originally intended.

When we experience what our limited perception would describe as “favorable circumstances”—we get a place at our first-choice university, or land a plum job—we attribute our good fortune to hard work; and when an unpleasant event sweeps us down an unexpected path, we become confused. We forget that during the process of gathering the causes and conditions for success, we will also have planted those that result in rather less welcome situations. Our plans might work out once or twice, or even a dozen times, but as we are entirely vulnerable to and dependent on causes and conditions that are beyond our control, there is always the chance they won’t work out at all.

Ironically, to be successful in worldly life requires that we gather specific causes and conditions, such as a good education, honest business partners, reliable legal advice, and so on, but in the process, the very mechanism that allows us to assemble these causes and conditions also ensures that we are vulnerable to them. And this is what we forget. We are convinced we are invulnerable, and unless this false sense of security is shattered, it is very difficult to be a genuinely spiritual person. Only by accepting our vulnerability are we able to loosen our grip on the false assumption that our lives will always go as planned.

Ultimately, in order to attain enlightenment, we must exhaust *all* our karma, both good and bad, not merely collect as much good karma as we can and get rid of the bad. Karma is by nature conditioning, and since all our activities are conditioned by karma, they can only be a direct contradiction to our independence. Therefore it is not possible for any of us to be truly independent.

The point of thinking about cause and effect before we start to practise each day is not merely to gather knowledge about karma’s complex functions and systems, but to remind ourselves that we have no control over anything at all.

It also leads us to our next subject: the defects of samsara.

#### 4. The Defects of Samsara

Sentient beings, like silkworms, create their own traps and die in them. Think, for example, of how we deal with the concept of money. Historically, human beings have been extremely diligent about developing this particular abstraction, and our appetite for suffering in the name of making more of it appears to be insatiable.

The pain of acid on bare flesh is the kind of gross suffering that is relatively easy for us to imagine, but there exists a wide variety of sufferings to which we are oblivious until we experience them personally. Most difficult of all is to identify and understand the *cause* of our suffering, especially when it turns out to be something that, until recently, we had considered “good.” A robust world economy, for example, is widely touted by our most famous financial experts as being a very good thing, and it’s true that in a healthy economic environment wonderful products become available to us. Yet, for those living on the other side of an economic boom, life is likely to be anything but good, let alone wonderful.

We often hear the cliché “Life is wonderful,” but is it? To interpret the lives most people lead as being wonderful is, at best, overly romantic, and if you do choose to see them this way, you have definitely not grasped the first noble truth: “Know suffering.” In reality, as life is made up of vast quantities of compounded, perpetually drifting and ever-shifting phenomena, it can only be awash with suffering, because compounded phenomena inevitably cause uncertainty and decay, and dismantles all we hold dear. And what’s so wonderful about that?

Uncertainty is one of samsara’s cruelest flaws. When modern people read in books like *The Words of My Perfect Teacher* about the intense suffering experienced by beings in the hell and hungry ghost realms, we rarely take it seriously. Falling back on our habitual skepticism, we charge Patrul Rinpoche with using scare tactics in the same way other religions use hellfire and damnation teachings to frighten their followers into toeing the line. We don’t want to believe in hell. We choose instead to believe that all eighteen are abstract Buddhist concepts, not even suspecting that the sufferings of hell, as well as those of all the other realms, can be seen and experienced right here in our own human realm.

If nothing else, a close examination of the ongoing poverty mentality that plagues most human beings (we always need more of everything and get worked up about nothing) as we occupy every moment of our day and are never able to relax shows us quite clearly just how painful human existence really is.

It is extremely beneficial for all practitioners to contemplate these common foundations, but especially so for beginners, as they make up one of the most highly praised of all methods for turning the mind towards the dharma and away from worldly values.

## 5. Using Your Imagination

### Visualisation Practise

The technique of visualisation is employed throughout ngöndro practise, and its use of our imaginations makes it quite different from other meditations, like shamatha practise. Imagination also plays a major part in our deluded experience of life. Everything we encounter and perceive in our daily lives is a product of our imagination, but as we believe in the illusions we create, they become such deeply rooted mental habits that we completely forget they are little more than fantasy. The imagination is therefore one of our most powerful tools, and working with it by changing and diluting how we look at our world is what we call the “practise of visualisation.”

One small problem for beginners is that the English word *visualisation* can be misleading. For most people, to visualise is to focus on an image and then hold it in their mind’s eye. But physical appearance is only one element of visualisation practise, and by no means the whole story. An individual’s attitudes and understanding changes according to their situation and education. Until very recently, Buddhist masters brought up in Tibet would have looked on salad, green vegetables and grass as animal fodder and would never willingly eat it themselves. Now that Tibetans have become familiar with food outside Tibet, their attitudes have changed, and it is precisely this kind of shift in our perception that we work with in “creation meditation.”

Another example can be found on the World Wide Web. Most erotic pictures are usually quite small—certainly nowhere near life-sized. Logically, it is hard to believe that such tiny images could cause living, breathing human beings to become aroused, but they do. Our habits are so entrenched that having programmed ourselves to respond to a specific kind of image, it will consistently have the power to turn us on or make us angry, sad or even depressed, even when we see it on a tiny 14 x 8 cm YouTube screen. To a certain extent, this is how visualisation works, and neither size nor so-called realism have anything to do with it.

Were you to tell a worldly friend that what we see around us—the houses, cars, trees and shops—do not truly exist as we believe we see them, he would most likely think you had finally lost it, or at the very least were terminally irrational. Yet, according to vajrayana theory, your perception of this world is unique; it is not seen or experienced in the same way by anyone else because what you see does not exist externally.

Vajrayana students who were born and brought up in the modern world often have difficulties with visualisation practise. Part of the problem, I think, is that Tibetan teachers like myself, assume all sentient beings process things the same way Tibetans do. We teach you to picture the Buddha as he is traditionally depicted in Tibet, adorned with ornaments that are valued by and convey specific meanings to Tibetans. But to become a perfect Tibetan iconographer is not the point of visualisation practise.

The main purpose of visualisation practise is to purify our ordinary, impure perception of the phenomenal world by developing “pure perception.” Unfortunately, though, pure perception is yet another notion that tends to be misunderstood. Students often try to re-create a photographic image of a Tibetan painting in their minds, with two-dimensional deities who never blink and are surrounded by clouds frozen in space, or whose consorts look like grown-up babies. To practise this erroneous version of visualisation is to instill in yourself a far worse form of perception than the one you were born with, and in the process the whole point of pure perception is destroyed.

What, then, is really meant by “impure perception”? As we have already seen, “impure” does not mean that the object of our visualisation is covered with dirt or polluted or defiled in any way; the impurity isn’t “out there.” “Impure,” in this context, means that the problem is “in here.” We look at the world through emotional filters that we label “desire,” “jealously,” “pride,” “ignorance” and “aggression.” Everything we perceive is coloured by myriad variations of these five emotions, many of which do not even have names. Imagine you go to a party and as you glance at someone you find attractive, your passion filter quickly clicks into place and you immediately label that person “desirable.” If someone else gets in the way, your aggression filter is activated and you label this person “hideous.” As the evening wears on, other people provoke your insecurities, causing you sit in judgment on them, make comparisons, defend your choices and bolster your personal pride by denigrating them, all of which is triggered by the filter of “profound ignorance.” And the list goes on and on.

All these different perceptions arise in our very own minds and are then filtered through our emotions. In fact, everything we experience, big and small, will always lead to disappointment because we perpetually forget that everything we perceive is a product of our own minds, and instead we fixate on perceptions “out there” that we are convinced truly exist. This is what we work with during the vajrayana practise of visualisation.

It’s all a matter of training the mind, and one of the many methods offered by the shravakayana is to relinquish clinging to “self” by disciplining body and speech using many of the methods that have already been mentioned, for example by shaving the head, begging for alms, wearing saffron-coloured robes and refraining from all kinds of worldly activities like getting married or having sex. Training the mind in the bodhisattvayana is about disciplining body and speech and, in addition, meditating on compassion, arousing bodhichitta and so on. Lastly, the vajrayana not only trains the mind through discipline and meditation on compassion, it also offers methods for transforming our impure perception into pure perception.

#### The Dissolution of a Visualisation

Ultimately, the most important goal of Buddhadharma, particularly the bodhisattvayana, is the realisation of nonduality. And one of the most effective methods for accomplishing that realisation is the practise of visualisation, central to which is the dissolution of the deities or gurus as they merge to become one with the practitioner. But how does the practise work?

Imagine the reflection of the moon as it would appear in a mirror or a lake. Although the reflection is pristinely clear, it is still just a reflection, not a direct view of the moon that has somehow been submerged beneath the water or inserted into the mirror. Another example is a rainbow. Even though we can see the rainbow quite clearly, at the same time it is empty of intrinsic reality; similarly, even though a rainbow is empty, we can still see it. Both the reflection of the moon and the rainbow are simultaneously empty and visible.

So, “nonduality” here means the nonduality of appearance and emptiness; nothing we perceive, not the guru, the student nor anything else, truly exists externally. And until we fully realise nonduality, the exercise of dissolving or merging the deity or the guru with ourselves is an extremely useful tool. It is also a method that works well if you want to receive blessings, empowerments or even inspiration.

Often, though, practitioners have difficulties with this part of the practise because they tend to turn over in their minds all the theories about visualisaton and dissolution that they have learnt while they are supposed to be practising. This is a good example of how stuffing your mind with too many concepts can hinder your spiritual progress, and is why we are told to put theory aside altogether when we practise.

The best advice here is to keep it practical. Spiritual practise is a bit like riding a bicycle. Once you have learnt how to cycle there is no need to go over the theory behind how the gears work or the best height for your saddle every time you go for a ride. All you have to do is get on your bike and start pedalling. And the key to this practise is to do the best you can and don’t worry too much about whether what you are doing is right or wrong; eventually you will get the hang of it.

The pith instructions are extremely pragmatic: just do it! Which makes realising nonduality a little like learning to drive. However preposterous it may sound when you start out, having spent weeks learning about where all the different buttons and levers are in your car, there will come a time when you have no choice but to put the manual aside, turn on the engine, and drive. The same goes for visualisation practise. At first the dissolution may be more like dropping an apple into a bag than merging one with the guru, but unless you take a risk and have a go, nothing will change. With practise, though, your guru will become less like an apple and more like a glass of water that you then pour into a bucket of water—which is an indication you are beginning to understand the process of nonduality a little better.

Eventually you will come to realise that the dissolution happens in the same way an enclosed space mixes with the sky—and this is the part of the practise that many students misunderstand. Imagine a clay pot. It is both surrounded by and filled with space. When the pot breaks, the space that had been inside mixes with the space that had been outside and the two become inseparable. It is not possible to tell the “inside” space from the “outside” space; space is just space and there is no way of knowing where any part of it originated. This is how the practitioner and the guru dissolve into each other to become inseparable.

Right now, as you cannot help seeing the guru or the Buddha as an independent entity separate from yourself, try to remember that what you see is exclusive to you and everything any of us sees, hears, and thinks is based on our own personal interpretation. This is the principle that not only forms the basis of all Buddhist philosophical theory, but is the reason visualisation practise works. Louise may think of herself as “Louise,” but she would never describe herself as a “visualisation of Louise,” even though that is precisely what she is. In fact, every one of us is a visualisation of ourselves.

Questions often come up about whether or not visualisation is a culturally inspired method or somehow theistic. But as we have already seen, to visualise Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara as they appear in a Tibetan thangka is a mistake. Even if it were possible for everyone to use exactly the same thangka, each individual’s perception of it would be different and not come close to what the artist had in mind. So, as we visualise Guru Rinpoche, or any other deity, we might as well be bold about it. Guru Rinpoche is a sublime and superior being, and one aspect of “sublime” is usually “beautiful,” or at least very good-looking. But “good-looking” to one person is “ugly” to another because, again, our interpretations are so very different. And surely there is no need for Americans and Mexicans and Bulgarians to have to learn the Tibetan version of “good-looking.” All we can do is make the best use of our own interpretation. And don’t forget that even as you read these words, the mind interpreting this text is *your* mind, and its interpretation is based on *your* habits and perceptions. You may think you have understood what I mean by “good-looking,” but you haven’t; all that has happened is that you have developed your own version of what you think I mean by “good-looking.”

Another important point is that we do not visualise deities holding a vajra or kapala for aesthetic reasons or because ritual objects are especially useful. Some students wonder whether they should visualise deities holding something more modern, perhaps an iPad or an iPhone. But the attributes, ornaments and implements associated with each deity hold important symbolic significance and should therefore remain intact, just as they have been described in the sacred texts.

We know very little about the physical appearance of many of the great masters. What Guru Rinpoche actually looked like is anybody’s guess. If you were to search through every single Tibetan Buddhist text, you would not find a single definitive description of his features. There is a famous photograph of a statue of Guru Rinpoche called the “Looks Like Me” image, but several other equally famous statues of him look quite different.

We should also remember what Buddha said in the *Diamond Sutra* (also known as the *Diamond Cutter Sutra*):

> Those who see my body as ordinary form,

> And hear my voice as ordinary sound,

> Have set out on a mistaken path.

> Such people do not truly see me.1

There is one point that the general teachings on ngöndro practise tend not to emphasize and that is usually only mentioned in the context of sadhana practise: as you create a visualisation in your mind, the deity you picture should be clear, vibrantly alive and sealed with an appreciation of nonduality. To give you some idea of what this means, take the example of visualizing Guru Rinpoche as small as a sesame seed, sitting in a palace as large as Mount Meru, or in some cases the whole universe. It sounds awkward and ugly, but in practise it works perfectly because the container is neither too big nor the contents too small, and the difference in size between the sesame seed and Guru Rinpoche presents no problems at all. Other visualisations involve imagining the palace to be as small as a sesame seed and Guru Rinpoche the size of the whole universe, but he still fits into his tiny palace quite comfortably. This is an exercise in nonduality and is used in visualisation a great deal.

Vajrayana practitioners, as Gendün Chöpel pointed out, must get used to believing in the unbelievable. Tantric methods of visualisation often involve creating a raging inferno in your mind’s eye, in the midst of which sits a deity on a fragile lotus flower and a cool moon seat, embracing a very passionate consort and surrounded by an unruly mob of angry deities wielding deadly implements. Yet the heat and the flames do no harm whatsoever and no one gets hurt. A rational analysis of such a situation can only result in disbelief since everything about this scene is contradictory and nothing in it could possibly exist in our ordinary reality. But the point is that tantric practitioners have to get used to believing in the unbelievable. Our aim is to unite and dissolve subject and object so they are one: the desire with the anger, the heat with the cold, the clean with the dirty, the body with the mind. This is known as “the union of jnanas and kayas,” and is the ultimate kind of union.

Gendün Chöpel also said the reason we cannot grab hold of inexpressible notions like “dharmadhatu” is not because we strongly believe in what exists. On the contrary, it is because we strongly *disbelieve* in what does *not* exist. But it will take quite some time to insert this new knowledge of nonduality into our very stubborn system of duality.

#### Field of Merit

Visualisation usually involves first creating a field of merit, the details of which will depend on the ngöndro tradition you are following.

As a beginner, try not to get too paranoid about each and every detail of the visualisation—unless, of course, details inspire you. At all times, remember that whatever you visualise is itself an illusion, a figment of your imagination based on your own mind’s interpretation of various bits of information. The bottom line here is that illusions do not truly exist.

What is a “field of merit”? Imagine you want to get rich and need some form of capital to invest: a farmer will need a field in which to plant seeds or graze animals, and a businessman will need a business loan or investors to finance a new venture. Likewise, those who follow a spiritual path, because they long to liberate themselves and all other suffering beings from this net of samsara, will need to accumulate merit. To do so, two fields of merit are used, one of sublime beings and the other of sentient beings, through which we are able, ultimately, to harvest the fruit of enlightenment.

Both fields of merit are therefore employed throughout ngöndro practise. We visualise the sublime field of merit of the buddhas and bodhisattvas and consider that they support us by providing all the power, compassion and omniscience we need to bring all sentient beings to enlightenment. We visualise sentient beings in the ordinary field of merit and feel compassion for every one of them. In this way, we accumulate merit through both fields. Practitioners should therefore bear in mind that as we accumulate merit through relative practise, we will always either be praying to the buddhas or offering compassion to sentient beings, and in one form or another, these two fields of merit will be part of every one of our practises.

### Common Questions

Questions about exactly how you visualise the guru in front of you come up all the time. “How high in the sky above my head should he be?” “Should he be facing me?” “Should he face in the same direction as me?” But again, the best advice is, don’t be too fussy. The path of vajrayana is nondual. In fact, the whole point of the practise is to actualize that nonduality, and so in the process, we must learn to be as open and relaxed as possible about all the details.

Having said that, there are certain elements about which we can be specific. For example, in the context of ngöndro, as you visualise Vajrasattva sitting on top of your head, imagine that he is facing in the same direction as you. And when you practise a guru yoga sadhana, since deluded beings tend to imagine that a person who sits or stands higher than everyone else is better or more powerful, visualise your guru and his retinue a little higher than you, and that they are all looking at you.

For other practises you might find it more effective to imagine your guru on top of your head, or sitting inside your heart. The teachings even mention a visualisation of the guru in your throat, so that as you eat or drink, you are automatically making offerings to him. The choice of possible visualisations is limitless.

## 6. Why Do We Need a Guru?

Aspiring dharma practitioners today are very fortunate. They have at their fingertips a huge amount of information about the dharma in the form of books, recordings, websites and videos, all of which can be very useful. But for those who are serious about following a spiritual path, information is just a small part of the story. To follow the teachings of the Buddha fully and completely, you must first be accepted as a student by a spiritual master, a guru. He will then give you very specific advice about what you need to do on your path to enlightenment and when you need to do it, and by doing so will save you a great deal of time and effort.

Setting out on a spiritual path is a little like planning a trip—to Machu Picchu, for example. Some travelers will approach the project by investing a lot of time in reading travel books or Googling Internet sites about the best route to take and where to stay—a method that works, but only to a certain extent. Other travelers prefer a much simpler and safer method: to ask someone they known and trust who has already been to Machu Picchu to go with them and show them the way. Similarly, those wishing to follow the Buddhist path to enlightenment should rely on what are called in the teachings the “four authentics”: the authentic words of the Buddha (his teachings); the authentic clarification of the teachings that can be found in the shastras (commentaries) written by great masters of the past; the further clarification that is the result of authentic personal experience; and for this experience to find expression, an authentic guru.

In practise, this means that an authentic Buddhist master will share his authentic personal experience of the teachings and practise that has been clarified by relevant descriptions in authentic shastras and accurately represent the authentic words of the Buddha. And as an authentic guru is the pivotal element in this process, it is essential that students have absolute confidence in and devotion for their guru.

### Examine the Guru

How, then, can you tell whether or not the guru you meet is authentic? The most practical and easiest method available is to apply logic and common sense. Is the guru selfish, egotistical or too interested in samsaric life? If he is, then perhaps he is not the guru for you. When you find a guru who has no interest in samsaric life and is also a very good dharma practitioner, you should still ask yourself, “Is she compassionate enough to be genuinely interested in *my* enlightenment?”

A guru could inspire you in any number of ways, for example, the way he smells, or smiles, or dresses, or the fact that he supports the same football team as you. Whatever it is that initially draws you to a guru, however superficial, it has the potential to create a connection between you. Then, having made that connection, it is important that you examine him thoroughly. This advice comes from the Buddha himself, who, long before the modern world made it fashionable to celebrate freedom of speech, demonstrated just how much he respected the power of logic and reasoning by emphasizing how important it is not to follow a teacher blindly. He vigorously recommended we make an exhaustive examination of the guru, and in addition, these days we must be fully aware of all the cultural baggage that accompanies spiritual teachings.

Globalization is making our world seem smaller, and one of the symptoms of the democratic systems many First World countries live under is that those in power must necessarily operate transparently. It is a sound principle, but the enforcement of transparency isn’t necessarily beneficial. Over the past few years a few Tibetan lamas have faced a great deal of criticism that, by and large, originated in European and American newspapers. Quite recently a journalist wrote about how materialistic Tibetan lamas are, and when I first read the article, I felt a little defensive. Then I read it again, and I have to say she made some good points. She described how Tibetan lamas scold their rich, modern students for being too acquisitive, then pointed out how much money those very same lamas hoover up from their First World sponsors to take back to Nepal or India, where they buy expensive new cars and gold watches. And she is right, it happens all the time. One could argue that, from a certain rather narrow perspective, were all the high lamas to sell their silver and gold teacup holders, the proceeds would likely feed hundreds of starving children for months.

Before we judge these Rolex-wearing lamas too harshly, though, we must not forget that even monks who appear simple and humble may in reality be hiding behind a mask of hypocrisy. Some lamas become paralyzed with fear at the mere suggestion that their carefully crafted image of humility and simplicity could be exposed as fraud. For them, being seen to be humble and simple is so important that if they were ever accused of hiding a different “face,” it would cause them tremendous suffering. From this point of view, I admire those lamas who just do what they want without bothering to cultivate a humble image, and if they want to wear four gold Rolex watches, they should go ahead and wear them! However, we must be careful about the kind of logic and reasoning we apply as we examine our gurus.

### Teachers Manifest to Inspire Students

A good teacher knows how to teach effectively, but a great teacher inspires students at every level, from beginners to the more experienced practitioners, whose sources of inspiration change as they become more spiritually sophisticated. Our world is a judgmental one and we all know that when the U.S. president or a multimillionaire sportsman is caught doing something remotely fishy, a big deal will be made of it by their rivals and particularly by the media. This same kind of judgmental mind is often directed at a guru as students attempt to assess his value based on their own versions of how a morally upright and dependable person should behave. The problem is, though, that the more spiritually mature a student becomes the higher the truths they seek, and over time a guru must necessarily be able to provide them with quite different kinds of inspiration. Beginners are often inspired by a guru’s humility and simplicity. Later on, though, when the time comes to start smashing concepts apart, if a guru is stuck with the appearance and character of “moral” renunciant, he will be unable to be challenging enough to break down the barriers inhibiting a student’s progress.

A willingness to be inspired and a tendency to be turned off are both relative reactions: some people are inspired by the sight of a serene monk with a begging bowl; others by a half-drunk, half-naked yogi. Certain vajrayana students derive far more inspiration from the sight of a bizarrely dressed guru sporting a good deal of gold jewelery and breaking all the rules of social etiquette than a perfect monk, which, again, shows that the sources of our inspiration are both relative and subjective. Not only does each one of us find inspiration in different places, but what inspires a fifteen-year-old will no longer have the same effect once that person hits forty.

So, however close our scrutiny of each guru, however many ways we try to evaluate his worth, and however logical and reasonable our arguments may be, it’s important to bear in mind that all methods are limited and to trust our own logic and analysis 100 percent would be extremely foolish.

### The Guru

One of the meanings of the Sanskrit word “guru” is “teacher” or “master” and represents a person who teaches and transmits knowledge, like a master carpenter teaches an apprentice. Imagine that although you have no talent whatsoever and are unemployed, you also have a large family to feed. One day a carpenter offers to teach you how to work with wood. After a couple of months working with him, your neighbours begin to pay you to do carpentry for them. Imagine how grateful you would be to your teacher for giving you the means of making a living, and how much you would appreciate his help. On the spiritual path we can only attain complete spiritual enlightenment—a rather larger project than simply feeding empty bellies—with the help of a guru, and when we find one who is willing to accept and teach us, our appreciation should know no bounds.

Vajrayana texts state that for one who seeks enlightenment a guru is more important than all the buddhas of the three times put together. His job is not only to teach students but to lead them. He is our most important companion, our family, husband, wife and beloved child, because only he can bring us to enlightenment.

Sadly, in recent years, the word *guru* has all but lost its original meaning. The deluded beings of this time are greedy for everything pure and stainless, so they grab at the principle of the guru, spoil it, reject it and then move on to another perfect treasure to lay waste. It has happened far too often and as a result gurus are now mistrusted in the modern world and often ridiculed in popular culture. Nevertheless, for someone serious about following a spiritual path there is no substitute for being guided by a guru.

We follow a spiritual path because we want to defeat our emotions and attain enlightenment, and to achieve that goal we need discipline, guidance and the courage to confront everything we have spent many lifetimes trying to avoid. This is precisely what a guru provides us with by challenging our preconceived concepts, disrupting our lives and most important of all by denying ego’s every wish. Therefore, as Jigme Lingpa strongly advised, we should do a great deal of research about a guru before we give him or her carte blanche to torpedo our lives, because we must be able to trust him completely. Unfortunately, very few people these days focus on such details, and this stage of the process is too often overlooked.

### What Makes a Student Look for a Guru?

What is it that makes a student seek out a teacher? Ideally, the wish for personal enlightenment and the enlightenment of all sentient beings—but we rarely start out with such a wish. People usually start getting interested in a spiritual perspective when faced with tragedy, physical pain or a hopeless prognosis, because it is only when we recognize that life is stuffed full of the endless suffering we fear and dread that we start to look around for ways of avoiding it. Against such a backdrop, when we first stumble upon a guru, we may just see him as a person we can bring our complaints to, whine at, talk things over with or pray to—a kind of stand-in for our psychiatrist, psychologist or even God. It may take a while, but eventually we realise that the only way out of this painful existence is enlightenment and that we can only attain enlightenment by following a spiritual path and finding a spiritual master.

A hunger for enlightenment helps us develop genuine devotion for the spiritual path, but such a longing is difficult both to evoke and to maintain. Devotion might pop into our overcrowded minds once in a blue moon, but it is quickly polluted by the many jostling emotions that are its neighbours. Yet, although we only experience snippets of authentic devotion, their value is incalculable because even the tiniest glimpse can lead us to the right path.

Theoretically, we look for a guru because we long for enlightenment, but in practise students usually become attracted to a guru for quite different reasons. He may, for example, be physically gorgeous, or have an irresistibly pert bum, or a beautiful voice, and for some of us, these may be the only criteria we are capable of responding to. Once hooked, though, we should try to convert these ordinary attractions into devotion and follow our guru out of a longing for enlightenment. If we can adjust our motivation in this way, our relationship with our guru will never falter. However, if our motivation is tainted by the wish for material gain, or some other worldly desire, it will definitely go wrong. Faulty motivation leads to false expectations, which no guru can ever meet. And even though we think of ourselves as being students of a spiritual path, that faulty motivation means we will continue to experience all the mundane disappointments we have always felt when our samsaric plans are shattered.

Gradually, as the relationship matures, we begin to perceive our guru not just as our master, but as the key to our spiritual path. He will no longer merely be the one who provides us with book lists and answers our interminable questions, but nothing less than the manifest embodiment of our buddha nature. As buddha nature is so abstract as to be virtually impossible to tap into on our own, we ask our guru, who is the reflection of our buddha nature, to do it for us. This is just one way of “taking the guru as the path.”

It is said in the vajrayana that the guru *is* the Buddha, the guru *is* the dharma and the guru *is* the sangha. *Guru*, here, does not only refer to the outer guru, but to the inner and secret guru as well. So, on the most profound level, the guru is not just our teacher but our entire spiritual path.

### The Guru Is the Dharma

In the sutras, the same advice about how to follow a guru (often known as the four reliances) appears again and again: do not depend on the person, but on the dharma he teaches; do not focus on the superficial meaning of his words, but on their full and complete meaning; do not depend on teachings that require interpretation, but on absolute teachings; and do not depend on the mind, but on wisdom.

In our everyday lives we are rarely successful at judging others by their appearance and behaviour alone; and on the spiritual path it is never a good idea to become too dependent on the personality and character of your guru. Having analyzed him thoroughly and decided to take him as your path, the important thing is no longer to categorize him as a “person,” because from this point on the guru *is* the dharma.

### Don’t Second-Guess Yourself

Although you may have found a guru, convinced yourself of his authenticity, and been accepted by him, after a while you may start to wonder if you have done the right thing. Perhaps your analysis of your guru was flawed? And did you really examine everything in his charater and lifestyle that you should have? If you have plenty of time on your hands and are eager to satisfy every one of your discursive thoughts, you can continue to analyze your guru for as long as you like. But if you are impatient to make it to the other shore (enlightenment) and recognize that time is short, the quickest method is simply to choose a guru and get on with it. Having made that crucial decision, your guru will become your path and you must develop unwavering devotion for him, because where a fully qualified, authentic vajra master is concerned, devotion is an extremely powerful method that is guaranteed to bring you immeasurable blessings. But for all this to fall into place, you must first solve the problem of whether or not the master you are thinking of taking as your guru is qualified, and in particular what being “qualified” really means.

Jigme Lingpa said that it is extremely important to examine both the guru *and* your own motivation for choosing him. But do bear in mind that by the time you are able to tell the difference between a qualified and an unqualified master, you will already have progressed quite far along the path, and until then this is an aspect of spiritual practise that can be confusing.

### The Qualities to Look For in an Authentic Guru

These days qualified masters are extremely rare and, according to Jigme Lingpa, were already few and far between three hundred years ago—his lamentations over the paucity of qualified masters continue to be studied by students to this day. So, what are the qualities we should we look for in a guru?

#### 1. An Authentic Guru Is Also a Dharma Practitioner

Many so-called gurus these days are like deaf musicians who neither practise nor have enough dharma knowledge to be able to teach properly. And if they do, they never put what they teach into practise.

Longchenpa said that those living in these degenerate times are spoilt. Just as a baby bird has to wait for its wings to strengthen and its feathers to grow before it can attempt to fly, practitioners are unable genuinely to benefit sentient beings until they have first nurtured and matured themselves spiritually. And without some degree of omniscience, it is almost impossible truly to help others. Longchenpa therefore advises us to pay attention to nurturing ourselves at the outset of our spiritual path and to remind ourselves at all times of our wish to benefit others.

To accept advice from someone who has not practised, said Kongtrul Rinpoche, is like consulting a book on healing when you are ill: it may be an intellectually satisfying read, but when you really need help—practical help—a book is utterly useless.

#### 2. An Authentic Guru Does Not Adjust the Teachings to Turn a Profit

Propagating the dharma is important, but it should never be taught for material gain. If it is, like so many of the world’s ancient wisdom traditions, it will lose its power and degenerate. Take, for example, the fashion for practicing Indian yoga as physical exercise that has been popular for decades now. How many contemporary yoga teachers even mention the view and motivation behind authentic yoga practises? Or include traditional elements like making prostrations to the guru or chanting prayers in their presentations? There are a few, but not many. More often than not, everything spiritual about yoga practise is pushed aside so that modern yoga sessions fit more comfortably into the schedules of health-conscious corporate employees, making them more profitable for the teachers. And once an activity like yoga has become just another cash cow, it quickly loses its spiritual character.

Have you ever enjoyed a cup of traditionally made English tea, poured from a porcelain teapot and served in matching cups and saucers? It is so elegant! The tea itself takes on a completely different quality when the atmosphere of “English tea” is authentically created. Yet, proper English tea is another relatively rare experience these days. Most people drink tea served in lidded polystyrene cups bought from fast-food stores and gulp it down on their way to work. It may be a quick and efficient method of ingestion, but so much is lost in the process. Of course, most commuters are quite happy with their quick cuppa, but for a real tea fanatic nothing beats the traditional ritual. Similarly, gurus should not adapt the dharma to the needs of busy, materialistic people who demand a businesslike method for receiving teachings that fits into their lifestyles. Were that to happen, far too much that is crucial to an authentic spiritual path would be lost.

#### 3. An Authentic Guru Belongs to an Authentic Lineage

The great Sakyapa master Drakpa Gyaltsen said that a guru without a lineage is not able to transfer blessings.

The fundamental view of Buddhadharma is that nothing happens randomly and everything arises out of causes and conditions, therefore we have no choice but to depend on the right causes and conditions to achieve liberation. The cause and condition for meeting and being accepted by a spiritual master is absolutely crucial because only by being close to a guru can our virtuous and spiritual qualities ignite and increase.

The practise of guru yoga has nothing at all to do with the worship of external beings, nor is it a rather humdrum method for acknowledging that the guru is our teacher. Guru yoga is the only way of building a bridge between ourselves and the nature of our mind, so not only is it important to find an authentic guru, but he must also hold an authentic lineage. As limited beings we can only measure the efficacy of a path and one who dwells on that path (in this case the guru) by consulting the historical records of our guru’s spiritual experiences, as well as those of his gurus and of all the other gurus belonging to his lineage. To put our trust in someone entirely independent of all lineages who claims to have had a revelation about a new path to enlightenment is very risky. If you do decide to follow a self-appointed guru, bear in mind that you’ll be on your own, because there is no way of checking the veracity of his claims. He may well be a charlatan. So, to be assured that your guru belongs to a lineage goes a long way towards authenticating his wisdom and realization, in the same way that the universities where a scholar studied and from whom she received her qualifications go a long way towards authenticating her academic standing.

At the very least, a qualified guru must be compassionate and always work for your benefit, not his own.

### Merit and the Guru

Here again, merit plays an important role, because finding the right guru requires a great deal of it. Without merit it is not possible even to conceive the wish to meet a spiritual master, let alone develop the ability to discern soberly and with the right motivation whether or not a guru is both authentic and the right one for you. Finding a guru is one thing, having enough merit not to doubt your choice and never cease to trust him completely is quite another and requires oceans of merit. In fact, merit is required at every stage of your relationship with a guru.

This lifetime, the merit accumulated through your karmic link with your guru has been lying dormant, as if wait-listed for a seat on the flight to enlightenment. Once you meet a guru, that merit begins to ripen, and then absolutely anything could happen. How your spiritual path eventually unfolds, though, will be decided by you and your guru. He might, for example, tell you to quit your current way of life and do something completely different. If he does, then that is the path you must pursue, because you must do whatever it takes, ultimately, to attain enlightenment.

### Spiritual Progress Is Measured in Devotion

Throughout every stage of dharma practise, understanding, experience and realization will grow in your mind. So, however much confidence you have, or however little, the conclusions you draw as a result of your realizations will be entirely your own. For this reason, the only true indicator of your spiritual progress is whether or not your devotion for your guru and your compassion for sentient beings increases. All the visions, dreams or medals you win from dharma practitioners’ guilds are all totally meaningless and signify nothing.

# Part Two. The Ngöndro

## Part Two

## 7. Taking Refuge

### Why Take Refuge?

There are three situations, or “conditions,” that lure us into taking refuge: fear, trust or devotion and compassion. And of these three, fear is the most powerful.

#### Fear

We tend to feel fear in relation to worldly activities, particularly those to do with gain and loss. For example, when it rains, because we are afraid of getting wet we take refuge under an umbrella. The same kind of fear prompts farmers and businessmen in Buddhist countries, who have no interest in philosophy, to say prayers at Buddhist shrines. Neither interdependent reality nor shunyata mean anything to these people, but as theirs is a Buddhist country, Buddha, dharma and sangha have become their “god.” Therefore they pray, parrot-fashion, to the triple gem for long life, good business and bumper harvests, and in return make offerings and circumambulate holy relics.

Although such an attitude is not the ultimate goal of taking refuge, it is not one we should deride, because for those of simple faith it can be the stepping-stone to a more sophisticated understanding. Neither is it an attitude exclusive to those born into traditional Buddhist societies. Most people who take Buddhadharma as their path tend to think of the Buddha as their saviour, the way Christians think of Jesus, and often take refuge in him because they are afraid and feel the need for protection. This is the first kind of fear that induces practitioners to take refuge.

The second is a fear of the sufferings of samsara, like being reborn in hell. Those suffering from this kind of fear care little for failed harvests or rises in the interest rate; their great fear is of getting caught up in and duped by samsara. Having developed the “wisdom of knowing,” they recognize the dangers of becoming trapped in samsara, and their compassion for all sentient beings leads them to fear not only samsara but also the selfishness of longing for their own liberation (nirvana).

The third kind of fear suffered by vajrayana practitioners (in addition to a fear of samsara and nirvana) is of “impure perception,” because while our perception is not pure, the root of extreme duality can never be dismantled.

#### Trust

Whatever we do in life requires a certain amount of trust. For example, when you want a cup of tea, you have no choice but to trust that the ingredients, the tea-making utensils and the method and skills of the tea maker will all come together to make a delicious cup of tea. To do it successfully, not one element can deceive you. So, if a beautifully designed tea bag, complete with elegant string and label, does not contain tea leaves, it will not be able to infuse the hot water with tea. Although it looks like a tea bag, as it cannot fulfill its task, it is “deceptive.”

The decision to follow a spiritual path is the central most important undertaking of a person’s life, one that is usually made when trust in more worldly objectives is betrayed, causing you to resolve to switch to a more trustworthy path. If listening to and contemplating the teachings and practicing meditation inspires you, the likelihood is that you will choose to rely on the logic of the dharma. And having gradually developed an unshakable trust and belief in Buddha, dharma and sangha, you will start to long to follow what you now believe to be the only truly nondeceptive path in this world. Based on that trust, you take refuge.

Of course, there are other reasons for taking refuge in the triple gem, for example the development of a steadfast conviction in the enlightened qualities of liberation, and once all these reasons begin to make sense to you, the idea of liberation will become more and more attractive. This is just another way of saying that devotion and trust in the Buddha, dharma and sangha will have been established in your mind.

Essentially, once you believe that the triple gem will not deceive you and that it represents the highest truth, you surrender to that truth by taking refuge.

#### Compassion

For mahayana students, a more significant incentive for taking refuge is the compassion we feel when we realise that every single sentient being bound by samsaric suffering has, at one time or another, been someone we loved deeply and who may even have sacrificed their very lives to satisfy our selfish emotional demands. But even once we have had this realisation, we are still powerless to help because we too are bound by and entirely dependent on our own debased conditions and circumstances.

How, then, can one helpless refugee give refuge to another? As Shantideva said, to be protected by someone more powerful than yourself makes it possible for you to give protection to others. Therefore, once you have taken refuge in the Buddha, dharma and sangha, you will also be able to give refuge, support and protection to others.

### Taking Refuge: The Theory

Vajrayana texts often present Buddha, dharma and sangha using different terms, for example “guru, deva and dakini,” or in the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro “prana, nadi and bindu,” or even “dharmakaya, sambhogakaya and nirmanakaya.” The words we use to specify who or what we take refuge in matter very little. Far more important is that we understand the meaning behind the words we recite, which is that we accept we have buddha nature and can therefore attain enlightenment.

Imagine washing a badly stained coffee cup. When you plunge the cup into hot, soapy water, what are you doing? Most of us would say, “I am washing this cup.” But are you? Are you washing the cup, or removing the stains from the cup? The instruction your mother gave you when she taught you how to wash up—for our purposes the “pith instruction”—was “Go and wash your cup.” But although she said it virtually every day, she never explained that the coffee stain and the cup are separate and different, and that “washing up a cup” really means removing the stains from a cup that never is, never has been, nor ever will be anything other than clean. In practise, though, her pith instruction, based on years of washing-up experience, is simple, anyone can understand it and it gets the job done. Yet the cup and the dirt are two separate entities. You do not wash the cup, you wash the dirt; if you were to wash the cup, it would disappear completely. So, it is the dirt that is washable, and it has nothing at all to do with the cup.

This example is quite a good illustration of one of the most profound theories of the bodhisattvayana: we all have the potential to become buddhas because we all have buddha nature. The problem is, we have yet to realise it. What is it, then, that prevents us from realizing our buddha nature? Or to put it another way, what is it that prevents us from understanding that the cup is, always has been and always will be clean? Aeons and aeons of accumulated obscurations.

If you lack the information that buddha nature is inherent in all of us, to follow the pith instructions alone could cause you to make a very common error. Throughout the centuries, a great many vajrayana practitioners have made the mistake of imagining that Buddha is a person who sits in the sky in front of you and from whom you beg favors. It’s a theistic approach that has much more to do with more conventional religions like Christianity than it does with Buddhism. So, when students ask, as they often do, “How long should I practise taking refuge?” the answer itself, “Until the refugee and object of refuge become inseparable,” is a reminder that the ultimate object of refuge is not an external one, but in the nature of their minds.

In Indrabhuti’s *Yeshe Drubpa,*1 Buddha, dharma and sangha are said ultimately to exist within our minds, and all thoughts, even the most fleeting, are their manifestation. So, at this level, even the least of our thoughts is unstained by defilements and primordially pure, and consequently contains all the qualities of the Enlightened One. As Jigme Lingpa said, complete trust in the triple gem is the final stage of the relative practise of taking refuge; and to see your own mind as the triple gem is the final stage of the ultimate practise of taking refuge.

### Taking Refuge: The Pith Instructions

The pith instructions for taking refuge vary. One method is to take refuge in the Buddha as the teacher, the dharma as the path and the sangha as your companions on the path. Another, taught by the vajrayana, is to have absolute confidence in the guru as the Buddha, the guru as the dharma and the guru as the sangha; and even more explicitly, mind is the Buddha, mind is the dharma and mind is the sangha.

Why do we visualise our guru in the form of a buddha or some other extraordinary form? In fact, why do we visualise the guru at all? Why not simply visualise the form of Buddha Shakyamuni and believe him to be Shakyamuni? One very good reason is that we human beings find it hard to imagine that buddha nature can exist in creatures as ordinary as ourselves. Secondly, we find it equally hard to believe that our human guru, who yawns and drinks tea, and so on, is a real buddha. How can he be? There is still so much he does not know, he is totally spaced out most of the time and his behaviour is completely irrational. Yet sometimes we also experience an overpowering sense that something is truly extraordinary about him. As a result, we yo-yo between trusting him wholeheartedly and losing that trust to doubt and suspicion.

Jamyang Khyentse Chökyi Lodrö wrote in his explanation of guru yoga that according to both the bodhisattvayana and the vajrayana, buddha nature manifests continuously. However, if this manifestation has not yet been refined by merit, it usually finds expression as defilements, such as aggressive emotions. Once the refining process is under way, though, buddha nature will manifest as compassion, love, understanding and tolerance, and on occasion as one of its most refined configurations, devotion. It is only when we peer through the doors of devotion that we are able to see someone we believe is worth listening to and emulating, and we call that person “guru.”

It is said that although countless buddhas and bodhisattvas have appeared and continue to appear in this world, most of us lack the merit to meet them; and even if we were to meet a buddha, we lack the merit to recognize who he really is or understand what he says, or feel any devotion for him. Devadatta suffered from this problem. He was Buddha’s cousin and lived alongside him throughout his life, yet benefited not one jot from the experience because he was so jealous of him.

For us, our guru is our own personal buddha. Not only has he spoken to us, he has tirelessly and selflessly gone out of his way to upset us, disappoint us and fatally wound our fragile pride, all to fulfill the goal of eradicating our egos. So, from now until we attain enlightenment we take refuge, not only to destroy all our surface delusions but to dig out the root of delusion itself by asking our guru to beat it up and tear it apart until it has been entirely pulverized.

So, why not just visualise our guru as we see him in everyday life? We see him so often that we know exactly what he looks like. We may even have had dinner with him a few times or accompanied him on one of his walks. Why make things difficult for ourselves by trying to imagine him dressed up in the heavy brocades and funny hats that Tibetans love Guru Rinpoche to wear? After all, no one living today ever met Guru Rinpoche or knows how he really dressed; our only references are Tibetan pictures and statues.

Again, theoretically, the main purpose of the vajrayana teachings is to show us how to transform impure perception into pure perception. When we scrutinize our human guru, it is through the filter of our ordinary, impure perception, the very perception we must work so hard to transform. Although we have been told that everything around us, including the walls, ceiling and floor, look very different when perceived purely, few of us can immediately make such a huge leap in the way we see the world. But that inability is not our immediate problem. Crucially, what we need to rid ourselves of as quickly as possible are any impure perceptions we have of our guru. When he yawns, sleeps, shops excessively, or behaves oddly, instantly remind yourself that what you see is your own mind’s interpretation and this very interpretation is precisely what needs to be transformed.

The transformation of a rigid and dualistic mind requires a great deal more effort than holding in your mind’s eye the image of your guru as an ordinary person. Therefore, when your practise requires you to visualise your guru, imagine him in the sky before you in the form of Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara. To visualise him in his ordinary human form will only perpetuate your habit of perceiving him impurely, or at best, as a peer, making you unable to work with the idea that your guru is “peerless.”

### Taking Refuge: The Practise

When we take refuge, it must be in an “authentic object of refuge.” But what is it? An object of refuge must be omniscient, compassionate and omnipotent, and can only be authentically “nondeceptive” if he or she embodies all three qualities. If he is not omniscient, he will not be able to discern our difficulties; similarly, if he is omniscient but lacks compassion, or omniscient and compassionate but lacks the power to help, he will not be up to the job of “object of refuge.”

The omniscience of those in whom we take refuge is like the sun, all-pervasive and all-illuminating; their compassion is as quick as lightning and entirely impartial; and they possess omnipotent power to uproot the ultimate source of all our problems. We must remind ourselves of these three qualities again and again. We should also make full use of the intellectual “make-believe” we discussed earlier. But it’s not easy. How many people do you know who are omnipotent, omniscient or compassionate? Have you ever met such a person? Or is the idea simply too outlandish even to consider?

Of course, we can usually find a way of accepting such a notion on an intellectual level, but the problem then is that taking refuge is not an intellectual exercise. As we take refuge, we put the dharma into practise and therefore must almost force ourselves to believe that omniscient, compassionate and omnipotent people who qualify as genuine objects of refuge really do exist, and develop the habit of repeatedly reminding ourselves about them.

#### Visualise the Refuge Tree

Before you begin offering prostrations, you should first create the field of merit by either visualizing all the buddhas and bodhisattvas in front of you—the guru, devas, dakinis and the entire guru lineage—or if you prefer, focus exclusively on your guru in the form of Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara. (A further elaboration of this visualisation can be found in descriptions of the field of merit that are usually included in the “taking refuge” section of longer ngöndro texts.)

If you choose to visualise the whole field of merit, imagine the main deity of the mandala (Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara) sitting on the central branch of a wish-fulfilling tree. On the branch in front of him sits Shakyamuni Buddha and all the buddhas, on the branch to his right stand all the bodhisattvas, to his left the noble sangha of shravakas and pratyekabuddhas, and behind him are huge stacks of dharma books. Above your guru’s head sits his guru, and above your guru’s guru sits his guru, and so on up to Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara. But don’t get too carried away by trying to visualise each member of his retinue. The masters of the past have told us that when a king arrives, his retinue will follow automatically, so once you have visualised your guru as Guru Rinpoche, it is simply a matter of developing complete confidence that all the members of his retinue are also present.

At first you may find it difficult to see the field of merit. This happens a lot when you are not yet used to looking at it, perhaps in the same way Australian Aborigines were unable to comprehend the sight of huge European ships that arrived offshore more than two centuries ago and were therefore virtually blind to them.2 Or like the emperor’s subjects in the Hans Christian Andersen story, who didn’t want to admit to not being able to see the emperor’s nonexistent new clothes.

Again, there is no need to worry too much about the details. Concentrate instead on evoking complete confidence that Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara is present before you, vivid and vibrantly alive, surrounded by all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

I must stress here that *visualise* does not mean you should try to paint a picture of the refuge tree in your mind; that would be impossible. Visualisation is very like thinking about the person who is closest to you in this life—your mother, for example. Try imagining her standing in front of you right now. As you do so, I’m quite sure you are not thinking about the exact shape of her ear. Or whether or not her toes curl. Or how many moles she has on her back. In fact, that kind of detail has probably never even occurred to you. At the same time, I am equally sure that you have created a strong sense of your mother in your mind and that you are completely confident it really is her. This is how you should visualise the objects of refuge, and the confidence you feel in your visualisation is of utmost importance.

Picture the people who annoy and irritate you standing right at the front, closest to the objects of refuge, alongside those who have hurt you. Every one of us has been hurt by people we have cared for, and although we may insist we have forgotten all about it, we rarely have. To help ease any lingering pain, visualise them in the place of honor, and as you arouse bodhichitta, wish them everything that is good. If thinking about them continues to be painful, it is a sign that you have not let go of feeling they have wronged you. Try not to focus on it. Instead, admit to yourself that you are still holding on to your pain. Then concentrate on wishing them every happiness and long to take all their sufferings on yourself. And do bear in mind that for those who are really serious about practicing the dharma, difficult relationships provide the most fertile ground for practise.

Now begin your prostrations. As you practise, visualise that everyone, your friends, family and all the countless sentient beings that exist, prostrate and take refuge with you. By doing so, you take refuge with the attitude of one who follows the bodhisattvayana path.

### Accumulating Prostrations

#### Why Prostrate at All?

Why fling yourself full-length on an often filthy floor, then get up and do it again hundreds of thousands of times? Prostrations are a very immediate method for taking refuge and one of the best available for destroying pride. They are an outer gesture of surrender to the truth of dharma, and an expression of our intention to give up and expose our pride. So, as we take refuge, we prostrate to demonstrate our complete surrender by throwing ourselves at the feet of our guru and pressing the five points of our body—forehead, hands and knees—to the floor as many times as we can. (In the Tibetan tradition there are two ways of doing prostrations: one is the full-length and the other the half-length prostration, and we usually accumulate the full-length version.)

Prostrations are said to bring a number of benefits, such as being reborn with an attractive appearance, or our words carry weight and are valued, or our influence over friends and colleagues is positive, or that we are able to manage those who work for us. It is said that practitioners who accumulate prostrations will one day keep company with sublime beings and as a result become majestic, wealthy, attain a higher rebirth and eventually attain liberation.

For worldly beings, though, to contemplate all the spiritual benefits of prostrations and the amount of merit they accumulate is not necessarily the most effective way of motivating ourselves. The fact that prostrations are good for our health, on the other hand, is often just the incentive we need to get started. It’s true, doing prostrations for the sake of taking healthy exercise is a worldly motivation, but not one I would ever discourage. In these degenerate times, absolutely anything that will inspire you to practise dharma has some value, so please go ahead and start your prostrations for the sake of the exercise. If you do, not only will you save money on your gym membership, you will build up muscle and a great deal of merit.

#### Counting Prostrations: Just Do It!

The traditional goal is to complete one hundred thousand prostrations this lifetime, or more if you are physically up to it and have the time, and it’s a goal you should do your best to accomplish. How quickly you reach that goal, though, is relatively unimportant—to accumulate one hundred thousand prostrations more quickly than your sangha friends will not necessarily mean you are the first to attain enlightenment. More important than speed is your attitude and motivation. At the same time, bear in mind that one hundred and fifty prostrations take about half an hour, and by doing that amount daily, in less than two years you will accomplish one hundred thousand prostrations quite comfortably.

For many of us, having a goal to aim at really helps. Some people argue that numbers should not matter, but many practitioners find that keeping track of how much practise they do is both a constructive discipline and a welcome encouragement. Often, those who say they prefer not to count prostrations are, more truthfully, too lazy to bother—just buying a mala to count on is too much effort. And by giving in to their laziness they completely miss the point because making the effort to do prostrations is an essential part of the practise. There are also people who worry that by keeping a record they will become too proud of their own achievements. They argue that dharma practise was designed to crush ego and pride, so why risk bolstering it? There are even those for whom the whole idea of “accumulation” implies selfishness, like hoarding money, and consider it beneath them.

In practise, if you have strong feelings about not wanting to count your prostrations and are absolutely convinced that to do so would not encourage you, then without doubt *you should count*. Unless you happen to be a hidden buddha, ignorant beings like us usually achieve more when motivated by the wish to accomplish a goal. Therefore, record your daily tally, even when you only manage ten. And remember that, as doing prostrations is more difficult than reciting verses, you are counting the amount of prostrations you do, not the amount of times you recite the verse.

Having said that, as you prostrate, you should recite the refuge formula continuously, on the way down and on the way up, but there is no need to sync the words with the prostration. When you need a rest, sit quietly in meditation posture and continue reciting the prayer. Try refreshing your motivation after every twenty-five prostrations, or ten if you prefer; it can really help. Regularly remind yourself that you are practising in order to benefit all sentient beings with the confidence and certainty that your guru is actually there watching you, and arouse bodhichitta.

Inevitably there will be times when you find yourself going through the motions of doing prostrations without “feeling” anything at all. When this happens, just keep prostrating. It is better to keep at it than to waste time doing nothing while you wait for the right feeling to come along. Personally, I hardly ever feel anything when I do prostrations.

### How to Break Your Resistance to Practise

#### Meditate Between Prostrations

Boredom can be a problem, and if you allow it to take root, you could build up a strong resistance to doing any prostrations at all. One way of breaking through your resistance is to intersperse your prostrations with sitting meditation. Other good methods include focusing on physical sensations, like the pain in your knees; or think about how each prostration is bulldozing lifetime after lifetime of bad karma; or that when you do a prostration properly, you accumulate infinite merit. By practicing in this way, once you have been introduced to the higher mahasandhi meditations and the nature of the mind, you will eventually experience a genuine taste of meditation.

For those who take to the mahasandhi teachings, once you get that first taste, everything will suddenly fall into place and you will find yourself irrevocably hooked. Of course, it takes time to get used to new habits, but if you are persistent and practise regularly, your world will start to open up in ways you never imagined possible. And from then on, meditation will be anything but boring.

#### Overcoming Laziness

Many students complain that they are simply too lazy to do any kind of practise, let alone prostrations. One of the methods I suggest to help them overcome their laziness is to read stories about the great masters of the past, like Milarepa. Be warned, though, your clever mind will lose no time in finding ways of escaping this source of inspiration. If you rely on it exclusively, you will quickly become jaded, and once that happens, even if Buddha Shakyamuni himself were to spend a whole month at your side, there is no guarantee you would become less lazy. Another very good method for overcoming laziness is to make aspiration prayers asking for protection from the menace of being lackadaisical and the loss of inspiration. Lighting incense and candles also works, as does making the effort to create an inspiring atmosphere each time you practise.

Finding the inspiration to practise isn’t always easy; even the most experienced practitioners lose inspiration when they forget that every day we move one step closer to death. And for those with families to care for, busy jobs and bills to pay, finding time to practise is that much harder. Ironically, though, when we do have the time, we often lack the inspiration and will to inspire ourselves, and can end up putting more energy into watching a football match than practicing dharma.

#### Take Every Opportunity to Practise

According to Patrul Rinpoche, we should follow the example of the hungry yak, who eats every blade of grass he sees without even thinking of saving a patch for later. Try to capitalize on every moment you have to practise and never forget to count. Always remember that unless the mind is tamed and mindfulness is intact, no matter what we do, the very methods designed to crush our ego and pride will become the tools that strengthen them.

You should also try to practise in daily life. Obviously, it is not possible to continue to prostrate at work, but you can discipline yourself to think of bodhichitta every time you walk through a door, or hear a phone ring. Do bear in mind, though, that whatever you choose to use as a reminder will quickly become old and degenerate into another meaningless ritual. So keep your practise fresh by constantly changing your reminder.

Practicing the dharma is like peeling away layers of skin. At first you have no choice: you are presented with an outer layer and your only option is to peel it off. Having done so, it is quite possible you will suddenly be overcome by confusion and doubt, so much so that you might start to doubt the method itself. But not all doubts are necessarily harmful. Some are put forward by your much-valued “discriminating mind,” and at times, critical analysis can help, especially when it exposes the flaws in ego’s interpretation of your experiences, which few of us ever question. In fact, we tend to absorb ego’s version of events far too easily, so if you begin to wonder whether the truths you have accepted for so long really are “true,” it may well be a sign that dharma is beginning to seep into your mind.

Although becoming more discriminating could be a good sign, try not to dwell on your criticisms. Keep moving forwards, because although you may have made a little progress on the path by successfully removing one layer, you will soon be confronted by another that must also be pared away. In fact, whenever you get stuck, the solution will always be to peel off another layer, and you will reach the end of the path only once all those layers of progress and stuckness have been completely removed.

Never forget that we practise the dharma because our goal is to attain complete enlightenment, not merely to become a kind or good human being—a philanthropist or a hospice worker—or for the sake of recognition or to win awards. Students of the bodhisattvayana long for enlightenment not only for themselves but for all sentient beings, and the bottom line is: *enlightenment* is the prize.

#### The End of the Session

Once you have accumulated your daily quota of prostrations, move straight on to the next practise. As you arouse bodhichitta, your objects of refuge will become the witnesses to your practise and right at the end of your session will dissolve into light and then into you. Or, if you prefer, imagine they move closer and closer to you, slowly dissolving to become one with you, and then remain in that inexpressible state of unity for as long as you can.

## 8. Arousing Bodhichitta

Many people in this world are kind. Jigme Lingpa tells us he met umpteen kind people during his life, but adds that very few had any idea about what the bodhichitta of aspiration and application really are.

As we have already seen, to take refuge is the *foundation* of the path to liberation. Next we arouse bodhichitta, which is the *quintessence* of ngöndro practise.

### What Is Bodhichitta?

In very general terms, bodhichitta is the wish to bring all sentient beings to complete enlightenment. It is the heart, head, eyes, blood and spine of the mahayana path and an absolutely indispensable practise. In fact, it is such a powerful idea that just to value the wish to transmit the wisdom that is enlightenment to others is one of the most immense offerings it is possible to make.

All too often, though, even so-called Buddhists misunderstand bodhichitta and reduce it to little more than compassionate loving-kindness. Love, compassion and wanting to make people happy are very popular ideals these days and are often promoted as bodhichitta’s most important features. We forget that without bodhichitta there would be no mahayana or vajrayana Buddhism, and that the most crucial aspect of both these yanas is the understanding of shunyata (emptiness). Without wisdom, compassion alone is not “bodhichitta,” and vice versa; both qualities are essential.

Most Buddhists are aware that bodhichitta has something to do with tolerance, thoughtfulness and charitable endeavour, but rarely notice the part—the very large part—that deals with the wisdom that understands nonduality (emptiness). However, without a comprehensive appreciation of bodhichitta, all Buddhist practise degenerates into spiritual materialism because it lacks the one essential component that brings the path so radiantly alive: bodhichitta.

According to Karma Chagme Rinpoche, the only way to transform unvirtuous, negative thoughts and actions into those that are beneficial, and beneficial thoughts and actions into the path of liberation, is through bodhichitta. To make it possible to generate bodhichitta, we must first create certain causes and conditions, namely take refuge, arouse compassion and accumulate merit. (For a very thorough and precise explanation of why bodhichitta is so crucial for a Buddhist practitioner, see *The Way of the Bodhisattva* by Shantideva.)

#### Bodhichitta: Love, Compassion and the Wisdom of Nonduality

Mahayana practitioners not only wish to liberate all sentient beings from suffering itself but also the *causes* of suffering, the root of which is the dualistic mind. Consequently, a bodhisattva does not limit herself to philanthropic or humanitarian activities. As Jigme Lingpa pointed out, there are many kind and deeply compassionate people in this world and some remarkably active humanitarians, but as bodhichitta is not merely a loving concern for living beings, these people are not necessarily bodhisattvas.

Those who work on behalf of others can sometimes do so for all the wrong reasons. They can also become obsessed with and inflexible about their goals. Then, if projects fail to play out as they had hoped, they persecute themselves with self-recrimination, guilt and anger—all of which are the side effects of obsession. Very good people lose heart, hope and determination, and aspiring bodhisattvas might even give up trying to pursue bodhisattva activities altogether. Therefore, however caring and considerate people may appear to be, there is always the danger that they are also inveterate egoists, and another reason bodhichitta is praised so highly is that it is an infallible antidote to egocentricity.

One of the most obvious signs that bodhichitta is misunderstood is when masters who appear gentle and humble, are always smiling and never display irritation or anger, are described as “great bodhisattvas,” whereas lamas who regularly appear to lose their tempers and may even hit their students are described as “arrogant.” Such distinctions are a clear indication we have forgotten that wisdom is an inseparable aspect of bodhichitta. And as we have no way of knowing whether or not any of these lamas have fully realised bodhichitta, to sit in judgment on whether they are bodhisattvas or not based solely on how we perceive their behaviour is downright foolish. What this example shows is how easy it is to fall into the trap of misunderstanding bodhichitta as kindness, gentleness and selfless concern for others. Once we grasp a fuller picture of bodhichitta, we begin to realise that it is not a person’s appearance and behaviour that demonstrate whether or not they are a bodhisattva but their understanding of shunyata, the essence of which is compassion.

**A Beginner’s Practise of Absolute Bodhichitta**

Begin your practise motivated by the wish to bring all sentient beings to enlightenment and remind yourself that everything you do is your own idea and a manifestation of your mind. By doing so, you will move a little closer to the idea of emptiness and nonduality, and at the same time will quickly discover that developing an intellectual understanding of shunyata is probably the best you can manage. Of course, it’s relatively easy to talk about “emptiness,” read about it and even make a stab at imagining it, but the conclusions we draw will always fall within the confines of our own extremely limited rationale. Our intellectual version of nonduality may well be logical, but on a practical level, nonduality is extraordinarily difficult to grasp.

As Longchenpa said, how can the flavour of salt be explained to someone who has never tasted it? All we can do is give them something sweet and say, “Salt does not taste like this.” We face the same problem when we talk about absolute bodhichitta. A major obstacle to understanding it fully is our lack of language capable of deciphering and explaining it. Yet in practise, absolute bodhichitta is far from being unattainable, quite the opposite in fact. Right now, though, we are dwelling in the territory of dualism. Our goal may be to pass through dualism to nondualism, but we can only attempt to understand nonduality by employing dualistic methods. And although we have no choice but to use such methods, we continually aspire for a nondualistic result.

**Develop the Strength of Compassion**

Until we have established a complete understanding of bodhichitta, we will continue to make the mistake of imagining that pain truly exists. As a result, we will almost certainly become the victims of our own compassion, and without fully appreciating nonduality, our every activity will lead to disappointment. For example, as a therapist, how far are you prepared to go to help an alcoholic or drug addict? Imagine that one of your clients is certain to be reborn a drug addict for the next five thousand lifetimes. As a bodhisattva, your determination to help her should be so great that you aspire to be reborn at the right time and in the right place during every one of those lifetimes so you can continue her treatment.

Fast forward to the five-thousandth lifetime. Your client has been reborn in a lonely, godforsaken country, and all you have to do to ensure she will never again be reborn as an addict is hang out with her for half a day. This means you must also be reborn in that wretched place and that the entire focus of your life will be to get you to the right place at the right time to spend those few hours with your client. To do this, you will need what the teachings describe as “strength” or “determination,” and you will only find this strength once you come to realise the illusory nature of all phenomena, or in other words “emptiness.”

So, how do you develop the remarkable strength of compassion you need to be able to behave so selflessly? How do you develop the determination never to give up? Where do you find the confidence and tenacity to keep trying? Every one of these qualities spring from the realisation that the drug addict, her problem, that she can be healed, and even the idea that she needs healing are all merely the products of your own mind. None of it truly exists outside mind. And having realised this truth, you can go on to develop the strong and genuine compassion that is bodhichitta.

In the *Prajnaparamita Sutra* we are told that Buddha was once approached by a bodhisattva practitioner who complained that, having dedicated many years to helping sentient beings, he was now utterly exhausted. Buddha responded by explaining that time is relative. The bodhisattvayana teachings tell us the period between the moment a bodhisattva first takes the bodhisattva vow up until his last moment on the tenth bhumi lasts for three countless aeons. Yet, one of the realisations of a tenth-bhumi bodhisattva is that all those countless aeons pass as quickly as it takes a spark to shoot out of the fire.

On another occasion, a bodhisattva told the Buddha how discouraged he felt because helping sentient beings was so painful and there were still so many left to liberate. The Buddha responded with an analogy. Imagine a mother dreams her only child has been swept away by the powerful currents of a fast-moving river. She is entirely powerless, yet in her agony and desperation she is willing do anything to save her son, regardless of her own welfare and safety, even sacrifice her very life. How long the rescue might take is entirely irrelevant to her, and the amount of effort required is not even a consideration. So, in her dream she leaps into the river. Eventually her strength and single-minded determination are so great that she succeeds in saving her son. Then she wakes up. All the pain she endured, all the immense effort she made and all the time she invested in saving her child never existed; even the idea that she saved his life is an illusion.

This is our reality. And as practitioners, we should keep this reality in mind. Trying to help sentient beings is extremely painful and difficult, yet even when we believe we have woken up from the nightmare that is samsara, we are still dreaming. Most of the time, though, the conditions we experience are tailored to make us believe the dream is a reality and rarely show us that our existence is, in fact, entirely illusory.

### Relative Bodhichitta

If all aspects of bodhichitta are ever to arise naturally in our minds, we need to undergo some serious training, and a crucial part of that training is the development of relative bodhichitta.

Relative bodhichitta has two aspects: the bodhichitta of aspiration or intention, and the bodhichitta of application or action. The bodhichitta of aspiration helps arouse the motivation and wish to be beneficial to others through, for example, the practise of the four immeasurables. To practise the bodhichitta of action is actually to engage in beneficial activities, like practicing the six paramitas of generosity, discipline, patience, diligence, samadhi and wisdom.

Many of us already practise generosity to a degree, perhaps by tossing a coin or two to beggars in the subway, but to engage fully in the kind of generosity described in the teachings is another story entirely. Can you imagine cutting off your arms and legs, then feeding them to a hungry tiger? Such selfless acts of generosity were not uncommon amongst the great bodhisattvas of the past. They are not, however, the kinds of practise beginners are able to engage in straight away.

In a moment of inspiration many a newly hatched bodhisattva has given up everything, home, family and all their material goods. More often than not, though, within a matter of hours they bitterly regret their actions. So, the advice offered again and again by great masters like Jigme Lingpa is that beginners like us should not instantly try to accomplish the more extreme practises, but instead emphasize the practise of aspiration before gradually working up to the bodhichitta of application and absolute bodhichitta.

#### Bodhichitta of Aspiration

The bodhichitta of aspiration is, relatively speaking, quite an easy practise for beginners. For example, it is easy to aspire to be a king. All you have to do is think, “I wish I were king,” then throughout the day imagine you are king, and mentally give everything a king might own to the beggars who approach you on the street. If you think about it, there is no end to the possibilities for this kind of aspiration. For example, when you see someone in need, think, “May all sentient beings have everything they need.”

Of course, it is also possible to fall into the trap of thinking that aspiration has no effect at all. “It’s just a wish! To make a wish is all well and good, but it doesn’t change a thing. It certainly isn’t the same as actually *doing* something!” To undervalue the power of aspiration in this way is very unwise. In this mundane world it is the thought and the wish that counts, so do not make the mistake of imagining, as some do, that arousing the bodhichitta of aspiration is not “walking the talk” because it is, and it is also an important part of our spiritual training.

So, why is it so difficult for beginners to put bodhichitta into action? The main problems are that we lack determination and our bodhichitta of aspiration is weak because the only aspirations we ever make are those included in the prayers we thoughtlessly reel off each morning. We must therefore actively practise arousing an intense longing in our minds to bring all sentient beings to the ultimate happiness of enlightenment.

As Longchenpa said, for beginners, aspiration is our sole mission and task. Not least because until we reach the first bhumi, it is impossible physically and practically to perform the activities of more highly realised bodhisattvas, like chopping off a finger to feed to an iguana. We have no idea if our actions will really benefit the being we are trying to help, and since our bodhichitta is immature, although we may start out with great enthusiasm, when the person or creature fails to change or get better, we are likely to become discouraged, disappointed or angry, and may even give up trying altogether.

In the *Sutra on the Lion’s Roar* (the *Srimaladevi Sutra*), Queen Srimala made three great vows of aspiration, then confessed that had it not been for the presence and blessings of Lord Buddha himself, she would not have had the courage to make them. Firstly, she dedicated her accumulated merit towards the fulfillment of her aspiration always to be able to hear the dharma throughout every one of her future lives; secondly, she aspired never to tire of sharing the dharma with sentient beings; and thirdly, she aspired always to treasure the dharma more highly than her life and body, and to put it into practise. This is a person we should all try to emulate. And we can, because even though we are riddled with guilt, bound by duty and endlessly busy trying to take care of our families and pay the bills, none of these conditions prevents us from practising the bodhichitta of aspiration. It is, in fact, the perfect practise for us.

The Buddha offered a king some advice nearly three millennia ago, but it is just as appropriate for practitioners today who simply have too much to do. While he sympathized with the king about what an enormous job it was to run a country and the immense responsibilities and duties it entailed, it was clear to Buddha that however hard the king tried, it would be difficult for him to engage day and night in the activities of a bodhisattva. So, he suggested that the king develop a strong aspiration and longing for enlightenment, arouse the bodhichitta of aspiration in his mind as often as he could and rejoice in the virtuous activities of others. By dedicating all the merit these practises accumulated towards his enlightenment, Buddha assured the king, not only would he carry out all his royal duties successfully, he would also fulfill his duties as a bodhisattva and stockpile an infinite amount of merit.

To arouse bodhichitta is not simply a matter of feeling sorry for those you believe need help. Concern for the welfare of others is a much lower class of compassion than the bodhichitta of aspiration’s heartfelt wish to enlighten all sentient beings. Can you think of a greater gift than to introduce all sentient beings to the truth by dismantling and exposing the net of delusions that entangle them? Or to release them from the self-imposed limitations they endure as a result of the cocoon they have spun around themselves? There is simply no comparison between longing to enlighten everyone in existence and wanting to provide street kids with a hot meal.

Imagine a cave on a mountain somewhere in the Himalayas. Inside the cave sits a yogi. Day after day he practises relative bodhichitta, while in villages surrounding his mountain babies die of hunger at a horrific rate. The babies obviously need immediate, practical help, yet this yogi remains in his cave thinking about how much he would like to bring all the babies, their mothers and fathers and all sentient beings to enlightenment. However bizarre it may seem from a worldly point of view, this cave-dwelling yogi is far more worthy of homage than the international organization that ends up providing humanitarian aid. Why? Because truly and genuinely to wish that other people attain enlightenment is surprisingly difficult; it is so much easier simply to give them food, medicine and education. Those who fail to understand the true value of enlightenment find this hard to accept.

Shantideva pointed out in the first chapter of *The Way of the Bodhisattva,*

> Could our fathers or our mothers

> Ever have so generous a wish?

> Do the very gods, the rishis, even Brahma

> Harbor such benevolence as this?1

To relieve physical illness is a worldly activity and one that only provides momentary relief, whereas planning the enlightenment of all sentient beings is a permanent solution to all suffering. Should we not appreciate those who wish to remove all our pain and suffering for good more than those who only offer a temporary respite? Sadly, most of us are unaware of this distinction because we have never genuinely wished that someone else achieve enlightenment. If we were offered a one-way ticket to enlightenment, we would most likely grab it for ourselves without even considering offering it to someone else.

Imagine you are given a potion which, if you drank it, would make you clairvoyant and omniscient. Would you drink it all yourself or would you share it with someone else? Before you decide, remember how often practitioners feel jealous of sangha members who appear to be more dedicated practitioners than they are. Or how jealous you feel when you learn another sangha member received a high teaching, but you were not invited. If you had genuinely aroused bodhichitta in your mind, you would be happy that your friends practise so much and rejoice at all the teachings they receive. It is so easy to forget that, should anyone at all attain enlightenment, it would mean that your wishes and aspirations had, at long last, been fulfilled.

**Aspiration Keeps Beginners Humble**

I must stress how unwise it is for freshly minted bodhisattvas, whose love, compassion, bodhichitta and confidence in the view have yet to mature, to try to act too quickly or, as many do, start imagining they are on some kind of mission. If you have any mission at all, it is to pray and aspire that one day you will *become* a hero. The last thing you should do is start behaving like a hero from day one, and if you do, it will only bring disappointment and frustration. If you were to launch into what you imagine to be a bodhisattva activity, like building a children’s hospital or feeding a street full of beggars, and it was a success, you would probably become a victim of your own blistering pride. It is far less likely that your pride will run away with itself if you make a *wish* that all sick children be helped or hungry beggars fed, and this is why the practise of making aspirations is so strongly recommended for beginners. The natural sense of humility built into the practise of aspiration is one of things that makes it so beautiful.

Usually, though, beginners consider prayer and aspiration to be a poor alternative to “action” because we feel so useless when we are not busy. Being useful and having something useful to do have become extremely important to us—we are often quite desperate to be considered “capable” and “valuable”—but “useful” or “useless” are both relative states, and for newbies it is far more important to aspire to become useful than to achieve anything concrete.

As Shantideva wrote,

> May I be a guard for those who are protectorless,

> A guide for those who journey on the road.

> For those who wish to go across the water,

> May I be a boat, a raft, a bridge.

> May I be an isle for those who yearn for landfall,

> And a lamp for those who long for light;

> For those who need a resting place, a bed;

> For all who need a servant, may I be their slave.2

Aspiring again and again to be of use in this way gradually develops your confidence and strengthens your mind until, eventually, you become useful quite naturally. Without such confidence and strength, any one-off, accidental success may put you on a pedestal for a day or two, but you will soon be knocked off, which can be terribly disillusioning.

**How to Care for Others**

Why should we care about other people? Especially these days, living as we do in a culture of greed and selfishness where the majority are solely concerned about feathering their own nests. Perhaps the most potent reason for caring for others is that we long to be cared for ourselves. One of the most insidious symptoms of these degenerate times is that so many people feel uncared for, and put all their effort into trying to feel loved by friends, lovers, and even members of the clubs they join.

Classic Buddhist texts about bodhichitta tell us we should begin to practise feeling love and compassion for others by first thinking about what our mother went through when she had us. Of course, we must also remember that the concept of “mother” is perceived differently depending on the culture and times in which we live. These days, for example, too many children are neglected and abused by their parents for many of us automatically to respond positively to the idea of a mother. Therefore, some of you may not find this example helpful, but it’s just an example. If you prefer, think about an aunt or a brother or a good friend who you know loves you, cares for you and only wants the very best for you, misses you when the two of you are apart and wakes up in the middle of the night worrying about you. Now imagine that every single sentient being has loved and cared for you in this way.

For ignorant beings like us, what we feel and experience right now is far more powerful than memories of past relationships. I am sure we can all think of someone we were close to ten years ago yet we hardly ever think of them now. If we forget people we have cared for in this life so easily, what hope is there of remembering those we cared for in our previous lives? Nevertheless, many hundreds of thousands of sentient beings have sacrificed their food, wealth and happiness for our sakes, and isn’t our habitual response to such kindness to want to repay our debt. But how? How can we repay in this life the kindness of those we met in previous lives? How can we give them food, shelter, money and comfort when we have no idea who they are? The best we can do is care for all sentient beings equally by doing everything we can to bring them to enlightenment.

Some practitioners think that the most important kind of bodhichitta is absolute bodhichitta, with the bodhichitta of application rather less important and the bodhichitta of aspiration the least important of all. But it doesn’t work like that. All three aspects of bodhichitta are equally important. Most of the time, and certainly in the case of beginners, when we think of “emptiness,” we immediately create a mental picture of emptiness that is the antithesis of what it really is. Which is why the best advice is to start out by practicing the bodhichitta of aspiration and gradually work up to the bodhichitta of application and absolute bodhichitta.

**The Objects of Our Compassion**

According to Rongzompa, beginners should use sentient beings as the objects of their compassion, but he is not implying that sentient beings truly exist. Sentient beings are an illusion and their nature is empty, yet their sufferings are easy for us to see, like a rainbow in the sky. Compassion aroused by such an object is not tarnished by attachment and will never exhaust you; it is limitless, inexhaustible and not entirely lacking in wisdom.

According to Maitreya in the *Mahayanasutralamkara,*3 sentient beings, the objects of our compassion, fall into a number of categories:

- beings who crave desirable objects;
- beings under the influence of the “enemy” (their emotions);
- beings suffering from the effects of negative actions, which include those reborn into the lower realms, and the “ignorant,” who do not know right from wrong and have no idea about cause, condition and effect (for example, the butcher who imagines he has to kill in order to survive);
- beings who hold wrong views and therefore have “an extra binding force” (in addition to being ignorant sentient beings they have been taught by theoreticians and are therefore burdened with even more wrong views that need clarification), and those who like the “poisoned food” of worldly enjoyment;
- practitioners who are “lost” because they are proud; and those who are devoted, diligent and without pride, but have been sidetracked into choosing a lesser path, like the path of self-liberation; and
- bodhisattavas who have yet to accumulate vast merit and are therefore “weak.”

Certain practitioners of mahasandhi or mahamudra might also be added to this list if their motivation to practise these paths is curiosity about the nature of mind and a wish to understand it better. This is the not the right motivation for any Buddhist practitioner to practise any aspect of dharma, because whether you practise shamatha meditation or mahasandhi, all Buddhist practise should be motivated by compassion for the sufferings of sentient beings.

#### Bodhichitta of Aspiration: The Practises

As we have seen, the bodhichitta of aspiration is a practise that lies comfortably within the capacity of beginners. It involves reading the wonderful aspiration prayers composed by great bodhisattvas, like *The King of Aspiration Prayers: Samantabhadra’s “Aspiration to Good Actions*,”4 and avoiding anything that might sabotage your aspirations. At first glance it looks fairly straightforward because all you really have to do is wish that all sentient beings become enlightened, again and again. But there are stumbling blocks, so you should do whatever it takes to maintain this practise.

There will be times, for example, when you feel you are faking it. However hard you try genuinely to practise, it just doesn’t feel right. And on the rare occasions it does feel authentic, the sensation is over almost before it began. So, try to be content with your practise, whatever it feels like, even when you are doing little more than paying it lip service, because at least you are making an effort.

Sentient beings are ignorant and intensely emotional; what we long for today we entirely reject tomorrow. Our moods swing from one extreme to another and yet we have no idea what we really want. Even if you had the money to buy your friends the expensive luxuries they long for, would you ever be able to satisfy their lust for material wealth? Although the vast majority don’t know it yet, what all sentient beings really need is enlightenment, and so it is up to you to decide for them. Make the wish “May I bring all sentient beings to enlightenment” and then discipline yourself to follow your practise schedule, come what may. Resolve never to give sentient beings what they think they want, but only what they really need: freedom from all dualistic distinctions, obsessions and entanglements, and freedom from constantly falling into the extremes of good and bad. And remember, all this can be accomplished through the practise of aspiration, so even when it feels as though you are faking it, just keep practicing.

Two methods for arousing bodhichitta are particularly popular: tonglen practise, and a series of contemplations called the four immeasurables.

**1. Tonglen**

Tonglen is a wonderful practise because it provides a specific technique through which we can invoke compassion and bodhichitta. Time and again we have been told that our habit of self-cherishing and thinking solely of our own well-being is the source of misery and saboteur of our spiritual path, so the obvious method for countering it is to cherish others.

The method is simple. As you breathe out, give all happiness, virtue and material wealth to each and every sentient being without exception; as you breathe in, absorb all their pain, suffering, problems, obscurations and unvirtuous thoughts and actions.

Tonglen practise is where we start to attune our motivation and is perhaps the best practise for a beginner. As the great Kadampa masters of the past have told us repeatedly, the practise of bodhichitta is about cultivating the attitude of giving all profit and gain to others, and taking all loss and defeat upon ourselves.

One of the great blights suffered by modern people is a lack of self-esteem or healthy sense of self. It leads some new students to ask if taking on the suffering of others in tonglen practise might cause them to lose confidence in themselves. Quite the opposite is true. The attitude we cultivate as bodhisattvas—of longing to offer the best of everything to others and willingly accept all loss, unpleasantness or difficulty—actually bolsters our confidence and completely eradicates a lack of self-esteem.

Shantideva wrote,

> What need is there to say more?

> The childish work for their own benefit,

> The buddhas work for the benefit of others.

> Just look at the difference between them.

> If I do not exchange my happiness

> For the suffering of others,

> I shall not attain the state of buddhahood

> And even in samsara I shall have no real joy.5

Where does low self-esteem come from? Those with low self-esteem tend to have highly developed egos; they long to be best at everything and valued highly by everyone they meet, and imagine their ego is repressed, weak and needs boosting. But once we develop the attitude of a bodhisattva, we have little or no ego and therefore there is no “I” to worry about giving all the good stuff away or to be bothered by the bad stuff. Bodhisattvas lack “ego” as a reference point, and therefore their confidence continues to grow, giving even the idea of low self-esteem no chance of raising its ugly head. So don’t be afraid of applying the bodhichitta of aspiration again and again.

**2. The Four Immeasurables**

There is widespread misunderstanding about love and compassion that is difficult to correct. It is certainly wrong, but the snag is that it also contains an element of truth. Take, for example, the love we feel for our pet dog. Generally, this kind of love is prompted by our need for companionship, unquestioning adoration and obedience—it’s satisfying to see a dog sit when we tell him to. In a way, it is a kind of love, but it is incomplete. Profound love is entirely unconditional, devoid of expectation, personal agendas and selfish motives. To feel profound love for a dog would mean not only caring for him by feeding him, taking him for walks and keeping him clean, but at the highest possible level, to give him the opportunity to discover the dharma, which will eventually lead him to enlightenment.

As the term suggests, the four immeasurables is a practise that is made up of four contemplations that are immeasurable in every way, including the immeasurable objects of our practise: *all* sentient beings.

What are the four immeasurable thoughts?

#### Love

The contemplation of love develops the wish that all sentient beings will be happy and always encounter the causes of happiness. Start by thinking, “May all sentient beings be happy right now, at this very moment.” Then call to mind someone who is suffering from serious depression right now. As you think, “May all sentient beings enjoy happiness,” imagine that this person gets exactly what he needs to alleviate his depression and make the wish, “May he encounter all the causes of happiness and, ideally, the dharma.”

Therefore, the first immeasurable thought is “May all sentient beings enjoy happiness and the causes of happiness.” It differs from the ordinary humanitarian idea of “giving,” because rather than just providing one person with a meal or a bed, we are giving absolutely everyone both happiness and the causes of happiness.

#### Compassion

Here, we develop the wish that sentient beings be free from suffering and the causes of suffering. Focusing once again on the person suffering from depression, as well as all the depressed people in this world, think, “May they be free from both depression and the causes of depression.” The causes, in this case, are their emotions and their unvirtuous thoughts and actions. “May they be freed from all their sufferings this very moment!” And also, “May sentient beings refrain from killing, stealing, lying and all the negative emotions that cause unhappiness.” With this contemplation you have both given sentient beings happiness and its causes, and separated them from unhappiness and suffering and the causes of unhappiness and suffering.

Karma Chagme Rinpoche said that although all sentient beings had, at one time or another, been his mother, as they have no memory of that relationship, they feel free either to attack him or remain strangers. We, on the other hand, know of our connection; how, then, can we not be friendly with everyone?

This is probably true for all of us. For example, if your loving mother suddenly went crazy and attacked you, your first reaction would probably be concern, followed by an upsurge of even more love and compassion for her.

#### Joy

Next, we develop the wish that sentient beings will never be separated from “happiness” and “not suffering” by arousing a sense of appreciative joy: “May all sentient beings remain joyful forever.”

Joy is extremely important within the context of these immeasurable contemplations because even though we make a wish that everyone be completely happy, we still tend to feel jealous of those who appear better off than we are. Jealousy and envy are what I call “loser” emotions, and the antidote to them both is to arouse a sense of appreciative joy.

#### Equanimity

Lastly, since sentient beings suffer as a result of their attachment and aggression, we wish for them to be freed from all hopes and fears, aggression and attachment to worldly things (the eight worldly dharmas), so that eventually even the idea of making distinctions will not occur to them.

Think,

> May all sentient beings abide in equanimity, free from bias, attachment and anger.

> May they be free from hope and fear, passion and aggression.

> May they have no notion of “family” or “enemy”;

> May they remain impartial, neither aggressive to enemies nor attached to friends,

> And thus may all beings and all phenomena be equal.

Allowing this thought to rest in your mind is the practise of equanimity.

Practitioners must invest time and energy in calling to mind these four contemplations, and although it may sound abstract, like building an infinitely long ladder into the sky, always remember that this is a very important method for training the mind. Bodhisattvas need to develop unwavering courage and motivation, and you should start the process here and now. Contemplate each thought separately, applying it first to your loved ones, then extending it to your friends and neighbours, and eventually to all sentient beings. And it is not just an exercise in wishful thinking. Ultimately, we are making a heartfelt aspiration that all sentient beings be freed from making the distinction between good and bad, right and wrong.

There are good reasons for these four thoughts to be described as “immeasurable”—it’s a word we should pay some attention to, and should be understood in the context of “the path is the goal.” As it is now, when we pray, “May all sentient beings be enlightened,” we automatically imagine that bringing all beings to enlightenment is a measurable goal and that there is an end to the process. But there isn’t. If there were, we would be contemplating “the four measurable thoughts.” So, we must get used to the idea that our spiritual path has an immeasurable goal and that we follow it with an immeasurable attitude and an immeasurable motivation. In other words, no goal, no end to the process and a goal-less motivation.

### The Bodhichitta of Application

#### The Six Paramitas

To practise the bodhichitta of application is to become involved in the specific activities known as the six paramitas. They are the practises of generosity, discipline, patience, diligence, samadhi or meditation and wisdom. Why are there six? Because, as Lord Maitreya points out, we need them to counter six specific habits that he describes as “fondnesses” or “loves.” Whichever paramita you practise, if you are motivated by a wish to enlighten all sentient beings, it can be classified as a genuine practise of bodhichitta in application, albeit at the beginner’s level.

**1. Generosity**

We have a fondness for imagining we are poor as well as for being occupied; for many of us, not to be busy is almost unbearable. We can counter this and all our habits related to poverty mentality with the practise of generosity.

Buddha himself advised that even when our generosity extends to little more than giving away a vegetable, if we imagine the vegetable is one of our limbs or a hunk of our flesh, it will help us become accustomed to far greater forms of generosity. As always, our desire to be truly generous should be motivated by a longing for enlightenment, but too often it is tinged with the ambition to be recognized as a good person and a pillar of the community, or the hope for a more luxurious rebirth. So, a bodhisattva must get used to resisting the lure of minor temporal achievement.

As Maitreya said in the *Mahayanasutralamkara,* for a bodhisattva a beggar can be a cause for perfecting generosity. So when you are approached by a beggar, think to yourself, “This person is giving me the opportunity to plant the seed of inexhaustible wealth in the future. He could even be the manifestation of a buddha or a bodhisattva. By making an offering to this beggar, however small, I may be creating the initial link through which I one day bring him the inexhaustible wealth of enlightenment.” The vajrayana teaches that this beggar, or any pest, could even be a manifestation of your guru.

**2. Discipline**

We are also fond of trouble, and to challenge this habit, as a follower of the mahayana path a bodhisattva will apply three kinds of discipline: the discipline of refraining from unvirtuous actions, such as killing, lying or slandering others; the discipline that helps gather virtuous qualities, such as taking refuge, arousing bodhichitta or practicing the six paramitas; and the discipline of helping others.

By the way, if, as a bodhisattva, you must lie or interrupt your spiritual practise in order to keep the discipline of helping others, then so be it. You must always do whatever is necessary to help sentient beings, even if it means forfeiting the first two disciplines.

**3. Patience**

Getting worked up and taking our feelings to extremes is another fondness we indulge in, and to kick this habit, we need patience.

There are many different ways for a bodhisattva to practise patience. You could, for example, voluntarily take on the pain and suffering of others; or never compromise over your dharma practise so that no matter what happens in your life, dharma will always take precedence; or cultivate a “couldn’t-care-less” attitude about those who want to harm or slander you.

The practise of being uncompromising about the dharma can be viewed from two perspectives: relative and ultimate. If someone harms you, as a bodhisattva you should develop the attitude that samsara is pain and suffering in the same way that fire is hot; pain is inevitable, therefore there is no reason for losing patience with the perpetrator of your pain. At the same time, since the nature of samsaric beings is inherently good, the harm they inflict is temporary, and once again, there is no reason for you to lose patience. Instead, as Shantideva suggested, ask yourself why you acted as you did to create the karma that caused someone else to harm you.

> Instead, why did they act in times gone by

> That they are now so harmed at others’ hands?

> Since everything depends on karma,

> Why should I be angry at such things?6

“Absolute” patience is to recognize that you, the act of harming and the one who harms are all illusions. This is how we develop patience.

**4. Diligence**

However much we insist that we want to work hard and really focus on what needs to be done, the reality is that we have a great fondness for being lazy, unfocused and utterly careless, and the antidote is diligence.

To be diligent is to develop joy in the path and activities of a bodhisattva. However, having only experienced joy through samsaric endeavour for so many lifetimes, to delight in the activities of a bodhisattva is difficult; the idea of sitting still and meditating for an hour can seem impossible. We must therefore be diligent about developing joy by every means available, for example by listening to dharma teachings, contemplating them and hanging out with an inspiring crowd.

Similarly, when attacked by unfavorable circumstances and sadness, we must learn to use them as a reminder not to focus obsessively on trying to solve our problems—which by and large are insoluble—and instead contemplate the pitfalls of samsara as a way of helping us to overpower our laziness.

**5. Samadhi**

The next “fondness” is particularly pertinent to modern people for whom individual rights, the need for personal space and independence are so important. Independence, we declare loudly and often, is crucial to our well-being. In practise, though, we have a fondness for slavery. We love to be ruled by other people and things, to be chained up, dragged by the hair and pushed and pulled by atmosphere, circumstance and situation. And to combat this fondness, we apply meditation practise because, as the practise involves doing nothing at all, it is as unlike submitting to circumstances as it’s possible to be.

One of the biggest challenges we face is our inability to focus on one thing at a time, and this is the result of having developed a fondness for going astray over many lifetimes. This old habit makes transcending the blinding glare of our contradictory emotions extremely hard—too often our emotions outshine us. Therefore, we must continuously develop our ability to remain undistracted.

**6. Wisdom**

We have a fondness for the wrong view, which is an infinitely complex and fascinating subject, but to put it very simply, a wrong view is any kind of extreme view.

To be shocked when something fails to work the way we believe it should is an indication that we hold a wrong view. It happens because we only focus on surface causes and conditions, not the more subtle ones. On the face of it, the scientific view that nothing happens without a reason and everything is a result of causes and conditions appears to be a “right view.” But there are also people these days who are almost proud of not believing in the workings of cause, condition and effect and therefore of reincarnation. Logically, this means that neither do they believe in the concepts of virtue and nonvirtue, good karma and bad karma. So it is no surprise that, deep down, even those of us who regard ourselves as followers of the Buddha’s teachings (especially those on karma) habitually accept that situations and things can manifest randomly.

Wisdom is the most important attribute of a bodhisattva because, as Dharmakirti said, however important it may be to cultivate profound, precious love and compassion, doing so does not fundamentally contradict ignorance. Therefore developing love and compassion alone will not uproot samsaric existence.

A question often asked by students is, “What is ‘sin’ in Buddhism.” “Sin,” usually translated from the Tibetan term as “nonvirtue,” is anything that springs from the six fondnesses; it could be connected with just one fondness, a combination of several or all of them at once.

Traditionally, sentient beings who do not practise the six paramitas are said to be like a blind man who is lost and wandering in a jungle or a desert. He is blind because he does not know which activities to adopt and which to abandon, and has no friend or master to guide him. He lacks the rations of generosity for nourishment or the armour of discipline for protection. His wealth of patience has run out, and the diligence that impels his progress is unsteady. He has no meditation and therefore nowhere to rest, and worst of all, he has no staff of wisdom for support.

Never imagine, though, that the six paramitas are beyond your reach, because as Shantideva said, there is nothing to which you cannot become accustomed.

### Absolute Bodhichitta

To apply emptiness as an antidote to ego might be a little tricky when you first enter into the teachings of the bodhisattvayana. If that is the case, the best alternative antidote is simply to generate the wish to enlighten each and every sentient being (bodhichitta).

However, if you have received instructions on how to practise absolute bodhichitta according to the mahamudra and mahasandhi traditions, or vipashyana, or the nondifferentiation of samsara and nirvana, or the union of bliss and emptiness, or simply as part of a teaching giving a systematic guide to emptiness, then by all means do that practise. If you have yet to receive these instructions, as a beginner who longs to understand and practise absolute bodhichitta, to hear about and contemplate it can be your practise.

### The End of the Session

Once you have completed the practises of taking refuge and arousing bodhichitta, dissolve the field of merit into you and mix your mind with the mind of your guru. Then remain for as long as you can in the state of inseparability (a few moments, a minute or even an hour) without fabricating in any way. This can be considered a meditation on absolute bodhichitta.

The customary method for arousing bodhichitta is to recite a text, but do remember that to repeat the relevant verses from your ngöndro text without being genuinely motivated to practise for the enlightenment of all sentient beings is entirely useless.

By now you will have a greater appreciation of how arousing bodhichitta works as an antidote to ego, and so obviously, you should never try to generate bodhichitta motivated by the ambition to be recognized as a great bodhisattva.

Always remember, none of us can spend all our time with people we like, and so when you find yourself rubbing shoulders with people you really dislike, try to focus your mind on thinking good thoughts, however weak, fake or even hypocritical they may seem. Remind yourself that your reaction to others is merely your own interpretation of who they are, and instead try to put yourself in the shoes of the person who is getting on your nerves. Remember, they feel as paranoid as you do and are just as enthralled by their powerful emotions. Or imagine that this exasperating person has been sent by the buddhas and bodhisattvas to help you to generate more bodhichitta.

In every situation, always try to develop a good heart. After all, a good heart is the key to love, compassion and bodhichitta. Without bodhichitta, every practise you do will merely augment your already massive ego. To have a good heart and to practise loving-kindness (which are different ways of describing the same thing) are the seeds of bodhichitta, but unless you also arouse the wish to enlighten all sentient beings, bodhichitta will never fully blossom in your mind.

For those practicing arousing bodhichitta in a retreat situation, the visualisation of the field of merit is extremely important. It is exactly the same as it was when you took refuge, but here the buddhas and bodhisattvas are present to witness your resolve. To pledge to bring all sentient beings to enlightenment in the presence of these sublime beings will not only protect you from obstacles, it will also make bodhichitta seem more real for you and therefore more beneficial and powerful, and it will reinforce your sacred commitment to keep your word.

## 9. The Practise of Purification by Meditating on the Guru as Vajrasattva

The essence of all ngöndro practise is to train habitually rigid minds to become more flexible, and all of us suffer from rigid minds. Why? Mostly because we surrender so easily to our emotional responses and the objects of our emotions: hope and fear. For aeons, almost everything we think and feel, all our interpretations, have been rooted in hope or fear, which, in turn, have bound our minds up in turbulent emotions, constraining them to such a degree that we no longer have any control over them. This is why, according to the shravakayana teachings, we need to *tame* the mind, or from the bodhisattvayana point of view *train* it to become useful, or from a vajrayana perspective *recognize* mind.

For the sake of simplicity, though, let’s stick with the term “training the mind.” And the first step towards training the mind is repeatedly to recognize and reflect on the futility of samsaric life. As we have already seen, to continue to value any part of worldly life creates a loophole in our fundamental attitude that will eventually compromise our dharma practise. It is important, therefore, genuinely to recognize the pointlessness of worldly activity, material possessions and relationships, and as we have already seen, contemplating the common foundations is a very good method for vividly bringing to mind just how terminally barren samsara really is. Even though the dharma contains a vast treasury of extraordinary teachings, we can accumulate tremendous merit merely by listening to teachings on these four thoughts, or contemplations, over and over again.

Having laid the first two foundations of ngöndro practise by diverting our attention from the wrong path to the right path with the practise of taking refuge, and from the lesser path to the greater path by arousing bodhichitta, the practise of Vajrasattva now shows us how to cleanse and purify the vessel into which we will pour the nectar of dharma, body, speech and mind.

According to the vajrayana, our physical body is as much a vessel as our mind is, and it too must be cleansed and purified. The bodhisattvayana and shravakayana traditions hold a different view. They consider the sole vessel of dharma to be the mind, and although they agree it needs taming, they insist that the body is merely a servant of mind and advise us to treat it as a master would his slave. In return for her work a slave is given board and lodging, but the master must take care not to be overindulgent in case she tries to take advantage of him. As Shantideva wrote in verses that have already appeared in this text, but bear repetition,

> If servants who cannot be set to work

> Are not rewarded with supplies and clothing.

> Why do you sustain with such great pains

> This body, which, though nourished, will abandon you?

> So pay this body due remuneration,

> And then be sure to make it work for you.

> But do not lavish everything

> On what will not bring perfect benefit.1

From the vajrayana point of view, though, as our physical body is the most tangible and obvious part of us, it can be used as a tool at every stage on the path, not just as a slave or a vessel. Therefore the structure, sensations, well-being and nourishment of every part of our body, inside and out, are crucially important on the path to liberation.

### Obscurations and Defilements: Bad Karma

The *Jatakamala Sutra*2 tells us that demons suffer their greatest anxieties when they see a person devote himself to the path of liberation. New dharma practitioners are bound to discover many priceless jewels on the path, but it is equally likely that they will stir up a great many obstacles—which brings us to the subject of “bad” karma.

“Bad karma” is one of many ways the dharma describes the obscurations and defilements that generally manifest as obstacles and that we need to purify. Whether an unwelcome glitch in your plans really is an obstacle or a blessing, though, is hard to tell. Practitioners sometimes endure long periods when their practise appears to have no effect on what seem to be particularly stubborn obstacles, and as a result assume their practise is not working. But they are wrong. With hindsight, they come to realise that the “obstacle” they so desperately wanted to eradicate was actually the best thing that ever happened them. This kind of turnaround is quite common, and with experience practitioners begin to appreciate that “bad” circumstances create a far more fertile ground for practise than “good” ones.

So, what is it that we purify with Vajrasattva practise? Most crucially, the defilement of clinging to self. Although none of our defilements are particularly hard to purify, because by nature they are all temporary, having been part of our karmic makeup for so long, they are now such deeply entrenched habits that to separate “me” from “my defilement” can seem impossible. The very fact that such a distinction is so difficult to make is why it is so crucially important for us to make the effort to purify.

For a vajrayana practitioner, the name Vajrasattva automatically brings to mind the principles of purification and cleansing. Both words imply the presence of “dirt”—terms we are stuck with because we lack a more precise form of language—and tend to induce extreme paranoia about the possible exposure of all our defilements and obscurations, an ordeal most of us would do almost anything to avoid. We much prefer to focus on our good qualities. Yet, to purify defilements is to uncover our buddha nature; and if buddha nature were not an inherent part of us, there would be no point in trying to purify our defilements. This is quite an important point, because once we understand it, we can avoid getting worked up about our failings. But it’s an aspect of purification that many practitioners overlook. They put all their energy into avoiding admitting that they have any personal dirt at all, let alone the anger, jealousy and greed they are ruled by. And so, rather than worrying about everything that needs purifying, it is far more productive to focus on uncovering our buddha nature through the practise of Vajrasattva.

This point is best illustrated by the example of washing a coffee-stained cup. Why wash the cup in the first place? Because you want it to be clean. But if the cup were permanently dirty, no matter how hard you tried, you would never be able remove the stain, and the whole process would become both frustrating and extremely painful. This is why we think in terms of “uncovering” buddha nature, which is the main point of Vajrasattva practise, rather than “purifying” defilements. Do bear in mind that Buddhist teachings, particularly those from the vajrayana, have nothing against the dirt, and that the dirt is not the cup but a totally separate entity.

### Why Visualise the Guru as Vajrasattva?

Your guru is the reflection of your devotion and merit, and devotion is a manifestation of your buddha nature and merit. Without merit, you will not be able to recognize your guru when you meet him, or, like Devadatta, perceive a single one of his qualities. So, merit plays an important role in your ability to recognize your guru, and devotion is an expression of that merit.

The guru’s role changes throughout the ngöndro in the same way a woman’s role changes according to the purpose she serves in each interaction. For her mother she is a daughter, for her husband a wife, and for her employer the person who cleans his house. Likewise, all the buddhas and bodhisattvas are, in essence, exactly the same, but since we are dualistic creatures, the name, colour and appearance of each of them help us focus on specific tasks. In this practise, it is your guru, visualised as Vajrasattva, who purifies your defilements.

Technically, a perfectly valid form of purification practise would be to visualise Guru Rinpoche rather than Vajrasattva on top of your head, and chant either the hundred-syllable or six-syllable Vajrasattva mantra or Guru Rinpoche’s twelve-syllable mantra—the vajrayana is a treasury of incredible methods. The power to purify, though, has always been linked with Vajrasattva, and his practise is considered to be the supreme method of purification. Buddhist history tells us that Vajrasattva’s tremendous power is rooted in the aspiration he made as a bodhisattva for his name alone to purify even the most egregious defilements once he attained buddhahood.

In this practise you visualise your guru as Vajrasattva, and here again students often ask, “Why not just visualise the guru as he is?” In this case, one of the risks of picturing your guru as an ordinary human being is that as you make your confession, you might be tempted to hold something back. After all, if your guru is just an ordinary person, how can he tell whether or not you are being completely honest? Has he not, on numerous occasions, demonstrated that he is unaware of a great deal of what you have done, and are these apparent gaps in his knowledge not somehow reassuring? We are conditioned not to behave badly in front of our guru, but would be excruciatingly embarrassed were he to learn of things we do when we are not with him. All of which makes it much harder to develop a strong devotion for him or to think of him as the Buddha. This is why we visualise the guru in the form of the omniscient buddha Vajrasattva, who knows everything there is to know about the past, present and future, and from whom we can hide nothing. It’s a great opportunity to expose the whole spectrum of our shameful, negative activities, and often doing so comes as a tremendous relief.

### Devotion: Trusting Mantra and Visualisation

Mipham Rinpoche says in his explanation of the *Guhyagarbha Tantra* that regardless of which deity of the mandala you practise, your visualisation should be both clear and vivid. However, for those who find visualisation difficult and can only manage a fuzzy, abstract generalization, rest assured that if you feel genuine devotion for and trust in your guru, the path and the efficacy of the mantra, there is no doubt you will attain all the ordinary and supreme spiritual accomplishments.

There are many levels of devotion. Supreme devotion is the absolute conviction that the deity and the mantra are inseparable. If you are unable to manage supreme devotion, try to develop unwavering devotion, which is an implicit trust in the teachings of the Buddha and your master, and fully accomplishes the paths of mantra and visualisation.

Trust and devotion in the power of mantra are also extremely important. As a ngöndro practitioner, when you chant the Vajrasattva mantra, you should believe, with one-pointed trust and devotion, that the mantra *is* Buddha Vajrasattva—even during sessions when your mind is wild and chaotic. As Mipham Rinpoche wrote, accomplishment is not possible for those who lack the wisdom to understand the profound meaning of mantrayana and whose stubborn, doubting, incessantly analytical mind creates inner obstacles. Even when mind is distracted and whirling in all directions, mantra practitioners must trust completely in the power of mantra, which is so great that even if the sky were to cease to exist, the power of the mantra would remain.

### The Four Powers

Certain ingredients are vital if the practise of Vajrasattva is to work:

- the belief that Vajrasattva has the power to purify you;
- genuine regret and remorse for all your negative activities of the past, present and future;
- the resolution and determination never to repeat those negative actions again; and
- absolute conviction in the power of the practise.

I can almost hear you thinking, “I’m certain to end up acting negatively. I won’t be able to help myself! So perhaps I’d better not practise Vajrasattva.” But these thoughts are just a sign that you are taking this information too literally. Remember, you are a “practitioner,” which means you practise to get used to a new way of being and thinking, and nobody expects you to keep all your promises straight away (although you should always do your best).

Even though you may not remember doing anything wrong or that you regret in this lifetime, can you be sure you never will? Or that you have never acted negatively in a past life? Whether you are aware of your unwholesome behaviour or not, it is important to develop regret for any and all your negative activities throughout beginningless time, and that you resolve never to act negatively again. You must also believe that Guru Vajrasattva really is omniscient, omnipotent, immensely powerful and compassionate; that he is right there in front of you as you practise; and that the practise itself will work.

### The Visualisation

Think of yourself in your ordinary form, defilements and all, and visualise your guru in the form of Guru Vajrasattva on top of your head. He should appear like a reflection on water, perhaps a little empty and lifeless at first, but slowly becoming more and more animated. (To get some idea of how a reflection appears, take a look at your face in a mirror: when you move, your reflection moves.)

Vajrasattva is white in colour, adorned with all the sambhogakaya ornaments and embraces his consort. In his right hand he holds a vajra at his heart chakra, in his left a bell that he rests on his left thigh. A small moon disc seat appears at his heart, in the centre of which stands the syllable HUM. Believe beyond doubt that Vajrasattva is the manifestation of the mind of all the buddhas and invoke him strongly and with absolute confidence that he really is sitting above your head.

#### The Nectar Flow

As you chant the hundred-syllable Vajrasattva mantra, visualise that from the syllable HUM tremendous rays of light radiate in all ten directions emanating infinite offerings of flowers, incense, Indian bathhouses, elegant mansions, lush gardens, peacocks, elephants and all the beautiful and desirable things you can imagine. These offerings, borne on rays of light, are offered to the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions and then reconverge into the syllable HUM at your heart along with all their blessings. As you continue to chant the mantra, nectar flows from the HUM in the form of light, or a milky liquid, filling Vajrasattva’s body, then flowing through his secret place into his consort’s body and filling her too. Nectar continues to pour nonstop from the syllable and streams out through every pore of both deities and especially from their secret places, cascading like a waterfall down into the fontanelle on top of your head.

As the nectar passes through your body, it flushes out all illnesses, obscurations, defilements and bad energy, pushing them through your body and out of your anus, secret place and toes. Consider that it purifies all defilements related to physical activities, in other words the bad karma accumulated by killing, stealing or sexual misconduct, as well as all physical illnesses and symptoms, such as headaches, stomachaches—anything, in fact, that falls into the category of defilements of the “nadi.” Nadi are the energy centres and channels that run throughout your body. They are defiled when we kill people, or eat food prepared by obscured and polluted beings, mix with them or spend time where they live. We cleanse our blocked and rusty channels and chakras during Vajrasattva practise in the same way we use a strong bleach to clean the toilet.

Be creative with your visualisation. Imagine, for example, that as the nectar flows through and cleanses you, your body actually changes colour. So, once the nectar has reached your forehead, the top half of your skull will appear to be clean and white, while the rest of your body remains darker and dirtier-looking. This method will enhance the power of your visualisation, and if you think it might help, why not try it.

Again, remember you are a beginner, so it would be foolish to expect to be able to establish all the details of this visualisation in one session, although if you can, of course you should. Otherwise just do your best.

If you are accumulating one hundred thousand recitations of the hundred-syllable Vajrasattva mantra, focus on a different aspect of the visualisation during each practise session. For example, one day chant the mantra while concentrating on the visualisation of Vajrasattva above your head, and the next focus on the letter HUM at his heart, with the rest of the visualisation in soft focus. On the third day concentrate on the nectar flow, and on the fourth do all three together. If you have a lot of time, visualise the nectar flowing into your forehead for a week or a month, then imagine it flows down to your throat for another week or month. If you don’t have time, spend half an hour on your forehead and then on your throat. Alternatively, you could try practicing each of the different stages for fifteen minutes, five minutes or two minutes—it is entirely up to you. But please, be flexible.

As the nectar flows down to your throat, although strictly speaking it purifies *all* defilements, you can think specifically of those to do with speech, such as lies, abusive language, threats, harsh words, gossip and mistakes you make in your mantra recitations (for example, leaving out words, adding extra ones and poor pronunciation). It also purifies bad energy and disorders related to speech, like being misunderstood. For example, when you say “right,” people think you said “left,” or however good your heart is, everything you say annoys and irritates others, because your words and commands are not powerful, or seductive enough, or even too seductive.

It is especially important for vajrayana students to deal with the defilements of prana, or inner air, and Vajrasattva practise is a very good method for doing just that. There are many ways we can damage and obscure our prana, for example breathing bad air, smoking hashish and cigars, drinking alcohol, slandering vajrayana masters, making dualistic judgments about pure phenomena or even inhaling the wrong incense. Each and every day we breathe in all kinds of impurities that obscure our prana, and it all needs cleansing and purifying.

A really powerful visualisation is to imagine that once the nectar flow has carried the defilements of prana down through your body, it emerges in the form of black liquid from your anus, secret place and toes. Then all physical diseases leave your body in the form of pus and blood; and the bad energy of prana and nadi in the form of butterflies, scorpions, insects and animals.

As you continue to chant the hundred-syllable mantra, the nectar flows down to your heart chakra and cleanses all defilements related to mind, including, for example, the bad karma created by jealousy, competitiveness, aggression, selfishness, pride, covetousness, wishing others harm and wrong view—including extreme views, like sacrificing living beings in the name of spiritual practise. We must have accumulated a great many of these kinds of defilements throughout our previous lives and are still stained by them. There are also many disturbances of the mind that are the result of the defilement of “bindu,” which means “drop” or bodhichitta. Examples of this are depression, anxiety, nervousness and bulimia, as well as the obsessive ambition, bordering on madness, that prompts us to want to achieve enlightenment faster than anyone else. And these are just the obvious examples. There are many other subtle bindu-related defilements—we are absolutely loaded with them—that essentially separate subject and object. And our inability to unite subject and object, appearance and emptiness, is the most subtle kind of defilement.

There are many ways of being creative with this practise. You can go step-by-step from the fontanelle to the forehead, throat and so on; or you can do the whole visualisation in one go; or practise in stages, one day purifying the defilements of nadi and receiving the vase initiation, the next purifying the defilements of prana and receiving the secret empowerment, and the third day purifying the defilements of bindu and receiving the third and fourth empowerments. How you practise is up to you.

Although the main empowerments happen during guru yoga, I strongly encourage you to consider that having purified the physical defilements and obscurations of your nadi, you have also received the vase empowerment; then as the nectar flows through your throat, you receive the secret empowerment; and as it reaches your heart, you receive the wisdom empowerment and the word empowerment. (A fuller presentation of the four abhishekas, or empowerments, appears in chapter 13.)

Finally, imagine that all the dirty nectar either dissolves into the earth, or the emptiness that is shunyata.

#### Dissolution of the Visualisation

Once you have completed the amount of hundred-syllable mantras you want to say, Guru Vajrasattva dissolves into you. Information about the process of dissolution appears in chapter 5, so I will just add here that as it is such a powerful method you might think about effecting the dissolution and then watching the inseparability, over and over again. By doing so, you demonstrate that you are a buddha, and have been from beginningless time. And you don’t have to wait until the end of the practise to dissolve the visualisaton; you can also do it, for example, after each mala of mantras, then reestablish the visualisation before you begin your next mala. It is an excellent method and one I recommend for those who have plenty of time to practise.

At the end of the session, Vajrasattva dissolves inseparably into you one last time. Remain in that state and watch the inseparability.

Then, still as Vajrasattva, if you are doing the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro chant the six-syllable mantra, OM VA JRA SA TVA HUM. In the shorter texts, having been cleansed and purified, you simply rest in the confidence of being Vajrasattva as you chant the mantra. Don’t worry about dissolving this state, it will dissolve itself automatically—if you are very lucky, you might be able to remain there for about half a second.

### Samaya

Most practise texts include a reference to samaya in the confession. For those who practise Vajrasattva there are three general samayas to be observed: do no harm to others; rather, help them; and keep your perception pure, especially your perception of your guru.

What, then, is “samaya”? As practitioners of the vajrayana we put into practise all three vehicles at the same time—the shravakayana, the bodhisattvayana and the vajrayana—and so it might help to know a little about each vehicle’s theory of samaya.

In the shravakayana, samaya is thought of as a fence and is based on the principle of not harming others, which we apply by avoiding killing, lying and stealing. According to this system, if you follow the spiritual path to the mountain peak of enlightenment, your path will always be protected by the fence of samaya, which ensures you are never tempted to risk being sidetracked.

The view of the bodhisattvayana is rather more expansive and oriented to motivation than the shravakayana, and consequently its fences are higher and wider. According to this view, if stealing would somehow be of benefit to others, you would be breaking samaya if you did *not* steal. At the same time, benefiting others is not about becoming some kind of modern-day Robin Hood; genuinely to benefit others is to bring them to enlightenment.

The fences protecting vajrayana students are even more sophisticated, as they are grounded in the development and maintenance of pure perception. There are many samayas in the vajrayana, not least are the “fourteen root samayas,” and they are all very difficult to keep. For example, to judge one girl beautiful and another ugly is to break the “samayas of vajra body,” but if you were able to keep them, you would have completely done away with all dualistic thoughts. The eighth samaya prohibits you from mistreating your body, which is, in essence, the five buddha families; therefore to be contemptuous of your body and treat it badly is to break samaya. I should also add that those who consider themselves worthless, useless beings not fit to take up even the tiniest of spaces in the world are also breaking this samaya.

The seventh root samaya tells you to keep your guru, your practise and all ritual objects secret, which is something modern people find very hard to do. Even the purpose of the most mundane secret, like the date of a teaching or where it will be held, is so grossly misunderstood that those not in on it feel jealous and excluded. On a more subtle level, when a tantric master tells a student to keep an instruction secret, the student often fails to realise that by doing so, their own practise is more likely to grow and mature, but if they babble about it to all and sundry, they are likely to attract the kind of ill-informed feedback that opens the door to obstacles.

The pratimoksha vow is like a clay pot; once broken, it is difficult to mend. Therefore, if a shravaka monk who has not given back his vows sleeps with a woman to satisfy his lust, he will not be allowed to renew his monk’s vow in this lifetime. However, the bodhisattvayana and vajrayana vows are like golden pots. Although beginner vajrayana practitioners will, without doubt, break these vows and samayas on a daily basis, with a little care and attention they are easily restored (for example by reciting the hundred-syllable mantra) and, like a golden pot mended by a master goldsmith, may even end up being more beautiful.

Guru Rinpoche said that the view of a vajrayana practitioner should be as vast as the sky, and his actions as refined as grains of flour. But how can we keep all these rules, in spite of the contradictions? The best we can do is to try to obey the pratimoksha and bodhisattvayana rules, and at least make the effort to understand those of the vajrayana. For beginners, to keep all the vajrayana vows and samayas is virtually impossible, so focus on trying to do no harm, help wherever you can and aspire to keep samaya.

## 10. Mandala Offering

Having cleansed ourselves with Vajrasattva practise, we must now equip ourselves with all the tools necessary for accumulating merit; we do this by offering mandalas.

### Merit

What is “merit”? From one perspective, merit is “ability”; all the abilities, in fact, that make it possible for us to hear, contemplate, meditate on and practise the dharma, including the ability to feel curious. These days our merit is so limited that the vast majority of human beings are unable to recognize how special dharma is. Not only do we lack the merit to practise the dharma, we have so little that we can’t even enjoy samsaric life.

How we interpret information and our experiences of the world depends entirely on how much merit we have accumulated. For example, what does the word *impermanent* mean? On the grossest possible level, those who have very little merit believe that “impermanent” means decay and death, or the changing seasons. Once we start to accumulate merit, though, our understanding becomes more subtle. Imagine you are experiencing a moment of happiness. If you have a little merit you will be able, to a certain degree, to interpret and understand “impermanence” and watch your mood change from unhappiness to happiness and back again, making any disappointment or hope you might feel less intense.

One way of accumulating merit is to make offerings of food, money, flowers, music and so on. However, most modern people prefer to spend money on “useful” things and avoid wasting it. So it is easy to understand how offering butter lamps and large quantities of expensive food and drink to tsoks can appear wasteful.

Nyoshul Khen Rinpoche told me a story about a French woman who asked him to give her a mahasandhi teaching and introduce her to the nature of the mind. It was quite a casual request, and probably spur-of-the-moment, but since it inspired Khen Rinpoche to discover that there are still people in these degenerate times with an interest in such profound teachings, he agreed. As mahasandhi is an extremely precious teaching, students need to accumulate a great deal of merit in order to receive, listen to and hear it properly. Therefore, as a way of acknowledging her need for merit, Nyoshul Khen Rinpoche suggested she make a tsok offering. Delighted by the idea, the woman accompanied Khenpo on a shopping trip.

Paris is a wonderful place to shop and within minutes she had bought some chocolate that cost the equivalent of about one euro. She showed Khenpo the chocolate and asked him if that would be enough to offer in the tsok. Now, that’s a difficult question to answer. The dharma, and particularly mahasandhi teachings, are beyond price—even three thousand world-systems and everything contained within them are nowhere near enough to offer for a single word of mahasandhi. So what could Khenpo say about a bar of chocolate? But thinking that perhaps she didn’t have much money, Khenpo said nothing and they continued shopping. Before long they found themselves at the perfume counter of a chic Paris department store and, to Khenpo’s surprise, the woman did not hesitate to spend the equivalent of ten euros on a bottle of her favorite perfume, which she kept for herself.

Modern people lack the merit to be able both to listen to and hear what is really being said and often during dharma teachings that we have received many times before, miss the point of what is being taught. We are unable to listen intently, and at times do not listen at all, because mind is so easily distracted. Even if we do listen, we cannot truly hear, interpret or consider what we have learnt from any other angle. As a result, we take the scraps of information that almost by accident lodge in our sieve-like minds extremely seriously.

Sadly, we no longer have enough merit to be able to meet or receive teachings from great masters. However, even if today’s objects of devotion fail to measure up to the great masters of the past, if we could only increase our store of merit, it would not matter. Patrul Rinpoche illustrates how this works in his story of the dog’s tooth. An old Tibetan woman asked her son to bring her a relic of the Buddha as an object of devotion for her practise. Unfortunately the son forgets his promise, and is forced to pass off the tooth of a dead dog he finds in a gutter as a Buddha’s tooth to avoid disappointing her. However, her devotion for the tooth and the merit it generated were so great that before long the dog’s tooth was transformed into a genuine Buddha’s tooth from which relics (ringsel) miraculously spring.

You can test and experience the power of merit for yourself. Read a dharma text once, then make some mandala offerings as thoroughly and with as much concentration and determination as you can muster. Now read the same text again. You will find that your understanding has been transformed. Rather than skimming the gist of each chapter, you will be able to interpret each word far more precisely than you could before. You should also try receiving teachings from a teacher who had previously bored you; having made some mandala offerings, your experience will be entirely different. And if, before accumulating mandala offerings you are unable to allow your mind to settle and become frustrated by how far it wanders, try doing shamatha meditation. Again, your experience of practise will be transformed.

To have the merit to receive teachings is one thing, but to have enough to be able to listen to and hear them correctly is quite another, which is why the tradition of offering a mandala at the beginning of a dharma teaching was originally established. And merit is not only necessary for us to be able to understand the dharma, but for every other step on the path, including to be able truly to appreciate our guru. With enough merit, you will be able to interpret the way the guru looks when he is ill, or when he yawns, or when he appears to lose his temper, in a way that is beneficial. And isn’t that what you want? If you lack merit, even if your guru behaves like an angel you will find reasons for criticizing him and end up failing to benefit from your connection.

#### How Merit Is Generated

As we have already seen in the section on taking refuge, there are two different fields through which we can accumulate merit, and one of the unique features of Buddhadharma is that both are equally important.

Merit is generated by the actions or conditions that quickly bring us closer to the truth, not merely through good, wholesome actions or activities aimed at temporary worldly achievement, like a long life or higher rebirth. Of course, one of the ways it works is that if we are materially generous, we will also receive an abundance of wealth, and if we are patient, we will be physically attractive to others. But strictly speaking, if the result of a conventionally good activity is not dedicated towards the eventual enlightenment of all sentient beings, and if it has not brought you any closer to the truth, while the good karma it generates may feel great, you will not accumulate the kind of merit coveted by those who follow the path to enlightenment. A split second spent on doing something beneficial that also brings you closer to the truth, however unpleasant or unfashionable, is far more valuable than engaging in an extravaganza of more enjoyable and ostentatiously helpful activities that merely result in a higher rebirth, or other worldly boons.

In other words, the amount and quality of the merit you accumulate is not determined by the method of accumulation. According to the bodhisattvayana, whether an action accumulates merit or just good karma is determined by whether or not it is undertaken with a dualistic view. For example, offering a kilo of gold with a dualistic view will not necessarily accumulate more merit than offering a grape. As Chandrakirti said, giving without reference to the one who gives, the gift, or the recipient of that gift is described as a paramita that “transcends the world.” And to give without the slightest attachment to giving, gift and recipient is said to be the “perfect action of the worldly ones.”

As for the methods available for accumulating merit, there are many. For example, be generous, disciplined and patient; make offerings, both material and visualised; confess; rejoice at other people’s good fortune; and arouse love, compassion and bodhichitta. Taking refuge is also an important method. As is requesting sublime beings to turn the wheel of the dharma and asking them to live for a long time, which purifies the karmic consequences of having created obstacles for those receiving teachings in past lives.

The mahayana offers a wonderful combination of wisdom and skilful methods that are easy to execute, yet bring about remarkable results. You might imagine that in order to accumulate infinite merit, you will have to make tremendous sacrifices entirely beyond your capacity, such as offering your own flesh and bones, or your home; but material offerings are not the only kind of offerings we can make. If they were, there would be something wrong with the system, because most of us are unable to afford to make such extravagant gestures. Fortunately, the mahayana path has the wisdom and skill to accommodate *all* practitioners, not just the wealthy, and offers methods that involve visualizing offering substances that accumulate exactly the same amount of merit as the equivalent material offerings would have done. To put it another way, the mahayana path is easy, blissful and painless, yet its methods reap the same amount of merit and wisdom as material offerings.

#### The Seven-Branch Offering

An example of one of these extraordinary methods is the seven-branch offering. It involves seven different ways of accumulating merit, each of which serves a specific purpose. The offerings are as follows: prostration, offering, confession, rejoicing, requesting the turning of the wheel of the dharma, asking buddhas not to pass into parinirvana and dedication of merit. (You can choose to recite any of the seven-branch prayers from the sutras and Buddhist practises and consult the root texts and commentaries of your tradition to find out where in the ngöndro practise it appears.)

**Prostration**

We do prostrations to crush one of our most stubborn shells, pride. A person bloated with pride will have no space left in them for enlightened qualities to grow, and without enlightened qualities the activities of a bodhisattva will be impeded. Besides which, pride is by nature insecure and precipitates many levels of hypocrisy.

Imagine your body has been multiplied exponentially and that trillions and trillions of “you” are prostrating before the objects of refuge. The Buddha said that you and each of your imagined prostrations will accumulate exactly the same amount of merit.

**Offering**

The antidote to miserliness is to make offerings. Miserliness is rooted in an attitude of poverty that has nothing to do with being materially poor; there are many people in this world who are loaded with material wealth yet constantly feel they lack something. One of the side effects of miserliness is pettiness, and a petty person will never develop the qualities that make it easy and pleasant for others to be around them.

There is no restriction on the amount of offerings you can make—the sky’s the limit! And although “limitless offerings” sounds, to our ears, as though we should pile up mountains of sumptuous material goods, offerings can be “limitless” even when they are just a few grains of rice, as we can see from the story of a little boy called Chandragomi.

Chandragomi’s family was so poor that his parents, brothers and sisters were all forced to beg for food to avoid starvation. One day, as Chandragomi went about his business, he noticed a statue of Avalokiteshvara on a shrine by the roadside. He was so attracted by the statue’s compassionate expression that he sprinkled a few grains of the rice he had managed to collect that morning onto the statue’s hand. To his surprise, even though he made his offering very carefully, the grains of rice slid off the statue’s hand and into the dust. He offered a few more grains and once again they tumbled to the ground. Chandragomi began to worry that for some reason Avalokiteshvara did not want to accept his offering, and kept digging deeper and deeper into his pockets to find more rice to offer, until finally he had nothing left. By now Chandragomi was quite upset and with tears spilling down his cheeks said to Avalokiteshvara, with great regret, “But now I have nothing left to offer.” At that moment the power of the boy’s one-pointed trust that Avalokiteshvara was actually there in front of him brought the statue to life and Avalokiteshvara gave him an enormous hug.

Obviously we should all try to make material offerings, but perhaps for beginners, imaginary offerings are less risky. When Atisha Dipamkara went to Tibet, he advised the Tibetans to make water offerings (which is why so many bowls of water are offered in Tibetan Buddhist temples) because offerings should be made completely and wholeheartedly, with no sense of regret or concern about who receives them or how they are used. And for a beginner this is far easier to do successfully with water.

Visualise in your mind’s eye mountains of all the traditional offering substances, as well as anything else you can think of that is beautiful or expensive or desirable or extraordinary, for example Niagara Falls in Canada, or the Forbidden City in Beijing, an elegantly seductive geisha dancer in Kyoto, or a burly, uniformed U.S. Marine in California. Really let your imagination fly. And don’t limit your offerings to what is desirable in your own culture.

**Confession**

Next, in order to dismantle ego’s hideout, confess and expose your negative actions. Confession is one of the most effective ways of countering aggression. To stow your faults away in some deep, dark hidden place is like being seriously ill but not telling the doctor where it hurts, and to withhold such vital information will prevent the doctor from diagnosing your illness correctly, if at all. Don’t worry about what you might have forgotten, or how a negative action is defined from a Buddhist perspective, just do your best. Imagine you are in the presence of all the buddhas and bodhisattvas who already know what happened in the past, what will happen in the future, and what is happening in the present, then expose everything you regret and are ashamed of having thought and done, as well as the deplorable thoughts and actions you might be party to in the future, without leaving anything out.

**Rejoice**

A very effective and easy method for accumulating merit is to rejoice at the good fortune of others. Ironically, though, most of us find it agonizingly difficult.

There are three main causes for rejoicing.

- Rejoice in the happiness and causes of happiness of others; for example, when you see an attractive person, instead of feeling envy, rejoice in their beauty.
- When you see someone do good, rejoice rather than stooping to criticize them out of spite and jealousy.
- Best of all, rather than indulging in doubts about the possibility of enlightenment or whether or not buddhahood even exists, rejoice in it and its causes; and rejoice when other people engage in the causes of enlightenment, like hearing and contemplating the teachings and the practise of meditation.

To rejoice at someone else’s success is perhaps the easiest way of accumulating merit—it’s as if a huge stockpile of merit is just waiting around for you to gather up. When you see someone do something worthwhile, all you have to do to accumulate oceans of merit is rejoice. It’s a powerful antidote to the most ridiculous and pathetic emotional response we suffer from—jealousy. Instead of indulging your jealousy, rejoice when you see someone beautiful or successful, and remember that both qualities are the result of many lifetimes’ practise of patience and generosity. Think of the good qualities of everyone you know, and rejoice in the beneficial things they do, from the people who run hospitals to those able to create wonderful flower arrangements. Also rejoice in the results they enjoy, from fame and admiration to good looks. To rejoice in the activities of the buddhas and bodhisattvas is particularly powerful.

**Request the Turning of the Wheel of Dharma**

We live in degenerate times, and one of the most powerful antidotes both to degeneration and ignorance (the root of all our problems) is to request that the buddhas and bodhisattvas teach us, or “turn the wheel of the dharma.”

The Buddha passed into parinirvana two and half millennia ago, and so you might wonder what point there is in continually asking him to teach us. In everyday life, whenever we have a problem, the first thing most of us do is ask someone we respect and trust for help. In this case, the all-pervading problem we face in samsara is fundamental ignorance, and so we need to ask someone who knows how to dispel that ignorance. “But why and how do we ask the Buddha?” is most people’s immediate reaction. “He isn’t even alive! Will we have to wait for the next buddha to appear before we get our answer?” Unfortunately, such questions miss the point entirely.

To request the wheel of dharma to be turned is not just to ask to receive teachings in conventional ways; turning the wheel can take a great many forms. For example, it could turn while you are doing something very ordinary, such as watching an episode of your favourite soap opera, or reading a passage in a book, or watching a dead leaf fall from a tree, because anything that provides the spark to ignite your compassion and realisation of the futility of this life is a “turning of the wheel.” As we have already seen, time and again, dharma students have been baffled by sacred texts that, at first glance, they are unable to understand; then later, having accumulated a little merit, they understand them quite easily. This is one of the ways the buddhas and bodhisattvas turn the wheel of the dharma.

The Buddha said that whenever any one of us feels devotion, he will be right there with us. He is therefore turning the wheel of the dharma continuously, and such teachings can never be stopped.

**Request That the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas Do Not Pass into Parinirvana**

To request that the buddhas and bodhisattvas remain in samsara and do not pass into parinirvana is how we counter wrong view and doubt. The minds of samsaric beings are dualistic, and dualistic minds are, by nature, full of doubt, which in turn breeds wrong view. Spiritual seekers are no different. We spend an inordinate amount of time struggling with doubts about our practise, our path and the spiritual way of life—it is probably the toughest challenge most of us have to face and the one emotion that will accompany us until the very end of our spiritual journey. It is believed that doubts become sharper the more our intelligence increases, which makes perfect sense since the more intelligent we are, the more intelligent our doubts seem to become. That we have so many doubts in the first place is one of our biggest obstacles, because apart from anything else, they take up so much of our time. The worse it gets, the more likely we are to find ourselves stuck in a state of serial self-condemnation, which in turn diverts our attention from the right view, causing loss of faith in the laws of cause, condition and effect (karma), and corroding our belief in the ultimate truth of interdependent reality, shunyata and the triple gem.

Doubts invade the mind in a variety of ways. You may wonder why, having completed hundreds of dharma practises, your health is so poor. Or, if the buddhas really are omniscient, what is stopping them from eliminating the AIDS virus, or poverty, or the illegal drug trade? Do they have any power at all? Is there really a “next life”? Do any of us truly have buddhanature? Many such doubts can be cleared away simply by listening to the teachings, but most human beings also need some kind of inspiration that has nothing to do with reasoning or logic to give them confidence, like an extraordinary experience or a meeting an inspiring person. And since “seeing is believing,” it is usually meeting a Buddhist master—the physical manifestation of the Buddha’s body, speech and mind, and a person we can see, touch and hear—that provides the most profound kind of inspiration.

We are not only asking fully fledged buddhas with all thirty-two major and eighty minor marks to remain in samsara, we are also asking those who embody all the great qualities of the buddhas, bodhisattvas and the teachings, the source of inspiration for others, and for all the events and activities, however illogical or impractical, that uplift and encourage us, to continue their activities.

**Dedication**

Finally, we must always dedicate our practise and positive actions quickly so we waste none of the merit we accumulate. By dedicating our merit towards the ultimate happiness and enlightenment of all sentient beings, we not only keep that merit safe, we ensure that it will continue to accumulate—like interest on wisely invested money—so that positive actions become our path to enlightenment. If we do not dedicate immediately, the merit could be burnt away in a sudden flash of anger, or any of the other extreme negative actions or thoughts to which we are prey.

#### Merit: the Armour That Shields Us from Obstacles

The dharma is said to be absolutely priceless, and like a diamond, can cut through all kinds of dualistic nets. We, on the other hand, are more like destitute, ignorant beggars who lack any sense of enterprise, and when a precious diamond suddenly falls into our hands, we have no idea what to do with it. We are so ignorant of its true worth that we might trade it for a hotdog, or even carelessly lose it. Such situations arise surprisingly often because wherever the precious dharma is to be found, there will always be obstacles. Actually, when dharma practitioners attract obstacles, it is a sign that the practise is having an effect—if it wasn’t, why would the obstacles bother? They have no need to attack people whose practise is not going well because those people are probably creating quite enough problems for themselves.

That being said, many of the obstacles we face are strong and disturbing, which is why we try to develop the ability to manipulate, cheat, seduce, transform and ignore all the obstacles we attract by putting into practise many of the methods offered by the Buddhadharma. The “highest” of these methods is to welcome obstacles as blessings, but genuinely to do so requires a great deal of merit. Fortunately, Buddhadharma also provides us with the practise of mandala offering, which is the most profound of all merit-making methods.

### The Practise

Karma Chagme said, when beginners like us build a temple, it is impossible to avoid some form of unvirtuous thought or action from arising in our minds. Throughout the process, we will face many challenges, and once it is complete, we are bound to have become attached and miserly in one way or another. However, the practise of offering mandalas does not have the same effect at all, and this is why it is so important.

#### What Do We Offer?

As far as the recipients of our offerings are concerned, the kinds of offerings we make are immaterial, so we should offer whatever we ourselves consider to be precious.

For example, by offering our body to the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the past, we create an auspicious link with the body, speech, mind, quality and activity of Shakyamuni Buddha. By offering our body to the present Buddha—our guru—our body, speech and mind become a perfect vessel for wisdom. By offering our body to a future buddha, we perfect the auspicious circumstances that will enable us to benefit all sentient beings.

During the mandala offering we primarily use sublime beings as our field of merit. As for what precisely you should offer, be as creative and imaginative as you can. If you find Mount Meru and its surrounding planets difficult to picture in your mind, just offer a huge and beautiful mountain. Or imagine Asia, South America, North America and Australia and picture all the beautiful national parks, palaces, waterfalls and wealth they contain.

If offering things that do not belong to you makes you uncomfortable, always remember that the way you perceive your world is wholly and completely your own, never anyone else’s. Include in your visualisation sources of wealth, such as Deutsche Bank, forests, iron ore, steel, all the material wealth human beings love to accumulate and the wealth of gods (which is usually represented as flying carpets and parasols, etc.). Add to it the wealth of the nagas (which sometimes appears in the form of the conch that can wage war), or a small shell that can transform itself into a kingdom or a palace, or wish-fulfilling trees and cows, or even beautiful men and women. You can elaborate as much as you like, and always endeavour to go beyond the limits of your imagination. To enhance your visualisation, make use of different substances that you consider precious, like the rice that Tibetans use a great deal in their offerings, and coins and precious metals like silver and gold.

If your intention is to do this practise as part of your ngöndro accumulations, you will need a mandala plate and as many ritual offering substances as you can gather. According to Deshung Rinpoche, the mandala plate can be quite large, but if you prefer to use a small one, please do. What the plate is made of will depend on what you can afford; it could be stone, sandalwood, copper, iron, steel or even gold if you wish. Your main ritual offering, though, will be grains of all kinds, not just rice, and whatever else you add will serve as objects of concentration to enhance your practise. Visualise each grain as being everything mentioned above, and if, like me, Indian-style representations of wealth inspire you, you could also include things like beautiful bathhouses full of lovely dancing girls and, to be politically correct, handsome dancing boys.

Although the practise texts usually make it sound as though we make these offerings to please the buddhas and bodhisattvas, do keep in mind that this is absolutely not the case. The Buddha, dharma and sangha cannot be bribed, bought, influenced or even pleased by our meager offerings. If you think about it, the kind of offerings traditionally made were not designed to be useful to the buddhas and bodhisattvas in the ordinary sense of the word. In fact, their “usefulness” is quite beside point. Take the example of a precious cow. How useful would a cow be to someone living in the middle of New York?

#### Practical Tips About the Offering Substances

If you can take a few weeks off, you might like to do an intensive retreat to accumulate all one hundred thousand mandala offerings in one go. In that case, you will need two mandala offering plates, one to place on the shrine with five piles of offerings on it to represent the five buddha families, and the other on which to make your offerings during the practise. Since the mandala offering on your shrine will remain there for a relatively long time, if you mix the rice with butter, which is a traditional method for making the rice grains stick together, the offering will quickly go rancid. So instead, use offerings that will not rot, like semiprecious stones.

In terms of the mandala offerings you make daily, from time to time it is a good idea to renew them by replacing the old rice with new rice, and so on. The amount of offerings you make and how often you change them will depend on the size of your budget. And of course, if you go into retreat for a long time, say a year, you should change the offerings regularly. Once you have finished the accumulation, the unwritten tradition is to offer the precious materials you used to your teacher, or to a temple, or, if you prefer, quietly scatter them in a forest where no one will thank you.

Each day, before you start to practise, first clean the mandala plate with either saffron water or rose water, or some kind of perfumed water. Then, at the beginning of each session, use the inside of your wrist to rub the surface clean. If you want to elaborate the practise a little, imagine you are purifying and cleansing all the offerings you will be making, which of course include your body, speech and mind. You will hear many small details like this; almost every Tibetan or dharma practitioner you meet will come up with a slightly different variation, and from their point of view, each of them is probably right. After all, there are four lineages in Tibetan Buddhism that thousands of people practise according to the teachings of hundreds of lamas, many of whom have modified the traditional instructions. Ultimately, though, there is not one correct way of cleaning the mandala plate, so it’s best not to waste too much time on it.

#### The Visualisation

In this case, you will be making offerings to Guru Rinpoche or Vajradhara visualised in front of you and surrounded by his retinue. (A further elaboration of this visualisation can be found in descriptions of the field of merit that are usually included in the refuge section of longer ngöndro texts.) The main deity of the mandala sits on the central branch of a wish-fulfilling tree; on the branch in front of him sits Shakyamuni Buddha and all the buddhas; on the branch to his right all the bodhisattvas; to his left the noble sangha of shravakas and pratyekabuddhas; and behind him are huge stacks of dharma texts.

During the practise of taking refuge we describe the sublime beings in this visualisation as our “objects of refuge”; during the practise of arousing bodhichitta, they become those who witness our vow of bodhichitta; and now, as we offer the mandala, they become the recipients of our offerings.

In the context of this practise, the rose water or perfumed water you use to clean the mandala plate represents the application of bodhichitta, which means you are doing the practise for the sake of the enlightenment of all sentient beings. By sprinkling a little water on the plate you apply the “moisture of bodhichitta” to symbolize the profundity of this vajrayana practise and take it beyond being a mundane act of sprinkling rice onto a plate. The water also helps the rice to stick to the plate, and although you might not want to use water every time you clean the plate, it is a good idea to use it right at the beginning.

You are now ready to offer a mandala.

The sun and the moon are the sixth and the seventh piles, respectively, and since the sun must rise on the earth, the sixth pile should therefore be near to it.

If you like, you can add an eighth pile to represent the embodiment of all the wealth of the gods and humans.

Usually you would hold the mandala plate and mala in your left hand and place the piles of rice with your right hand, but if you are left-handed and prefer to do it the other way around, that’s fine.

Having made seven or eight piles of rice and precious materials on the mandala plate, sweep them off with your right hand. This counts as one mandala offering. All you have to do now is complete another ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine, plus an additional 10 percent to make up for any mistakes or inattentiveness, and you will have accomplished the practise. It’s that easy!

As you make each offering, repeat the mandala offering prayer from whichever ngöndro you practise, either once or three times.

The fact that you offer piles of rice and precious materials is also a product of your own perception, and as such is extremely limited. So offer *absolutely everything,* including everything you consider to be negative, like your own turbulent emotions, the essence of which is none other than wisdom.

Mentally offering various precious substances works extremely well, because when you think of flowers, for example, it is *your* mind that creates *your* flower. In fact, there is nothing that is not a product of your own perception. This universe, its atmosphere, stars, moons, planets and everything in it are created by mind, and while mind exists, so do the myriad distinctions that effectively created the universe.

The concept of offering, according to the Buddhadharma, is based on the view of dependent arising. In fact, all Buddhist practise and theory is based on this same view, and so for those who aspire either to practise the path related to the nature of mind or to complete any one of the many practises available for accumulating merit, it is a very good idea to make light offerings. Since the activity of “offering” is closely connected with that of creating auspicious connections, to offer a butter lamp will enhance your understanding of the nature of mind: in the same way a butter lamp sheds light that illuminates itself and everything around it, mind both knows itself and all that it encounters. The function of the lamp is therefore similar to that of mind and can be described as an auspicious “substance” through which the auspicious connection to the nature of mind can be created.

Everything is created by mind. The nature of the mind is dharmakaya, sambogakaya and nirmanakaya, and its realisation is what we call “enlightenment.” But to attain enlightenment is not an easy task, as we are constantly deceived by an infinite number of habitual patterns. We therefore need all kinds of methods and skills to counter those old habits, and to pile up a paltry substance like rice, again and again, is just one of those skilful methods.

## 11. Kusali Practise

Kusali practise is not included in many ngöndro traditions, but you will find it in the long version of the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro. It’s not a bad idea for those following the shorter version to practise it too, because it is an excellent method for accumulating merit. Kusali practise is recommended for practitioners who are poor and cannot afford mandala plates, offerings or any of the necessary special offering substances and as Patrul Rinpoche points out, *kusali* literally means “beggar.” Yet, from the dharma point of view, it ranks amongst the highest teachings.

### Visualisation: Victory over the Four Maras

Kusali practise is aimed particularly at destroying the four maras: the mara of the sons of gods; the mara of death; the mara of defilements (meaning all emotions); and the mara of the aggregates. Khenpo Ngakchung said that not to understand that all that appears is nothing more than the projection of your own mind is the mara of the sons of gods; this body, which is a compounded phenomenon and constantly changing, unstoppable by nature and an obscuration, is the mara of the aggregates; attachment, craving and fixation are the mara of defilement; and the aggregate that includes birth and death is the mara of death.

Start the practise by saying the syllable PHAT and visualise the total separation of body and mind by ejecting your consciousness from your body, leaving it behind like an old sack lying in a heap on the floor. The instant your body falls into a heap, you hover above it, a formless being of pure consciousness, and see that your precious body is now merely a corpse.

Generally speaking, pride arises in response to two factors, mind and body, and so if you have a body, you will also feel pride. Therefore, by destroying the body you also destroy your pride, which is a victory over the mara of defilements; and the moment you feel no attachment to your body, you will have destroyed the mara of the sons of gods. However, unlike the flame of a snuffed-out candle, although the body has been dismantled and destroyed, mind remains conscious and continues to be aware, which means you claim victory over the mara of death. This third victory is what we call “Krodhikali,” and hers is the form you adopt once you have destroyed your body. She is black, beautiful and adorned with all the ornaments of a dakini, including crowns, bone bracelets and a tiger-skin skirt. She also brandishes a curved knife to symbolize the destruction of the mara of defilements (emotions).

It is Krodhikali who must destroy the mara of the aggregates by slowly cutting the skull, your skull, away from your inanimate body. We now call the skull, complete with hair, a *kapala,* or “skull cup,” and as soon as you have visualised the skull cup in front of you, imagine it increases in size until it is as enormous as the three worlds.

Instantly, a tripod of three human skulls (representing the three kayas) appears and you place the kapala on top of them. Then return to your corpse and cut off its hands and fingers; peel off its skin; remove the lungs, liver and all the organs; and drain out all the blood. Continue dismembering your body, not forgetting to cut off your lips and nose, and prise out both eyes with your curved knife along with each one of your teeth, and place each body part, one by one, into the kapala.

Now an extremely intense wisdom fire blazes from the three skulls. As you chant OM AH HUM, imagine your body parts melt and boil like a rich stew bubbling on the stove. Gradually, this body-part stew becomes a powerful wish-fulfilling nectar, each drop containing everything that gives you pleasure, such as lakes, gardens, food, drink and so on. Anything that you, as the object of offering, wish for, is precisely what the nectar will become, and this is what you offer.

#### The Guests and Their Offerings

If you would like to elaborate this visualisation, imagine it’s a banquet. The first invitations go to all those who have destroyed ego, including everyone from arhats right up to the Buddha, borne on brilliant rays of rainbow light that shoot out to the Copper-Coloured Mountain, the realms of Amitabha and Akshobhya and all the other buddha realms, as well as to all the bodhisattvas. Next, you send out invitations to the six realms, and your guests arrive quickly and take their seats.

The first portion of the banquet is offered to the sublime ones. In return you receive all the common attainments, including long life and freedom from obstacles and sickness, and so Kusali practise is particularly good for those suffering from serious health or financial problems that are the result of karmic debts from previous lives. You also receive the uncommon attainments, which are rooted in the ability to feel love and compassion for others, be diligent, have devotion and recognize the nature of your mind.

Next, you serve the guests from the six realms. With every sip of wine, mouthful of asparagus, lick of ice cream, chew of steak, swallow of crème brûlée and suck of lobster, visualise their sufferings disappearing, and love, compassion, bodhichitta and devotion nourishing their being so they in turn will be able to help other sentient beings.

Two groups of guests are special. One includes those who bother you or who you think of as enemies, and as they receive your offering, they consider all scores to have been settled. The other group is the biggest because as it is made up of all those with whom we have karmic debts. There are many different kinds of karmic debt, for example, the karmic debt we owe to our fathers and mothers, our friends and those who have provided us with food, shelter and protection; or the debt we owe those whose space we have occupied, or who have served us. And each of us owes such debts to an immense amount of people.

#### Dissolution

As the banquet draws to a close, you realize that the whole thing has been a creation of your own mind. Everyone dissolves into you, host, guests and feast become inseparable, and you remain in that state of inseparability for as long as you can.

## 12. Guru Yoga

Vajrayana practise is about transforming perception, and we begin to effect that transformation during ngöndro practise, in particular in guru yoga. This is why almost all ngöndro traditions advise that we start the practise by transforming our ordinary, impure environment into a pure realm, and our ordinary, impure form into a pure being.

If you practise the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro guru yoga, you visualise yourself as Vajrayogini. Why Vajrayogini? In between first stepping onto the spiritual path and its culmination, there will be a point when you realise the nature of mind, and having done so, become a vessel for gathering your guru’s blessings. As a way of invoking those blessings more effectively, and to summon interdependent auspiciousness, you visualise yourself in the sublime form of the female deity, Vajrayogini.

(The details of the following visualisation are based on the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro and will differ depending on the ngöndro tradition you follow.)

### The Visualisation

Visualise yourself as Vajrayogini. You are red in colour, your right hand wields a curved knife and your left holds a kapala. You are semiwrathful, beautiful and slender, adorned with exquisite jewels, a tiger-skin skirt and bone ornaments. Your three eyes gaze into the sky at a hundred-thousand petaled lotus, in the centre of which lies a horizontal sun disc with a moon disc lying on top of it.

Seated on the moon disc is your root guru, the embodiment of all sources of refuge and essence of all the buddhas, in the form of Padmakara, the Lotus-born. He does not look like an ordinary person and displays no signs of aging or frailty. On the contrary, he is young, majestic, vibrant and adorned with royal regalia.

Guru Rinpoche, your guru, sits in a large sphere of rainbow light encircled by his twenty-five disciples led by King Trison Deutsen, and surrounded by millions more spheres of rainbow light. In the space above the guru sit all the mahasiddhas of the mahasandhi (or mahamudra) lineage—Vimalamitra, Jnanasutra, Shri Singha and Prahevajra (Garab Dorje), and so on—and the spaces in between are filled with deities of different lineages, dakinis and dharmapalas. So, all of space, especially that in front of you, is teeming with an immense cloud of the objects of refuge.

As you look at your objects of refuge, try to visualise them as being vibrantly alive, always remembering that, like the reflection of a full moon in clean, still water, they do not truly exist. However brilliantly clear the moon may appear to be on the surface of the water, it is still just a reflection, and this is how the gathering of the objects of refuge for this guru yoga should appear as they sit before you.

If you are doing a mahamudra ngöndro, or a Sakya or Geluk ngöndro, you will most likely visualise your guru as Vajradhara surrounded by all the lineage masters associated with the practise you are doing. The deities included may vary, for example in mahamudra ngöndros, deities like Chakrasamvara are prominent, while in the Sakya or Geluk ngöndros the featured deities are Chakrasamvara, Guhyasamaja and Kalachakra.

#### The Mantra Visualisation

With a heart overflowing with devotion, invoke your guru by reciting the seven-line prayer to Guru Rinpoche, followed by the vajra guru mantra.

As you say a prayer of invocation, concentrate on the mandala you have visualised and particularly on your guru. Gaze into his face for as long as you can. The masters of the past warn how easy it is to get bored during a mantra visualisation and advise that once you weary of looking at your guru’s face, shift your focus to his whole form, or his to activity, or the light radiating from him, or your devotion for him, or the sound of the mantra, or the entourage of deities that surround him. Then alternate your focus, over and over again.

Students often ask if they should only invoke the guru in the context of a formal daily practise, or if it can be done anywhere. The answer is that it depends on the student. Dharma bums who roam the streets of Kathmandu smoking hashish and sitting in cafés nursing a half-empty cup of cappuccino for most of the day should probably sit formally and recite ten million or one hundred million mantras. Whereas those who have demanding jobs in London, New York or Paris might benefit more from reciting the mantra on their way to work, or as they wait for a bus. The method each student is given will depend entirely on their personal situation and how disciplined they are.

### The Heart of Guru Yoga

Guru yoga, generally speaking, helps us develop and increase devotion, but the very heart of the practise is to mix our mind with the mind of the guru. Actually, this “mixing” process is not limited to mind, it encompasses our entire being—body, speech and mind. We should therefore try to mix every part of ourselves with our guru, from our identity, physical form and the sounds we make, right down to our senses of smell and taste.

Once again, terminology and language can be misleading. In this case, “mix” automatically implies that two separate entities exist and that it is possible to mix them together. But as Samantabhadra tells us, the essence of mind is by nature empty, as is the essence of all phenomena, and although from the perspective of the vastness of phenomenal existence it may look as though there are two separate entities to be mixed, since the outer guru is none other than a reflection of your devotion, this “mixing” process is rather more profound than the mere combination of two ingredients. You are, in fact, establishing the understanding that there never was a separate entity—a guru—with whom to mix your mind. In other words, your mind and your guru’s have never been separate.

#### The Outer Guru

As we have already seen, the outer, inner and secret gurus are none other than the nature of our mind. However, beginners like us look for a model to be inspired and guided by, in whom we can take refuge and to whom we can express our appreciation of the path of dharma by making offerings. Many of us feel the need to find someone, anyone, to whom we can describe all our emotional ups and downs using the only methods of communication available to us—whining, complaining, begging and praying. And, to satisfy that need, the path—which from the point of view of the vajrayana is “confusion”—provides us with an outer guru.

Eventually, of course, all paths will have to be discarded, but in the meantime you should continue to offer devotion to your outer guru, along with your admiration and unwavering pure perception. Then gradually, step by step, try to mingle your being with your inner and secret gurus, and in time you will realize that your outer, inner and secret gurus have never been separate from you.

#### Longing to Feel Connected with Your Guru

Many students like to talk about how they feel when they think of their guru. Some sense a fullness in their hearts and various sensations in their chests, as well as the feeling that they are loved and therefore complete. I think what these students are trying to express is their longing to feel connected with their guru. It’s quite true, strong feelings and sensations can arise when we think of him, but it’s also important to remember how fickle feelings and emotions are. Feelings belong to the temporal world and as such are just another layer that will eventually have to be peeled away.

An emotional connection with a person, our guru, is not the only outcome we should work towards on our spiritual path. So far, our guru has appeared to be separate from us, yet as we progress along the path, one of its skilful methods is to remind us continually that this is absolutely not the case. Not one of the teachings we receive ever, even for one millisecond, suggests that the guru is a separate entity. The idea that there are a “guru” and a “student” is a mistake we have made entirely on our own and then reinforced with habit. And if we had successfully convinced ourselves that our guru was and always had been inseparable from us, guru yoga would be entirely redundant. To cling to the idea that we have a connection with him in spite of the fact that to feel connected implies he is a separate entity is the very concept that binds us to duality.

The recognition that our guru is inseparable from us is the only realisation we will ever have that cannot and will not be peeled away.

### Practical Advice

#### Mixing Your Mind with Your Guru’s

I strongly recommend that you unite your mind with the mind of the guru again and again. Visualise Guru Rinpoche as you chant the vajra guru mantra, then, after every hundred mantras, dissolve Guru Rinpoche into you. The more often you go through this process, the better, so try effecting the dissolution every ten or twenty mantras, then just watch the inseparability. Needless to say, the experience will not leave your life unchanged.

The key to watching mind is to mix your mind with the guru’s, and it is a practise you can do in all sorts of ordinary situations. As you stand on an escalator on your way to catching a train, for example, or in a department store, or at a cinema, try mixing your minds together until you get off. It takes no time at all, and afterwards it is very easy to return to whatever it was you were doing, whether it was shopping or going to a movie or to a football match.

So, take a few moments every day to mix your mind with your guru’s and then watch the inseparability for a few moments. If you do, one of the blessings of the practise is that before long nothing that happens in your life will seem to be a big deal. But don’t expect immediate results. Be patient and just practise. You have so many habitual patterns, hang-ups and distractions to work through that it may take a little time before you notice an effect. And if it does, don’t complain! To complain would be like placing a raw egg on the table and then moaning when it fails to cook. An egg will only cook if you put it in a pan and heat it over a stove; if you fail even to put the egg in a pan, you have no grounds for complaint when it doesn’t cook, and if you do complain, it’s a sign that you have yet to understand cause, condition and effect. Most modern people fall into this trap. In spite of extraordinary achievements in scientific discovery, like sending men to the moon or discovering that the world is round (there are Tibetans today who still think it’s flat), they continue to lack this fundamental understanding.

What if it does takes ten years of practise before you notice any change? Compared to the billions and billions of lifetimes you have lived through without even taking an egg out of an egg box, ten years is nothing! At long last, you are making progress because you finally know that there is an egg and that the egg must be taken out of the box and put into the pan if it is ever to be cooked. That in itself is a vast improvement. You must have accumulated a great deal of merit just to know that much.

#### The Three Kinds of Poison Practitioners Must Extract

Practitioners will need to extract three different kinds of poison. The first is the poison of mistaken or “wrong view,” and to draw out this poison, you must apply yourself to hearing and contemplating the teachings. In the process, even though you may appear to have established the right view, you will inevitably be contaminated by various other poisons, concepts and doubts that will also need expelling. And to do so, the antidote of meditation is applied, for example shamatha practise, which is lethal to all kinds of “mental contamination” (*namtok* in Tibetan). The third poison to be removed is dualistic, subject-object “experience”—while you continue to have experiences, your path will always be poisoned—and to suck out this poison, you must apply yourself to the meditation of “insight wisdom” (vipashyana).

Each level of understanding that we struggle to achieve will eventually have to be discarded because, as the great masters of the past have told us many times, understanding is like a patch, sooner or later it will fall off; and experience is like a mist, sooner or later it will evaporate. But for those of us who have been brought up to value step-by-step progress, the idea that at some point on the spiritual path we must necessarily shed all our achievements is particularly difficult to grasp.

#### Three Nyams

Since guru yoga invokes such powerful blessings, you may find your life stirred up quite considerably by unexpected changes in your moods and mind, so be prepared. Most of all, guru yoga tends to invoke various experiences—although perhaps “experience” is not the best translation of the Tibetan word *nyam*.

In the context of guru yoga, there are three kinds of nyam. One is a feeling of such bliss that you are convinced you can deal with anything life throws at you. In the same way a cup and saucer fit together perfectly, everything in life dovetails so beautifully that you feel you could do anything, even balance an elephant on a thimble. Nothing is unacceptable or intolerable, and if you were told that Frankfurt had just turned upside down, you would accept it without hesitation. Not only are you able to believe the unbelievable, you also experience tremendous physical bliss. And although most of us never experience this particular nyam, rather pathetically, it’s the one we all long for.

Another kind of nyam is the experience of nonconceptualization. You have no thoughts, feel no aggression and no passion, make no judgments and no comparisons and feel no insecurity, yet everything you perceive is vivid and present. It can last for a few minutes, a few hours or even a few days. But let’s not go into this experience in too much detail, far better you discover it for yourself.

The third nyam is the experience of unusual clarity. Everything you perceive is so pristinely clear that, for example, you can see every leaf on every tree, and your intuition sharpens so much that it’s as if you can see into other people’s minds.

Eventually these three nyams will dissolve like mist and are therefore not your final destination. Neither are you likely to experience any one of them in the near future. Most of us have yet to experience our first good dream, so the prospect having to deal with nyams is still a very long way off.

#### When You Get Bored

No matter which practise we do, we all get bored. And these days, our boredom levels are higher than ever before. How many of us would be satisfied with just one TV channel? So when you find your mind wandering off in search of something new, just switch your focus.

Imagine, for example, that Guru Rinpoche is everywhere, filling every inch of space. As you chant the vajra guru mantra, six spheres of light instantly transform into six Guru Rinpoches. In the blink of an eye a blue banner appears, then another Guru Rinpoche, and suddenly every human being near you has transformed into a Guru Rinpoche, along with your thermos, pen, pencil, wristwatch, books, handkerchief, diapers and paper tissues. Outside, the trees, mountains, lakes, railroad tracks and cars all immediately take the form of Guru Rinpoche, and even the refreshing breeze becomes his manifestation.

Try imagining Guru Rinpoche above your head, or sitting in front of you, or in your heart. (As has already been mentioned, there is a visualisation for every situation.)

- As you chant the mantra, imagine an uninterrupted stream of nectar flows down from the guru and dissolves into you.
- At night visualise your guru sitting on a lotus in your heart.
- As you eat, imagine your guru in your throat.
- If you are facing an obstacle, perhaps a dispute in your family, or if you are depressed, visualise your guru on your shoulder, wrathfully baring his fangs.
- Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro practitioners can imagine sparks of fire and scorpions shooting out from Guru Rinpoche’s vajra, the scorpions snapping up each and every obstacle and swallowing them until they are all completely annihilated.
- As death approaches, visualise your guru in the form of red Amitabha and transfer yourself into Amitabha’s heart, again and again.

Imagine that everyone and everything you encounter throughout the day is none other than Guru Rinpoche.

This is quite a profound practise, and beginners may find it a little difficult at first, so start by doing it on an aspirational level. Basically everything—Buddha, dharma, sangha, deities, dakinis, dharmapalas—is a manifestation of your guru. So if, for example, you do a dharmapala practise, make the offerings and consider the protector to be a manifestation of Guru Rinpoche in the form of a dharmapala. By doing so, the offering you make to the dharmapala will also be an offering to your guru.

And there is no need to limit your offerings to that which is good and desirable. Offer everything, including sickness, bad news and loss, and remember that *everything* must be seen as a manifestation of your guru and his blessings.

#### Don’t Commit to Too Many Practises

As I mentioned earlier, in these degenerate times to hear the dharma is so rare and so precious that, of course, I will always encourage you to receive as many different teachings as you can. But if you are eager to receive initiations, I strongly recommend that before you do anything, first check the commitments you will be signing up for and ask yourself: do you really have the time to do pages and pages of sadhana practise every day? If your life is already very busy, think twice about stockpiling initiations; however short a practise may look, it all adds up. Of course, if you have time and receiving initiations and collecting commitments makes you feel good, then be my guest, especially if you don’t feel guilty when you are unable to keep your commitments properly.

At the outset of their spiritual path, practitioners are usually extremely enthusiastic and fall over themselves to practise absolutely everything they can lay their hands on. But, again, boredom is bound to creep in, often just as you approach the final stretch, and if you then switch to a different path, although the new practise may be inspiring for a few days or even weeks, in terms of spiritual progress you will have slipped right back to square one. Ironically, before long you will become just as bored with the new practise as you were with the old one.

Meet as many lamas as you can, hear as many dharma teachings as possible, particularly if you are eager to pursue the higher paths, and try to socialize with people from the same lineage you are practicing. But try to avoid being distracted by other practises that at first glance appear more attractive than those you are already engaged in.

### The Signs of Maturity in a Practitioner

Tsele Natsok Rangdröl said that as a practitioner’s understanding of the view matures, the fussier he will become about apparently trivial causes and conditions; as his experience and realisation increases, the less courageous he will be about exhibiting fake craziness; and the more he tames his own mind, the purer his perception of others will be. Not only his guru but everyone, even his vajra brothers and sisters will, from his perspective, be “good,” and this is a sign that his mind has been tamed.

You might imagine that the tamer your mind, the more likely you are to see the wildness in others, but quite the opposite happens. A mature practitioner will usually have a far purer perception of others than a beginner. The more enlightened qualities a practitioner acquires, the humbler he will become; the more time he spends with his guru, the greater his devotion; and the more he hears and contemplates the dharma, the quicker his pride and arrogance will diminish.

The supreme sign of a great practitioner is not that he sprouts a halo, has extraordinarily auspicious dreams, experiences bliss continuously, or can foresee our miserable futures. The supreme sign is that he no longer has any interest in material gain, fame, the respect of others, or being the centre of attention.

> Father Lama, embodiment of all the buddhas of the three times, hear me!

> Having experienced the ceaseless shifting of phenomena,

> I feel an urgency compelling me to practise—thank you, Lord Guru!

> And thank you for the blessing that makes me remember “impermanence.”

This is a very loose translation of one of many beautiful prayers by Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé.

We all need to pray for patience, but we should also pray for the dharma to manifest appropriately.

> Bless me, Lord Guru!

> May my mind enter into the dharma,

> And may the dharma enter into my mind.

> Bless me, Lord Guru!

> May dharma practise not be too rough on me.

> Bless me! May the defilements of the path be dispelled;

> Bless me! May all confusion and delusion arise as wisdom;

> Bless me! May all non-dharma thoughts cease this very instant!

> When strong habits impel me, or past karma matures

> And unworthy ambitions arise,

> Bless me, Lord Guru! May my actions

> Always fail to bear fruit.

In this last prayer, rather than asking for our worldly desires to be fulfilled, we wish for the opposite by asking the guru to make sure that if we were to pray for a worldly boon, our prayer would *not* be granted.

> Bless me! May love, compassion and bodhichitta flourish within me.

> Bless me! May relative and absolute bodhichitta arise in my mind.

> Bless me! May genuine devotion thrive in my heart.

> Bless me! May inspiration never ebb but always increase.

> Bless me! May my next digression not distract me.

> Bless me! May past karma and habit never overpower me.

> Bless me! May the appearance of good never deceive me.

Often situations arise that we feel sure must be a positive result of dharma practise. The trouble is we get carried away by them, which can turn what had seemed to be a positive result into a negative one. So we should pray that whatever happens, we will never be distracted.

> Bless me! May my health be good so I can help many beings.

> Bless me! May all that enters my mind lead me to the dharma.

This last prayer is a particularly beautiful one. Although our thoughts and actions appear to be mundane and worldly, we ask for blessings so that in the future the very same actions will somehow be of benefit to others. For example, a pint of Guinness flashes into your mind and you suddenly feel a strong desire for a drink. So you go to a pub, where, quite unexpectedly, you meet a stranger with whom you discuss dharma. The stranger is so interested in what you have to say that before the evening is over, he has taken down the address of a local dharma centre, and your very ordinary need for a drink has led this stranger to make a karmic link with the dharma.

> Bless me! May I be spared from poverty and always be prosperous.

> Bless me! May I live a long life that gives me the freedom to practise the dharma.

## 13. Abhisheka and the Four Empowerments in Guru Yoga

### Abhisheka: An Introduction to Buddhanature

Followers of the bodhisattvayana are usually introduced to buddhanature gradually through philosophical and contemplative analysis, but it is an approach that only provides a partial glimpse of buddhanature. To discover it fully, the dharma, and particularly the six paramitas or ten paramitas, must be contemplated and practised for three countless aeons. Students of the vajrayana, on the other hand, are introduced to buddhanature immediately and offered a path and method through which it is possible to discover buddhanature in one practise session. This method is sometimes known as the “first door to the tantric path,” but more commonly, “abhisheka.”

While the methods used by these two vehicles for introducing students to their buddhanature are different, it is a trap to assume either that one method is better than the other, or one is good and the other bad. The main difference between them is that while the bodhisattvayana teachings put the emphasis squarely on the mind, the vajrayana is also concerned with body and speech, which is why vajrayana practitioners invoke buddhanature through abhisheka. The manifestation of “body, speech and mind” used will have a name, perhaps Tara or Manjushri or Avalokiteshvara, so in a way, it’s as if the abhisheka itself is telling you that you have buddhanature.

Later, once your devotion has generated enough merit, your guru will introduce you to your buddhanature, but whether you recognize it or not will depend on the karmic connection between yourself as the receiver of the abhisheka and the guru who bestows it. So, ideally, those who receive abhisheka should feel steadfast trust in the person giving it, as well as the path itself—but this kind of devotion is not easy to generate.

#### What Does Abhisheka Mean?

Sanskrit is an extremely rich language. Each word is finely nuanced with many levels of meaning and therefore open to a variety of interpretations. *Abhisheka* is a Sanskrit term, and its two fundamental meanings have been translated into Tibetan as *torwa* and *lugpa*. *Torwa* is usually translated as “dismantling” and refers to the cocoon of ignorance in which we are wrapped and that needs to be dismantled; and *lugpa* is translated as “pouring”—as in “pouring blessings”—and more obliquely, as “discovering our buddhanature.” However, as we try to understand the full implications of this word, such translations can be misleading—particularly “pouring,” which is so closely connected with the idea of receiving an empowerment.

The most common description of abhisheka is that it is a transfer of power during a ceremony to give recipients the authorization to hear, study and practise the teachings of the vajrayana; we therefore “receive an empowerment.” But the problem is that receiving an empowerment suggests someone is giving us a power we previously lacked, in the same way the Queen Elizabeth invests a subject with the title “Knight of the Realm,” and is a long way away from the true spirit of tantric initiation.

During an initiation we are introduced to an aspect of ourselves that already exists within us but that we have yet to recognize, and it is the activation of this recognition that we call “empowerment” or “initiation.” This is the real meaning of abhisheka.

### Abhisheka: The Theory

According to the highest yoga tantra, there are four main kinds of abhisheka (plus a number of other kinds, each of which can be subdivided many times). Each abhisheka is tailored to dismantle one of the four kinds of ignorance or defilement:

- the defilement of the nadi, veins or channels;
- the defilement of prana, speech or wind-energy;
- the defilement of bindu, which is, more or less, a defilement of mind; and
- the defilement of the residue of the first three combined (something like “alaya”), sometimes described as the defilement of “the ground of everything.”

#### Ritual Implements

Abhisheka almost always involves the use of ritual objects, implements and substances to symbolize profound spiritual transformation. The master who bestows the empowerment, or “initiation,” first places a vase on the head of each initiate, then pours a drop of saffron water (the first initiation substance) into their hands. saying, “Drink this saffron water.” Next he gives them a spoonful of liquid from the kapala filled with nectar, or *amrita* in Sanskrit (the second initiation substance), which is considered in tantra to be a mixture of the essences of the father and mother consort. The ritual substance used in the third initiation is related to the consort, which these days is usually the picture of a dakini of some sort. And lastly, the substance used by Nyingmapas and Kagyupas in the fourth or “word” initiation (which is not always necessary) is crystal to symbolize the nature of mind.

Each section of the abhisheka can be performed very elaborately or very simply and all kinds of substances, mantras, mudras and samadhi can be employed.

#### Water as a Tantric Substance

The Buddhist teachings originated in India, where for millennia it has been customary to purify the body with water. It is a custom that can easily translate into today’s world where we, too, habitually associate washing in water with cleansing and purification. It is relatively easy, therefore, to appreciate that when we wash, we are, at the very least, not accumulating more dirt.

Ordinary, everyday water is commonly used as an exotic tantric substance, for many reasons. First and foremost, ordinary human habits are routinely incorporated into the vajrayana path. In fact, the entire vajrayana path is made up of skilful methods that reflect our habits and daily routines. For example, using water to wash a T-shirt is an extremely well-established habit, and no one would even consider using mud from the garden.

However, the vajrayana teachings also point out that what we call “water” manifests in all sorts of ways. Few of us would hesitate to wash with water from a tap or sink, but would shrink from washing in water drawn from the toilet. Of course in places like Singapore, techniques are being developed to recycle water used to flush toilets with all the other water used, and when we see it spurting from a showerhead, we forget its history completely, because now it is water for washing our bodies. Similarly, as soon as water is poured into an offering bowl, it immediately becomes offering water. So, while the generic term for all colourless liquid made up of one atom of oxygen and two of hydrogen is “water,” when associated with other objects and situations, our perception of it changes: water with bubbles is drinking water, water in a bath is person-washing water, water in an old iron tub in the garden is dog-washing water, the water we put into our car radiators becomes a cooling system and water in a pool is for swimming in. This can be taken to extremes, for example in the world of bottled water there is a brand from Japan that costs more than a hundred U.S. dollars for a 200ml bottle!

As we habitually transform water in all these ways, and the logic that insists this water is “pure” and that water “ordinary” is the same one we use to discern between drinking water and toilet-flushing water, why not used it as a tantric substance? This is why water is used as an offering substance during an abhisheka, and at times each and every drop is considered to be the mandala of the principal deity. Alcohol and torma are also used, and all these offerings are visualised as having different functions.

### The Practise: Self-Empowerment

Obviously, we don’t need to prepare all the ritual substances for our daily practise. In fact, to take self-initiation through meditation and visualisation practises is equally, if not more powerful, than during complicated rituals.

The four abhishekas are usually found at the end of the guru yoga practise, and the more often you receive them, the better. So you might consider repeating the abhisheka visualisations and recitations after each mala of mantras.

You might like to receive all four abhishekas one after another within the space of one practise session, or concentrate on each initiation for a set period of time (one month, or a year, for example) before moving on to the next. So, during the first year of guru yoga practise you could visualise the white light emanating from the guru’s forehead, and during the second, the red light emanating from his throat, and so on. If you decide to practise in this way, do not forget to complete the rest of the abhishekas, which you can abbreviate if you are pushed for time.

#### 1. The Vase Abhisheka

Visualise a white letter OM at the guru’s forehead. Don’t worry if you find it hard to see a clear image of this letter. Just imagine an intense and vivid, brilliant white light emanating from the guru’s forehead that then dissolves into yours—it’s similar to the visualisation in Vajrasattva practise. The white light cleanses your defilements, specifically those of the nadis (subtle channels) and five chakras (energy centres), as well as all the negative activities committed by your body. Then, consider the guru’s body and your body become inseparable, which is just another way of saying that your body becomes an “indestructible vajra body.”

You have now received the vase abhisheka, the door of developing meditation (utpattikrama) has been opened for you, and as the white light fills your body, the seed of the nirmanakaya has been planted in you.

#### 2. The Secret Abhisheka

Visualise a red letter AH at the guru’s throat from which brilliant rays of red light stream out and dissolve into your throat, cleansing the defiled activities of your speech and the obscurations to your prana (inner air). As the red light dissolves into you, consider your speech becomes inseparable from that of the guru (“vajra speech”).

You have now received the secret abhisheka, the door to completion meditation (sampannakrama) has been opened, and the seed of the sambhogakaya has been planted in you.

#### 3. The Wisdom Abhisheka

Visualise a blue letter HUM in the centre of the guru’s heart from which emanates dazzling blue light that dissolves into your heart centre, expelling all the defilements of mind and cleansing all obscurations of bindu (energy). Your mind is inseparable from the guru’s mind (“vajra mind”).

You have now received the wisdom abhisheka, the door to trekchö (the first of the two formless completion meditation practises) has been opened, and the seed of the dharmakaya has been planted in your mind.

#### 4. The Word Abhisheka

Visualise another blue letter HUM at the guru’s heart from which streams more blue light that dissolves into your forehead, throat, heart and entire body, dispelling all your defilements, the residue of your defilements and any remaining habitual patterns. You receive the blessings of the guru’s body, speech and mind, and your body, speech and mind are inseparable from the guru’s (“vajra wisdom”).

You have now received the word abhisheka, which has destroyed the alaya and with it all trace of “mind.” The door to self-arising wisdom has been opened and the seed of the svabhavikakaya has been planted in you.

Theoretically, in this final abhisheka we destroy the root of all defilements and receive the blessings of the guru’s body, speech and mind by visualizing more blue light radiating from the letter HUM at the guru’s heart centre.

If the ngöndro practise you follow contains slightly different details, don’t worry, just follow your text.

How can you tell whether or not you have received the abhisheka? We tend to imagine we have received the abhisheka once we have been through the ritual. To sip blessed water, and so on, may be enough from a symbolic point of view, but in reality there is much more to it. As Tsele Natsok Rangdröl said, if you experience phenomena (the outer container and the inner contents) as “ordinary,” you have not received the vase abhisheka; if you fail to experience all sound as mantra, you have not received the secret abhisheka; if you do not experience all mental factors and discursive thoughts as wisdom, you have not received the wisdom abhisheka; and if you have not understood everything as the dharmakaya, you have not received the word abhisheka.

### Recognizing the Nature of Mind

At the end of the fourth abhisheka, having united your mind with that of the guru, you have a great opportunity to practise the technique of uncovering the nature of your mind and stabilizing that recognition, which is the only way to attain enlightenment swiftly and, according to Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche, is the quickest method for recognizing the nature of mind. Gaze continuously at the union of clarity and emptiness as it always has been, without separating from yourself for a moment, and remain undistracted in that recognition. Not one sentient being, he said, has ever been separate from the nature of mind for the briefest instant, but as we are not aware of it, we chase after all manner of delusions. So, arouse compassion and aspire, “May all sentient beings understand the absolute truth that the ‘awareness-emptiness uncontrived mind’ *is* the absolute dharmakaya and is never, ever separate from any of us.” Pray to the nature of mind, rely on it exclusively and long to realise it.

Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche also said, if you pray from the bottom of your heart to the affectionate mother, Yeshe Tsogyal, there is no doubt that she will embrace you with her compassion and be inseparable from you in this life, the next life and as you pass through the bardos. So, when you receive teachings and abhishekas, consider you are receiving them from the mother, Yeshe Tsogyal.

### Utpattikrama and Sampannakrama

Although this text is mostly made up of suggestions about how to practise ngöndro, you might find a little information about utpattikrama and sampannakrama helpful. It may even act as an appetizer for those students who find themselves inspired by this kind of practise.

The vajrayana teaches two main kinds of meditation practise: creation, or development, meditation (utpattikrama); and completion meditation (sampannakrama). Both meditations reflect the fact that all phenomena have a beginning and an ending, an arising and a cessation, a birth and a death. To purify the beginning we apply utpattikrama, and to purify the end we apply sampannakrama. The purpose behind these two stages of meditation is immense and far-reaching. Although this may be rather a gross generalization, the purpose of creation meditation is to realise the essence of all appearance and existence, and the purpose of completion meditation is to realise the essence of emptiness, shunyata**.**

Ordinarily, the terms *appearance* and *emptiness* give the impression that there are two separate entities, but in vajrayana Buddhism they are not separate. The separation of “appearance” from “emptiness” constitutes “fundamental ignorance,” and so to unite these two, the vajrayana provides us with the techniques of creation and completion meditation. When we admire a rainbow, we are aware, to a certain extent, that even though it appears to be very beautiful, it is empty of any solid existence. On the other hand, when we look at our own flesh and bones, we lack the capacity to understand that just like the rainbow, what we see is simply the unity of appearance and emptiness.

Creation meditation involves various tantric methods, including the visualisation of oneself as a deity, a lotus, a sun and moon disc seat and the emanation and reconvergence of rays of light from the heart of the deity. The completion stage involves two kinds of meditation: the “completion meditation of form,” and “formless completion meditation.” The completion meditation of form includes many anuyoga practises, such as meditating on the chakras and channels, and controlling prana by consciously gathering it into the centre of the avadhuti (the central channel), as well as dream yoga, practises for the bardos and kundalini practises. These are all included in the completion meditation of form and are followed by formless completion meditation, which is the highest vipashyana practise.

Je Könchok Lhundrub said that by practicing creation meditation it is possible for practitioners to use all worldly objects as their path, and practicing completion meditation makes it possible to transform all emotions and use them as their path.

### Tsok Offering

These days, although tsok offering is not really part of ngöndro, tsok practise has been adopted by many vajrayana groups as a kind of monthly feast. It is, however, much more than a great excuse for a party.

To perform a “proper” tsok is quite difficult in a number of ways. Just gathering the appropriate assembly is hard enough, but when it comes to finding the right offering substances, things get really tough.

In India, the highest and most prestigious caste is that of the Brahmans. During the time of the Buddha, as Brahmanic sensitivities dominated Indian society, meat of any kind was considered to be the worst, dirtiest and least desirable of substances. Even today, a member of a strict Brahman family would not willingly invite meat-eating, leather-wearing Westerners to a meal at his home, as his family would consider people like you and me to be little better than animals. However, the methods offered in the Buddha’s highest teachings aim to counter the habitual taboos and prejudices of the Brahmans, and therefore all their taboo substances are now part of tsok feasts.

These days, bottles of champagne and the best steak are offered by many dharma students during tsoks, but making such offerings should be done with care. For those living outside a Brahmanic culture, meat and alcohol are quite normal additions to most meals, and not despised at all. So, the point of using substances that contemporary society considers unacceptable and undesirable is, therefore, entirely lost. Ideally, we should offer plates of shit, because bacteria and faeces are, to modern scientific minds, almost universally categorized as “dirty,” if not downright dangerous. And technically, the point of a tsok offering is that the practitioners should have no preference about what they eat; a plate of shit and a slice of delicious cheesecake should be anticipated with exactly the same relish.

Another problem we face as modern vajrayana practitioners is that often our tsoks become extremely rowdy, wild parties. The lack of discipline exhibited by both masters and students alike has not only tarnished the reputation of an already very suspicious-looking tradition, but violates most of the vajrayana samayas of tsok.

Many students are told to offer the very best and most expensive food and drink as a way of helping us stop grasping at money. And it works, but at the same time we must not ignore nonduality. As shit and steak are, for a vajrayana practitioner, essentially the same, a tsok offering should not necessarily include only the cleanest, gourmet foods. Having said that, though, I would never advise you to offer shit in a tsok because the revulsion most of us would feel when we smelled it could result in a great deal of samaya breakage.

The elaborate method of practicing tsok might be too overwhelming for those new to ngöndro practise, so for you there is a very simple method. Simply recite whichever of the many tsok prayers you like, for example *The Shower of Blessings* by Mipham Rinpoche, over your regular meal. Or just gather a few biscuits and some fruit for the offerings, and similarly, recite a tsok prayer.

## 14. Practise Advice

### Taking Emotion as the Path

In the *Vimalakirti Sutra,* Buddha points out that a lotus cannot grow in a dry environment and will only blossom when rooted in wet mud; likewise, mahaparinirvana can only grow within those whose egos and emotions are as vast as Mount Meru.

Emotions are made use of at every stage of the spiritual path. For example, if you tend to have “grand passions,” you might try following the path that deliberately avoids acting upon those passions. Alternatively you could try allowing your passions to drive your actions without becoming bound up by the obscurations that accompany them. As Buddha said, emotions are the race of the Tathagata.

Never feel discouraged or overwhelmed by the sheer scale of the emotions you experience, or dwell on how difficult, even impossible, it will be to wade through them all before eventually recognizing the nature of the mind. As Khenpo Ngaga said, all defilements are temporary. And he was right. Our defilements are temporary and therefore relatively easy to abandon and purify. Without practise, though, we do not notice them because they are so familiar to us, and this is precisely why they will not fall apart by themselves—which is a point worth remembering. However, as emotions are undoubtedly temporary, the application of even the smallest dose of meditation practise will cause them to fall apart quite easily.

Vajrayana students can be a bit chauvinistic. They repeat “take emotion as the path” parrot-fashion at every opportunity because they think it is an exclusively vajrayana method. But they are wrong. According to Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé, all three vehicles include teachings on how to take emotions as a path.

#### The Three Yanas: Abandoning, Transforming and Knowing

Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé taught the three yanas based on the three activities of “abandoning, transforming and knowing.” His teachings not only give practical advice about how to practise the dharma, but also clearly define the shravakayana, the bodhisattvayana and the vajrayana and offer the three principal ways for dealing with “passion.”

- The shravakayana method abandons passion altogether by suppressing, dismantling and discouraging it.
- The bodhisattvayana method transforms passion, so you do not necessarily have to abandon it, but it is not allowed to manifest indiscriminately in a wild, untamed and harmful way.
- The vajrayana method is to know the essence of all passion. This knowledge is perfected by doing nothing at all, in the sense of not complying with the emotion. At the same time, feel no animosity for passion, and certainly do not try to abandon or correct it by transforming it. Simply being aware of it will ensure that you are not caught up in its game.

The method of the secret mantrayana is not to fabricate and in the state of nonfabrication simply to recognize.

**The Shravakayana: Abandoning**

Whenever an emotion arises, try to discourage and abandon it. Remind yourself about the futility of samsaric life and analyze the pain and suffering that indulging in emotions brings. As you begin to realise that giving in to your emotions will not bring you any real pleasure, by encouraging yourself to feel revulsion for them you move a little closer to developing a sense of “renunciation mind.”

In practise, the moment passion arises it causes suffering, and although some aspects may seem to be quite pleasant, eventually *all emotions cause pain*. Desire may appear to bring bliss or at least satisfaction, but it also breeds hope for more similar experiences and fear that it may never happen again, making suffering inescapable.

Shravakayana practitioners try to abandon emotion by applying methods like meditation on ugliness, also known as “meditation on impermanence.” Imagine you are looking at someone intoxicatingly beautiful who arouses your desire. As your passions escalate, analyze the object of your desire and break down each and every part of his or her body. Peel back the skin to reveal the blood, pus and mucus it contains and cut open their intestines to examine their shit. You will quickly learn that no being can remain “beautiful” under this kind of scrutiny, and quite suddenly the object of your desire will no longer attract or fascinate you, as there is nothing in them that is inherently covetable or precious.

Buddhist meditations on impermanence and ugliness should never be mistaken for righteous disdain or contempt for the human body or for women. They are designed to quench our desire by breaking down a beautiful whole into its rather less beautiful and very specific component parts (blood, pus, etc.). They are risk-free practises simply because they require us to deal solely with the raw, unvarnished truth about the objects we desire—but we usually find this truth difficult to stomach.

It is often more appropriate for us to practise this shravakayana attitude than that of the secret vajrayana, which is overflowing with risks and pitfalls. The shravakayana teachings are not only simple, straightforward and honest, they also deal with unchangeable everyday truths, such as all phenomena are impermanent and every single emotion painful.

**The Bodhisattvayana: Transforming**

In addition to employing the shravakayana’s methods, the bodhisattvayana emphasizes ways of transforming emotions. How? By aspiring to absorb the jealousy of all sentient beings, for example, so that they may be completely free of it. Using this method, all our emotions can be transformed.

As a Buddhist, you have been taught that aggression is generally considered to be a negative emotion. Therefore your immediate reaction to any aggressive tendency or thought that arises in your mind will be to reject it. And you reject it because you are in love with your “self,” which makes you an egoist. As an egoist, you do not want aggressive feelings or jealousy or any of the other negative emotions to spoil your chances of becoming renowned as a “good” Buddhist.

Yet according to the greater view offered by the bodhisattvayana, to reject your aggression is a weakness. To reject the bad and only accept the good shows that you are still stained by the clinging to self that we call “ego.” Instead, when a bodhisattvayana practitioner notices her aggressive emotions, she should think, “Aggression is really bad! But I am not the only one who suffers from it—*all* sentient beings do! So, may I take on the aggression, jealousy and pride of all sentient beings.”

From a relative point of view, what happens when you take on the suffering of others in this way? Fundamentally, you go against the wishes of your ego. So, if your ego wants to be the holiest and most sublime of all beings so it can boast it has no desire or jealousy, this is exactly the practise you need to do to oppose and resist it. By continually applying it, the ego becomes smaller and smaller until finally it has nowhere to live. Once that happens, what will become of your emotions? By then, they will have about as much life in them as a scarecrow or a mirage. Can you imagine what it would be like not to have a “you” or an “I”? Where would all your aggression and passion be? And what would you do with it?

A bodhisattva also knows that emotions are compounded phenomena and will therefore eventually be exhausted. Ultimately, since an emotion is not a truly or permanently existing entity, it is also empty of characteristics. If emotions were not inherently empty, transformation would not be possible because they would be “real.” Armed with this knowledge, bodhisattvas have the ability to transform their emotions.

**The Vajrayana: Knowing**

The vajrayana tells us that whenever an emotion like desire arises, just watch it and do nothing—“don’t fabricate.” But it’s an instruction that is quite easy to misunderstand. When emotion arises, “don’t fabricate” simply means stop doing anything at all. What it does not mean is that if you are walking down the street, you should stop, find a bench, sit crossed-legged and try to “watch” the emotion. The point here is that having noticed an emotion, most of us tend not to “watch” it but to follow it. We feel desire, so we follow our desires; we feel anger, so we follow our anger, or at best merely suppress it.

So, how should we deal with emotions? By not fabricating at all, just watch. And the moment you look at the emotion, it will disappear. Beginners will find the emotion reappears quite quickly, but that doesn’t matter. The important thing here is that the moment you start watching your emotions, they immediately disappear. And even if they only disappear for a split second, the fact an emotion has disappeared also means that wisdom has, momentarily, dawned. The recognition of this naked awareness is what “knowing” refers to.

To “know” the emotion is to understand that, as it has no root, there is and never has been an emotion. Some people talk about emotion, particularly the negative emotions, as if they were some kind of hideous, demonic force that willfully invades your being, but they are not like that at all.

When you feel angry, just watch your anger. Not the cause of the anger or its result, just the emotion of anger. As you stare at your anger, you will discover there is nothing you can point to and say, “That is my anger.” And the understanding that there is absolutely nothing there is what is called the “dawn of wisdom.”

**Apply Abandoning, Transforming and Knowing Simultaneously**

You can practise all three methods together. For example, if you suddenly feel very aggressive as you walk down the street, apply abandoning, transforming and knowing simultaneously.

- Remind yourself that aggression will only lead to more suffering, so what is the point of getting angry? Whatever triggered your anger is the exclusive product of your own perception, and to realise, in an instant, the futility of the emotions you feel is a sign that you are practicing the *shravakayana*.
- Aspire to take on the anger of all sentient beings so that they may be free of it completely, and by so doing you practise the *bodhisattvayana*.
- Just watch your emotion without any fabrication or judgment. This practise is associated with the *vajrayana*.

### Final Advice

#### Alternative Ways to Practise

If ngöndro is your daily practise and you are trying to accumulate the traditional number of repetitions for each element of the practise, follow the order they appear in your text, but spend the majority of your time on the specific practise you wish to accumulate. If you are accumulating prostrations, spend most of your time on taking refuge, then recite the rest of the practises, arousing bodhichitta, purification through Vajrasattva, mandala offering and guru yoga. Once you have completed one hundred thousand prostrations, continue to start your daily practise by taking refuge, but devote most of your session to arousing bodhichitta, and complete your practise by reciting the purification through Vajrasattva, mandala offering and guru yoga. Continue working through each element of ngöndro in this way until you have completed your accumulations.

It is usually assumed that practitioners will accumulate at least one hundred thousand of each practise, and often more than a million guru yoga mantras, and that they will follow the order the practises appear in their ngöndro text. But it is possible that your guru will instruct you to do each practise for a certain period of time instead, for example, four months, or one hundred hours.

If you really want to, you can also choose to follow a different order. For example, if you have time on your hands and a lot of energy, you might choose to accumulate prostrations in the mornings and offer mandalas in the evenings. Then later, when you feel like being less physically active, you might prefer to sit still and accumulate Vajrasattva’s hundred-syllable mantra. Whatever you do, your aim should always be to accumulate at least one hundred thousand of each practise. This is known as “finishing the ngöndro.”

Always make sure you begin by taking refuge and arousing bodhichitta, and end by dedicating your practise to the ultimate happiness and enlightenment of all sentient beings.

If you do a ngöndro retreat, practise in the same way, but divide your day into three or four sessions and complete the whole ngöndro in each session.

#### Retreat

A retreat can be any length, from a weekend to an entire lifetime, and how strictly you schedule it will depend entirely on your circumstances.

For a ngöndro retreat, it is not necessary to make elaborate mandalas, and the only ritual objects and substances you will need have already been mentioned.

If you do a long retreat (one to three years), it is a good idea to set yourself a more relaxed schedule. If your retreat is quite short (a couple of days or a week), make your schedule more intense, because when you only have a little time, it is best to make the most of every moment.

How tightly you set your boundary depends entirely on your personal needs and circumstances. If your retreat is for more than a month, always include in your boundary a doctor, electrician, plumber, dentist and anyone you might need to call on in an emergency.

Neither closing nor opening a retreat boundary requires much ceremony. I have noticed that those who make a big hoo-ha about this particular ritual rarely manage to keep to their boundary. A boundary enclosing your practise space is set to remind you that the point of retreat is to withdraw from distraction and only you have the power to make that kind of withdrawal; no one else can or should impose it on you.

A very strict retreat should probably involve very long sessions, very short breaks and absolutely no communication at all, either personally or in cyberspace through e-mail or sending text messages or whatever. And you should definitely not actively seek out news from the television or radio.

If you decide to do a short and strict retreat, your first session can begin at three A.M. Have breakfast at around seven A.M., followed by your second session. Lunch could be at around noon, after which you do your third session, and your fourth session will happen after dinner. When precisely you eat dinner is up to you. The length of each break is also up to you and will depend on your own willpower and diligence.

During a longer retreat it’s a good idea to enlist help from someone who is not on retreat—for example, to bring you food, buy medicine, do unavoidable shopping, and so on.

If you want to be fussy and include more of the paraphernalia of retreat, please refer your questions to a retreat master, someone who has already done a retreat or a retreat manual.

The important thing here is to keep all the pledges you make on the first day throughout the retreat. One very good piece of advice is never to pledge too much at the beginning, then try to bend the rules as your retreat progresses. Far better to make more flexible promises that you then keep and to discipline yourself day by day. By conducting your retreat in this way you will not be stained by the karma of having broken your vows, and you will accumulate the merit of doing even more than you originally promised.

#### Sleep Yoga and Waking-up Yoga

Those of you who wish to transform the mundane activity of sleeping into something worthwhile could try practicing sleep yoga. As you lie down in bed, visualise and concentrate your mind on Guru Rinpoche in the centre of your heart. That is all that is required at this point.

In the morning, Guru Rinpoche rises up through your central channel to sit on the crown of your head. You can chant the following prayer for invoking the guru from the Longchen Nyingtik Ngöndro if you like, or if you prefer, feel free to choose one from a different source.

> From the blossoming lotus of devotion at the centre of my heart

> Rise up, O compassionate lama, my only refuge!

> I am plagued by past actions and turbulent emotions:

> To protect me in my misfortune

> Remain as the jewel-ornament on the crown of my head, the mandala of great bliss

> Arousing all my mindfulness and awareness, I pray!1

The idea that the guru dwells in your heart as you sleep is a symbolic teaching to show you that the outer guru is actually your buddhanature. Of course, when you wake up next morning, being a dualistic being, you will still think of the guru as being outside of yourself, and the vajrayana allows you to do so by suggesting you move the guru from your heart to the crown of your head.

Many people find it easier to practise when they follow a preset practise schedule. They also like to be summoned to practise by a bell. For some, having a schedule works well, and while group practise is important, do remember that its purpose is to inspire individual practise.

Individual practise is vital, and if you can find the time, you should try to do a formal ngöndro retreat and practise in three or four sessions a day. Accumulating ngöndro can also be your daily practise and if you do not want to do all one hundred thousand prostrations in one go before you start to accumulate Vajrasattva practise, and so on, start by doing twenty thousand prostrations and then twenty thousand Vajrasattva mantras before continuing your prostrations. Alternatively, you could accumulate all four foundation practises at the same time. Whatever you decide to do, I suggest you focus most intently on the dissolution of each practise, and after mixing your mind with that of the guru, remain in the state of indivisibility for as long as possible.

#### Signs of Progress

What are the signs of progress in our practise? What can we expect? Should we wait for a signal from the guru—or an award? According to Karma Chagme Rinpoche, we will have no experiences, no special dreams, no pure visions. The “king of all signs,” also known as the “sign of no-sign,” which was highly prized by the Kagyupa masters of the past, is when renunciation mind, sadness and devotion blaze in your mind. The signs to be cherished most include an escalating appetite for dharma practise; noticing the futility of everything you do; ever-increasing conflicts as a result of old habits; and while you may still have the urge to party with your friends, to be plagued by the unwelcome sense that the whole thing is a useless waste of time.

Therefore do not constantly aim to finish the practise. Instead, try to accept that your spiritual journey will never end. Your journey began with the wish that you, personally, bring all sentient beings to enlightenment, so until that wish is fulfilled, your activities as a bodhisattva will never cease.
